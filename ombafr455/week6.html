Week 6 · 10–16 August · Chapter 6

Owning the problem, or selling it to someone else

Last week you lent money and got a contract: a use-of-proceeds promise or a coupon step-up, written down and enforceable. This week you buy the company — and every one of those levers disappears. An equity holder cannot ring-fence anything. What you get instead is a vote and a phone call. Léman is being asked to build a CHF 400m sustainable equity sleeve, and the first question the investment committee will ask is the one this whole field argues about: do you sell the worst emitter in the portfolio, or keep it and push? One of those makes your carbon report look dramatically better. The other is the only one that can change anything. They are not the same choice. Plan 4–5 hours: ~two for this lesson, its videos and labs, then 2–3 for the homework.

By the end of Week 6 you will be able to

  • Tell the five sustainable equity strategies apart from a factsheet — and say which ones change anything in the real world.
  • Read what SFDR Article 8 and Article 9 actually commit a fund to, and why so many Article 9 funds were downgraded.
  • Compute a portfolio's weighted-average carbon intensity, and explain exactly what it does and does not measure.
  • Quantify the exclusion trade-off: how much carbon a screen removes, and how much tracking error it buys.
  • Distinguish voting from engagement, and place a real case on the escalation ladder.
  • Argue the divest-or-engage decision on Rhône Cement with numbers on both sides — and know which number is the honest one.

Section 6.1 · The Hook

Sell it, or keep it and shout

Your decision

Léman holds CHF 16m of Rhône Cement. What do you do with it?

You know this company. In Week 4 it was the €60m holding responsible for 78% of the sleeve's financed emissions. In Week 5 it came back as the sustainability-linked bond issuer — the one with no green projects to ring-fence, so it had to put a target in the contract instead.

Now the equity desk has it too: CHF 16m, 4% of the new CHF 400m sustainable equity sleeve, in a company whose carbon intensity is 780 tCO₂e per CHF million of revenue — roughly seven times the sleeve average.

The investment committee wants it gone. The stewardship team wants to keep it and push for a decarbonisation plan. Both sides have a case, and both will bring a number.

Place your bet — then lock it

Léman sells its entire Rhône Cement position tomorrow morning. What happens to real-world CO₂ emissions?

Pick your answer.
Locked. On the day: nothing changes. A secondary-market sale transfers existing shares to another owner. No cash reaches Rhône Cement, no kiln is switched off, and the company's cost of capital moves — at most — by an amount too small to measure from one CHF 16m ticket. What does change, instantly and dramatically, is Léman's own carbon report: you will see in §6.5 that this single sale cuts the sleeve's reported carbon intensity by 24.6%. The uncomfortable version of the second answer is the sophisticated one, and it is the subject of a genuine academic argument — divestment works only at scale, slowly, through the price of new capital. Hold that thought until §6.5, where you will price both options.
The shape of the week

What you can actually do as an owner.

Debt gave you contractual promises. Equity gives you two things instead — a screen and a voice — and the week is about using both honestly. Four moves:

  • Choose a strategy — the five ways to build a sustainable equity portfolio, and what SFDR makes you promise (§6.2).
  • Measure and pay for it — carbon intensity, and the tracking error a screen costs you (§6.3).
  • Use the vote — proxy voting, resolutions, say-on-climate and the escalation ladder (§6.4).
  • Decide Rhône Cement — divest or engage, with the numbers on both sides (§6.5).

The action that most improves your carbon report is the one that changes nothing in the atmosphere. Every honest sustainable equity strategy starts by admitting that.

Section 6.2 · Strategies

Five ways to call a fund sustainable

The taxonomy of practice

They are not variations on one idea — they do different jobs.

"Sustainable equity fund" is not a category; it is at least five, and they disagree about what the point is. Learn to spot which one a factsheet is describing, because the label rarely says.

StrategyWhat it doesWhat it is good atIts weakness
Negative / exclusionaryRemoves sectors or activities — tobacco, controversial weapons, thermal coal above a revenue threshold.Values alignment. Simple, auditable, cheap.Says nothing about the companies you keep. Changes no behaviour.
Norms-basedRemoves companies violating international norms — UN Global Compact, ILO conventions, OECD Guidelines.A floor everyone can agree on.Depends entirely on the data vendor's violation flags.
Best-in-class / positiveKeeps every sector but holds only the top ESG performers within each.Keeps sector exposure, so low tracking error.You still own the best oil major. Which is still an oil major.
ESG integrationFeeds ESG data into ordinary financial analysis as a source of risk and return.Honest about being a financial technique.Promises nothing. Nearly unfalsifiable from outside — the most over-claimed of the five.
Thematic & impactBuys exposure to a sustainability outcome — clean energy, water, health — and, for impact, targets measurable additional effect.The only two that start from a real-world outcome.Concentrated, expensive, and in listed markets additionality is very hard to claim.
The additionality problem, in one line. When you buy a listed share on the secondary market, your money goes to the seller, not the company. That is why "impact" is a strong claim in private markets and a weak one in listed equity — and why the honest listed-equity impact managers talk about investor impact (stewardship, signalling) rather than company impact.

The regulation that bites

SFDR Article 8 and Article 9

In the EU, the marketing claim is now a legal category. Two of them matter for equity funds:

Article 6No sustainability claim. Must still disclose how sustainability risks are integrated.
›
Article 8"Light green" — promotes environmental or social characteristics. The catch-all, and enormous.
›
Article 9"Dark green" — has sustainable investment as its objective. Nearly everything in it must qualify.

The gap between Article 8 and Article 9 is where most of the argument lives. Article 8 says you promote characteristics — a single exclusion can satisfy it, which is why the category ballooned to cover a large share of EU fund assets. Article 9 says sustainability is the objective, and when the regulator clarified what that implied, managers downgraded hundreds of billions of euros of funds from Article 9 to Article 8 rather than defend the claim.

Read that downgrade wave correctly. It is not evidence that the funds got worse. It is evidence that the original classifications were made against an unclear standard, and that when the standard sharpened, the claims did not survive. That is precisely what disclosure regulation is supposed to do — and it is the single best example in this course of a rule changing behaviour without banning anything.
Section 6.3 · The Trade-off

Every exclusion has a price

The sleeve

Léman's CHF 400m equity sleeve, holding by holding

Ten positions, tracking a broad Swiss-and-global benchmark. The right-hand column is carbon intensity — tonnes of CO₂e per CHF million of revenue — which is the number every client questionnaire now asks for.

WACI  =  Σ ( weighti × intensityi )  =  112.81 tCO₂e / CHF m revenue

That is the weighted-average carbon intensity: what a franc of your portfolio's revenue emits on average. Note what it is not — it is not your share of the companies' absolute emissions (that is financed emissions, Week 4), and it does not change when the portfolio grows or shrinks. It is an intensity, so it is comparable across funds of different sizes. That is why it became the standard disclosure, and also why it is so easy to game.

Tracking error is the standard deviation of your return minus the benchmark's, in basis points a year. The figures here are illustrative but the shape is real: shallow screens are nearly free, and the cost accelerates once you start removing whole sectors, because you lose diversification and take on unintended factor bets. A client who wants a deep screen and benchmark-hugging performance is asking for two things that fight each other, and it is your job to say so before the mandate is signed.

The number that flatters you

Four ways a carbon-intensity figure lies

  1. It rewards selling, not fixing. Divesting your worst holding improves it instantly. Persuading that holding to halve its emissions improves it far less. §6.5 puts numbers on exactly that.
  2. It is revenue-based. A company that raises prices without changing a single process lowers its intensity. So does inflation.
  3. Scope 3 is usually missing. For a bank, an oil trader or a retailer, almost everything that matters sits in Scope 3 — and it is the least reliable, least comparable data in the set.
  4. Sector mix does most of the work. A fund heavy in software will beat a diversified fund on WACI while doing nothing at all. Compare like with like, or compare against the benchmark, never in the absolute.

A falling portfolio carbon number is evidence about your portfolio. It is not, on its own, evidence about the world.

Section 6.4 · Stewardship

The vote and the phone call

Two different tools

Voting is public and annual. Engagement is private and continuous.

"Stewardship" bundles two activities that behave completely differently, and confusing them is the commonest error in a client meeting.

Proxy voting
Once a year

Public, binary, on the record. You vote on directors, pay, auditors, and any resolution that reaches the ballot. Cheap, scalable across thousands of holdings, and fully disclosed — which is why it is the part that gets scored by NGOs.

Engagement
All year

Private, gradual, unverifiable from outside. Meetings with management, letters, investor coalitions. Where the actual change happens when it happens — and, precisely because it is private, where the most unfalsifiable claims are made.

The test to apply to any manager's stewardship report: not "how many engagements did you have" — that is a diary, not an outcome — but "name a company that changed something, say what changed, and show how your vote is recorded." Voting records are public. Engagement counts are marketing until they are tied to a named outcome.

Engagement is not one action; it is a sequence with rising cost and rising publicity. Léman's stewardship policy commits to climbing it in order, and to saying so. Click a rung to see what it commits you to.

Click a rung.
Why the order matters. Skipping to a public resolution before you have asked privately gets you a defensive board and no change. But an engagement that never escalates is indistinguishable from doing nothing — which is the honest criticism of a great deal of what is reported as engagement. A credible policy states the ladder, states the time limit at each rung, and states what happens at the top.
Section 6.5 · The Decision

Divest or engage: the arithmetic

Back to §6.1

Both options, priced.

Here are the two paths for Léman's CHF 16m in Rhône Cement, with the number each one produces. Read them side by side before you decide which you would defend to the investment committee — and then which you would defend to a client.

Divest today
−24.6%

Sleeve WACI 112.81 → 85.01. A quarter of your reported carbon intensity, gone in one trade, bookable this quarter. Real-world emissions change: zero. The kilns run; someone else owns them. You also lose the vote, the meetings and any future ability to influence the company at all.

Engage, and succeed
−9.9%

Sleeve WACI 112.81 → 101.61, if Rhône Cement cuts intensity from 780 to 500 by 2030. Less than half the reported improvement — and it takes years, might fail, and costs stewardship resource. Real-world reduction: 280 tCO₂e per CHF m of revenue, permanently, at the kiln.

The option that improves your report by 24.6% changes nothing.
The option that improves it by 9.9% is the only one that changes anything.

What the lab cannot tell you is the probability. Nobody knows it, and a manager who quotes one precisely is guessing. What you can defend is the structure: state the target, state the deadline, state the escalation, and state in advance the point at which failure means you sell. That last commitment — engage with a deadline, divest if it is missed — is what most credible policies actually say, and it is the answer the investment committee is really looking for.

You will be asked to defend this field to sceptics — clients, colleagues, the press. The strongest position is not to dismiss the critique but to concede the parts that are true (the ratings disagree, the labels were over-claimed, secondary-market purchases are not additional) and be precise about what is left standing: disclosure that changed behaviour, capital that got cheaper for genuinely green projects, and a vote that occasionally removes a board.

The cost-of-capital channel

When does divestment actually work?

The serious argument for divestment is not about the shares you sell. It is about the next financing.

If enough investors refuse a sector, the pool of buyers for its new equity and debt shrinks, its cost of capital rises, and marginal projects stop clearing their hurdle rate. That is a real mechanism, it has been measured, and it is slow. It requires scale and coordination — one CHF 16m ticket does nothing; a large fraction of the market moving together does something.

Which gives you the honest summary to take into any meeting: divestment is a market-level instrument that individual investors cannot operate alone; engagement is an investor-level instrument that works only where you have leverage. Most portfolios need both, applied where each actually bites.

Take it outside Léman Sustainable Capital

One fund, opened up.

Same as previous weeks: one small thing on material that is actually yours, not graded, nobody sees it. This week's is unusually easy to do, because the documents are all public and most people have never opened them.

Pick whichever you can get the documents for

  1. Your own pension or savings fund. Find its factsheet and its SFDR classification. Which of the five strategies is it actually running — and does the marketing language match? Look specifically for whether any exclusion has a revenue threshold attached; "we exclude coal" means very little without one.
  2. A manager's stewardship report. Take any large asset manager's annual stewardship or voting report. Count the engagements claimed, then try to find one named company with a stated outcome. The ratio between those two numbers tells you more than the whole document.
  3. A voting record. Voting records are public. Pick a manager and a climate resolution at a company you know, and look up how they voted. Then compare that with what their marketing says about climate. This exercise has ended a few mandates.

Then the three questions that matter more than the documents: would this fund's carbon number improve if it simply sold its worst holding — and would anything real have happened? · what is the escalation ladder, and has anyone ever reached the top of it? · if you were the client, which single disclosure would you ask for that you have not been given?

Bring what you find to the discussion forum — the voting-record exercise in particular tends to produce the best posts of the term.
