Week 7 · 17–23 August · Chapter 7

Exposure without ownership

Week 5 lent money and got a contract. Week 6 bought the company and got a vote. This week Léman's overlay desk does something that has neither: it takes the return of a company it will never own. Derivatives are the largest market in finance and the one sustainable finance has thought about least — and they quietly dismantle both of the levers you have spent two weeks learning. Then, in the second half, the word this course has circled since Week 1 finally has to be faced. Additionality. Impact investing is the only branch of the field that puts it in the definition, and it is the reason a fund can be entirely honest, entirely well-run, and still change nothing. Plan 4–5 hours: ~two for this lesson, its videos and labs, then 2–3 for the homework.

By the end of Week 7 you will be able to

  • Explain why a synthetic position carries the same economics as a share and none of the stewardship — and what that does to a carbon report.
  • Tell the five families of sustainable derivative apart and say what each one is genuinely for.
  • Price a sustainability-linked derivative's KPI adjustment against the capex it is meant to incentivise, and judge whether it has teeth.
  • Say why a virtual PPA can be additional when a share purchase cannot.
  • Apply the three-part test — intention, measurement, contribution — to any impact claim.
  • Read an impact value chain, tell an output from an outcome, and compute what a fund may honestly claim.

Section 7.1 · The Hook

The position you never owned

Your decision

Same exposure. Same P&L. No shares.

Last week the investment committee could not agree about Rhône Cement, so it did what committees do: it asked for another option. The derivatives desk has one.

Instead of holding CHF 16m of shares, Léman enters a total return swap with a bank. Léman receives the total return of Rhône Cement stock — price moves and dividends — and pays a financing rate. The economics are identical to owning the shares — synthetic exposure. The bank, to hedge itself, goes out and buys them.

Léman now has the exposure. The bank has the shares — and therefore the vote.

Place your bet — then lock it

Léman's sustainability report has a line for financed emissions. What should the swap contribute to it?

Pick your answer.
Locked. Economically the answer is obvious — the same as holding the shares. Léman carries every franc of the exposure. But carbon accounting is not economics. Attribution methodologies were built around ownership: your share of a company's emissions is your equity or debt divided by its enterprise value. A swap gives you no equity, so for years the standard simply had nothing to say about it, and in practice many reporters put zero in the box. That is the trap this week opens with. You can hold the identical economic position and report a fraction of the carbon — and you have handed the vote to a bank that will never use it. Week 6 ended by noting that the action which most improves your carbon report changes nothing in the atmosphere. Here is a stronger version: an action that changes nothing whatsoever, not even the ownership, can still improve the report. Hold your answer; §7.5 comes back to it.
The shape of the week

Two halves, one question.

This week is deliberately in two parts, and they meet at the same place.

§7.2 – §7.3 · Derivatives
Exposure

Instruments that give you the economics of a thing without the thing. Five families, what each is for, and the arithmetic of the one that claims to change behaviour.

§7.4 – §7.5 · Impact
Contribution

The only branch of sustainable finance that puts additionality in its own definition — and the machinery it uses to prove, or fail to prove, that money changed something.

Derivatives ask: if you never owned it, what are you responsible for? Impact investing asks: if you did own it, what did you actually change? Both questions have the same answer at the bottom, and it is not a comfortable one.

Every instrument in the first half of this week gives you exposure without contribution. The second half explains why that sentence is the sharpest test in sustainable finance.

Section 7.2 · The Instruments

Five families of sustainable derivative

The map

They have almost nothing in common except the word.

"Sustainable derivatives" is not one product. It is five unrelated things that got filed under one heading, and they differ in the only way that matters — whether anything in the world is different because the trade happened.

Family 1 · by far the largest

Carbon derivatives — futures and options on emission allowances

Futures on EU ETS allowances (EUAs) are, by volume, the biggest "sustainable" derivative in existence — most of the carbon market's trading is in derivatives rather than the allowances themselves. A utility buys December futures to lock in the cost of its compliance obligation.

They do not create abatement; the cap does that. What they create is a forward price, which is what lets a company decide today whether abating is cheaper than buying.

Price discovery, not abatement — the cap does the work
Family 2

ESG index derivatives — futures and options on screened benchmarks

A listed future on a low-carbon or ESG-screened version of a major index. A pension fund can move CHF 2bn of exposure in an afternoon instead of trading fifteen hundred lines, or hedge a mandate it cannot yet transition physically.

Pure exposure management. Nobody's cost of capital changes because a future traded — the contract is between two investors and never reaches a company.

Operational convenience · no contribution
Family 3 · the one with a behavioural claim

Sustainability-linked derivatives (SLDs)

An ordinary swap — interest-rate, cross-currency, FX — with a KPI adjustment bolted on. Hit the target and the rate you pay improves by a few basis points; miss it and it worsens. The mechanic is borrowed wholesale from the sustainability-linked loan and the SLB you priced in Week 5.

This is the only family that claims to change the counterparty's behaviour. §7.3 tests that claim with arithmetic.

Claims to incentivise — check the size of the incentive
Family 4

Catastrophe bonds and weather derivatives

Risk transfer for physical climate risk. A government or insurer pays a premium; if a defined event occurs — a hurricane of stated intensity making landfall in a stated box — the principal is forgiven and the cash is released. Payment is usually on a parametric trigger: an objective measurement, not an assessed loss.

The trade-off is basis risk. Parametric pays in days rather than years, but it can pay when you were not badly hurt, and fail to pay when you were.

Real function: it moves capital to where the physical risk lands
Family 5 · the one that can be genuinely additional

Virtual PPAs — a contract for difference on power

A corporate agrees a fixed strike price for the output of a specific new wind or solar farm and settles the difference against the market price in cash. No electron ever reaches the buyer. It is a financial contract — a derivative.

And yet it is the one instrument on this page that routinely causes a new asset to exist: the contracted revenue is what makes the project bankable. §7.3 returns to it.

Can be additional — the contract finances the build
Why the order matters. Reading down this list, the instruments get smaller and more contested — and more consequential. The largest market changes nothing on its own; the one most likely to change something is a niche corporate contract that most sustainable finance courses never mention.

The mechanic

An SLD is a normal swap with a small conditional adjustment.

The exception

The virtual PPA, and why it is different from everything else this week

A wind developer has a site, a permit and a turbine order. What it does not have is twenty years of predictable revenue, and without that no bank will lend it the construction cost.

A corporate signs a virtual PPA: for fifteen years it will pay a fixed strike price for the farm's output and receive the market price, settled in cash. No electricity changes hands. It is a contract for difference — a derivative in every technical sense.

And it is the thing that makes the project bankable. The lender is not lending against the wind; it is lending against the corporate's credit. The farm gets built because the derivative exists.

Developersite, permit, no revenue certainty
→
Virtual PPA15-year fixed strike, cash-settled
→
Lendernow underwrites the debt
→
New capacitythat would not otherwise exist

Hold that against §7.2's other four families. A future on a low-carbon index is a bet between two investors. A virtual PPA is a contract that causes a wind farm. Both are derivatives. The instrument type tells you nothing; the question is always whether the money changed a decision.

Virtual Power Purchase Agreement (VPPA), explained · 6 min
Watch on YouTube ↗
The honest caveat. A VPPA on a farm that was already fully financed and under construction is not additional — it is a hedge dressed as climate action. The test is whether the contract was signed before financial close, and whether the developer says on the record that it was needed. That is exactly the question the Apple assignment asks about a bond.

Additionality is not a property of instruments. It is a property of decisions — and it only ever exists at the moment capital is committed.

Section 7.4 · Impact Investing

Intention, measurement, contribution

The definition, and why it is unusually strict

Impact investing is the only strategy that defines itself by what it changes.

Week 1 put the five strategies on a spectrum. Four of them describe how you choose holdings. Impact investing is the only one that makes a claim about the world — and therefore the only one that can be wrong about it.

The GIIN's definition names four core characteristics: intentionality, use of evidence and impact data in the investment design, management of impact performance, and contributing to the growth of the industry. In practice, examiners and investment committees compress this into three tests — and a claim has to pass all three.

1 · Intention

The impact was an objective set in advance, not a happy fact discovered afterwards. Written into the mandate, the investment case and the incentives — before the money moved.

2 · Measurement

The outcome is defined, baselined and tracked. Not activity, not output — the change in the condition you said you would change, measured against what it was before.

3 · Contribution

Your capital made a difference to what happened. The counterfactual is stated and defensible. This is additionality, and it is where nearly every claim fails.

Test 1 is cheap — anyone can write an intention. Test 2 is expensive but tractable. Test 3 is the one that separates impact investing from marketing, and it is brutal: on a public secondary market, where you buy an existing share from another investor at a market price, your contribution is close to zero by construction. No new capital reaches the company. This is the same fact that made §7.1 uncomfortable and that the Apple assignment turns on.

Where contribution is plausible. Primary issuance · private markets and venture · first-loss or concessionary capital · underserved markets where the buyer pool is thin · anchoring a deal that would not otherwise have cleared · and non-financial contribution, meaning the governance, expertise and stewardship you bring. Notice that only the last of these is available to a listed-equity fund — which is why Week 6's stewardship material is the honest impact claim for public markets.

Section 7.5 · Measurement

From activity to the number you may print

The value chain

Five words, and most reports stop at the third.

Impact measurement has one organising idea, and it is a chain. Every dispute about an impact report is a dispute about how far along it the author was entitled to walk.

InputsCHF 30m committed
→
Activitieslending to solar distributors
→
Outputs41,000 systems sold
→
Outcomeshouseholds off kerosene
→
Impactthe part you caused

Outputs are countable and cheap. Systems sold, loans disbursed, megawatts installed, meals served. Almost every impact report you will read is an output report, because outputs are collected by the business anyway.

Outcomes require a baseline. Did the household actually stop buying kerosene, or add the solar lamp alongside it? Answering that means measuring the same households before and after, which costs money and sometimes gives an unwelcome answer.

Impact requires a counterfactual — the outcome minus what would have happened anyway. And "anyway" cannot be measured, only argued. Hence the last step is always a claim, never a count.

impact = outcome − what would have happened anyway
The theory of change is how you make that argument in advance. Written before the investment, it states each link in the chain and — crucially — what evidence would show the link is broken. A theory of change written after the fact is an explanation, not a hypothesis, and it cannot be wrong.

The standard vocabulary

Five dimensions, and a metric library

Two pieces of shared infrastructure do most of the work in practice, and both are worth knowing by name.

DimensionThe question it forces
WhatWhat outcome is occurring, and how important is it to the people experiencing it?
WhoWho experiences it, and how underserved were they beforehand?
How muchHow many, how much change each, and for how long?
ContributionWould this have happened anyway, without the enterprise and without you?
RiskWhat is the chance the impact is different from what you expect — and who bears that?

IRIS+, maintained by the GIIN, supplies the metric definitions underneath: standard, comparable indicators so that two funds counting "clients reached" are counting the same thing. Read one core metric set and the striking thing is how much definitional work is needed before a single number can be collected.

SDG-washing. The commonest failure at this stage is mapping an existing portfolio onto the seventeen SDG icons after the fact. Every company on earth touches an SDG. A map drawn after the holdings were chosen on financial grounds is decoration — it fails the intention test, and it is the impact-investing analogue of the greenwashing patterns you named in Week 5.

Back to §7.1

The swap, settled.

You now have the vocabulary to answer the question you bet on at the start of the week.

Economically, the total return swap gives Léman the identical exposure to holding the shares, so on any principle of exposure-based accounting it should carry the identical financed emissions. Attribution methodologies built on ownership struggle with this, and reporting practice has lagged badly — which allows a reported figure to fall while nothing whatsoever changes.

But run the swap through this week's three tests and the picture is sharper still. Intention: the trade was done for exposure, not impact. Measurement: the emissions exist and are perfectly measurable; only the attribution is contested. Contribution: zero, and unambiguously so — no capital reached Rhône Cement, and Léman has given away the one lever that could have changed anything, the vote.

The swap is the purest object in this course: maximum exposure, zero contribution, and a carbon report that improves. Every question worth asking in sustainable finance is a way of detecting that combination.

Take it outside Léman Sustainable Capital

One claim, taken apart.

Find an impact claim and run it through the three tests

Any fund factsheet, any corporate sustainability report, any bank's press release — including your own employer's, which is the most interesting choice.

  1. Locate the number. Find the largest impact figure in the document — tonnes avoided, people reached, megawatts financed. Write it down exactly as printed.
  2. Walk the value chain. Is it an input, an activity, an output, an outcome or an impact? Almost always it is an output presented in the grammar of an impact.
  3. Apply the deadweight question. What share of it would have happened anyway? Estimate a range and say what evidence would narrow it.
  4. Ask about contribution. Was the capital primary or secondary? If secondary, what non-financial contribution is being claimed instead — and is it evidenced with a named company and a stated outcome?

Three questions to answer in writing afterwards: Which of the three tests would the claim fail in front of an informed client? What single additional disclosure would most improve it? And if you had to restate the claim in one honest sentence, what would it say — and would anyone still put it in a brochure?
