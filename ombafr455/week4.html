The risk the rulebook exists to manage

Three weeks of definitions, systems and law. Now the substance underneath all of it: risk. Climate change is a financial risk — it damages assets, reprices whole industries and lands its owners in court — and nature loss is right behind it. This week you learn to identify it (physical, transition, liability), measure it (Scope 1–2–3, financed emissions, carbon intensity, scenario analysis), disclose it (TCFD, now folded into the ISSB standards) and finally to see the frontier: TNFD and nature. Léman's risk committee has put "climate" on the agenda for the first time, and wants to know whether it belongs on the risk register at all. Section 4.1 · The Hook

Which risk bites first?

Your decision

The risk committee wants climate off the register.

Léman's risk committee is reviewing a CHF 400m European industrials sleeve. Climate has appeared on the agenda for the first time, and the Chief Risk Officer is unimpressed: "We're an asset manager, not an insurer. We don't own coastline. Whatever climate does, it does it in 2050 — long after this portfolio has turned over. Why is it on my register?"

It is a serious objection, and answering it badly is how firms end up holding assets nobody wants. Commit first: over the next five years, which of these is most likely to destroy value in that sleeve?

Place your bet — then lock it
Pick the one you'd bet on.
Locked. Over five years the answer is almost always the transition. Policy and technology move on political and commercial timescales — years, not centuries. A carbon price rises at the stroke of a pen; a cheaper technology strands an order book in a single product cycle. Physical risk is entirely real but back-loaded and location-specific; nature risk is rising fast and barely measured; litigation is growing but still concentrated. And the CRO's answer — "it's a 2050 problem" — is the single most expensive sentence in the field. It has a name: the tragedy of the horizon.
The sentence that started an industry

The tragedy of the horizon.

In 2015, Bank of England Governor Mark Carney stood up at Lloyd's of London and named the problem precisely: the catastrophic impacts of climate change fall beyond the horizon of most actors — beyond the business cycle, beyond the political cycle, beyond the mandate of the people who could act. So no one whose job depends on the next few years has a reason to price it.

"Once climate change becomes a defining issue for financial stability, it may already be too late." — Mark Carney, 2015

That speech is why TCFD exists, why supervisors run climate stress tests, and why your CRO's objection has an answer: the horizon problem is not a reason to ignore the risk, it is the reason the risk is mispriced — and a mispriced risk is exactly what an asset manager is paid to notice first.

Section 4.2 · Identify

The anatomy of climate risk

Three families

Climate hurts a balance sheet in three different ways.

"Climate risk" is not one thing, and treating it as one thing is why it gets managed badly. Split it:

  • Physical risk — the damage the climate itself does. Acute: a flood, a storm, a wildfire — discrete events. Chronic: sea-level rise, shifting rainfall, sustained heat — slow, permanent changes in the operating environment.
  • Transition risk — the damage the response does to whoever is on the wrong side of it. Four channels: policy & legal (carbon prices, bans, CBAM), technology (cheaper substitutes), market (customers and capital moving), and reputation.
  • Liability risk — being sued. Claims by those who suffered damage against those they hold responsible, and claims by investors who say they were misled about either the damage or the transition.

Note the perverse structure: the more successfully the world tackles physical risk, the larger transition risk becomes, and vice versa. You cannot be exposed to neither. A portfolio built to dodge the transition is simply a bet that the physical scenario wins.

The confusion worth avoiding

Risk to the company, not risk from it.

Last week's double materiality gives you the clean vocabulary. Everything in this section is financial materiality — outside-in: how a changing climate and a changing policy landscape hit the value of an asset you own.

A company's emissions, by contrast, are mostly an inside-out impact. They matter here only because they proxy for transition exposure: a high-emitting firm has more to lose when carbon gets priced. That is the whole logic linking §4.3's carbon metrics to risk — and also their central weakness, because a carbon footprint is a snapshot of impact being used as a forecast of risk.

Emissions are what you do to the world. Transition risk is what the world will do back.

Section 4.3 · Measure

Putting a number on it

Start with the emissions

Scope 1, 2 and 3 — and why Scope 3 is the whole game.

Before you can price transition risk you have to size the exposure, and the standard currency is greenhouse gas emissions in tCO₂e. The Greenhouse Gas Protocol splits them three ways:

  • Scope 1 — direct emissions from what the company owns or controls: its furnaces, its boilers, its fleet.
  • Scope 2 — indirect emissions from the energy it buys: the power station burning coal to run its factory.
  • Scope 3 — everything else in the value chain, up and down: purchased goods, business travel, and — usually the giant — the use of the products it sells.

Scope 3 is where the honesty lives. For a carmaker or an oil major, Scope 3 is typically 80–95% of the total, because it counts the fuel burned by everything they sold. A company reporting only Scopes 1 and 2 can look admirably clean while its actual climate exposure sits entirely in the part it left out. It is also the hardest to measure, frequently estimated, and rarely comparable between firms.

Judging a carmaker on Scopes 1 and 2 is judging a cigarette company on its factory chimney.

From a company's emissions to yours

Financed emissions and carbon intensity.

An investor doesn't emit much. Its footprint is the emissions it finances — and the market standard for computing that comes from PCAF. The idea is simple: you own a slice of the company, so you own that slice of its emissions.

financed emissions  =  your holding ÷ the company's enterprise value  ×  the company's emissions

That fraction is the attribution factor. Sum it across every holding and you have the portfolio's absolute footprint, in tonnes. It is the number a net-zero target is set against.

The other headline metric normalises instead of totalling: weighted average carbon intensity (WACI). Compute each company's emissions per million of revenue, then take the portfolio-weighted average.

WACI  =  Σ ( portfolio weight × company emissions ÷ company revenue )

The two answer different questions and can point in opposite directions. Financed emissions is a total — it grows if you simply invest more money, and shrinks if you sell. WACI is an efficiency measure — carbon per unit of economic activity — so it is comparable across portfolios of different sizes but tells you nothing about scale.

Financed emissions, holding by holding:

  • AlpTech: 40 ÷ 800 = 5% × 120 = 6 kt
  • Rhône Cement: 60 ÷ 600 = 10% × 900 = 90 kt
  • Léman Utilities: 100 ÷ 2,000 = 5% × 400 = 20 kt
  • Total financed emissions: 116 ktCO₂e

Now look at what that reveals. Rhône Cement is 30% of the money and 78% of the carbon. Almost the entire transition exposure of a €200m sleeve sits in one €60m position — which is precisely the kind of concentration a risk committee exists to find.

And the trap. Léman could cut its financed emissions by 78% tomorrow by selling Rhône Cement to someone else. The atmosphere would not notice. Divestment moves your number; only changing what the company does moves the world. That tension — exit versus engage — is the argument you will have in Week 6.
Looking forward, not backward

Scenario analysis: the only forward-looking tool you have.

Every metric above is a photograph of the past. Risk is about the future, and the standard way to interrogate it is scenario analysis: pick several coherent futures, run the portfolio through each, and see what breaks. Not a forecast — a stress test.

The reference set comes from the NGFS, the network of central banks and supervisors, and its scenarios sort into three families:

  • Orderly — policy tightens early and predictably (e.g. Net Zero 2050). Transition risk is real but manageable; physical risk is contained.
  • Disorderly — action is late, then abrupt (e.g. Delayed Transition). The worst case for transition risk: the same destination, reached by a sudden repricing.
  • Hot house world — policy never really arrives (e.g. Current Policies). Transition risk stays low; physical risk becomes severe and, past some thresholds, uninsurable.

Read the middle row twice. Delay does not remove transition risk — it concentrates it. A firm betting that regulation won't come is not hedged; it is short an option that gets more expensive the longer it stays out of the money.

Why two analysts get two different answers

Aggregate confusion.

You will meet this immediately in practice: ESG ratings of the same company from different providers correlate weakly — far more weakly than credit ratings, which agree almost perfectly. Researchers call it aggregate confusion, and it has three causes worth being able to name:

  • Scope — providers measure different things (one includes lobbying, another doesn't).
  • Measurement — they measure the same thing with different indicators.
  • Weighting — they combine the pieces with different weights.

This is not a reason to give up on the numbers; it is a reason to know what is inside the one you quote. When Léman puts a carbon figure in front of the German pension client, the defensible move is to state the metric, the scopes included, the data vintage and the provider — not to present a single score as a fact.

Section 4.4 · Disclose

TCFD — and how the ISSB absorbed it

Four pillars, eleven disclosures

The framework everything else copied.

The TCFD was created in 2015 by the Financial Stability Board — Carney's own institution acting on Carney's own speech — and reported in 2017. Its genius was structural rather than technical: instead of another metric, it gave the world one shape for climate reporting, in four pillars:

PillarThe question it answers
GovernanceWho is watching this, at board and management level?
StrategyWhat does climate do to our business model, over what horizons, under which scenarios?
Risk managementHow do we identify, assess and manage it — and is it wired into the main risk process?
Metrics & targetsWhat do we measure, what are we aiming at, and how are we doing?

Underneath sit eleven recommended disclosures, and one requirement that made the framework bite: strategy must be tested against scenarios, including a 2 °C-or-lower world. That single line forced boards to state, in public, what their business looks like if the transition actually happens.

Two design choices explain its spread. It is voluntary in form but adoptable by regulators — and dozens duly made it mandatory, Switzerland among them. And it reports into the financial filings, not a separate sustainability brochure.

The bit that catches people out in 2026

TCFD no longer exists — and that changes nothing about learning it.

The TCFD was disbanded in October 2023. Its work did not disappear; it was absorbed. The ISSB — the sustainability sibling of the accounting standard-setter that writes IFRS — published IFRS S2 Climate-related Disclosures, which fully incorporates the TCFD recommendations, and monitoring passed to the IFRS Foundation.

So the four pillars survive verbatim inside IFRS S2, alongside IFRS S1, the general sustainability-disclosure standard. What changed is status: TCFD was a recommendation; IFRS S1/S2 are standards that jurisdictions adopt into law.

Which leaves a Geneva manager reading three books at once — and this is the exam question. EU: CSRD/ESRS (double materiality — impact and financial). Global baseline: IFRS S1/S2 (investor-focused, financial materiality only). Switzerland: the Ordinance on Climate Disclosures already requires large Swiss companies to report along TCFD lines. Different books, one shape — because they all inherited it from TCFD.

The one-line distinction. ESRS asks "how does the company affect the world, and how does the world affect the company?" IFRS S2 asks only the second question. Same pillars, narrower lens.

Section 4.5 · The Frontier

Nature, and the TNFD

The half of the problem that has no price

Why nature is climate's harder sibling.

Carbon is, analytically, a gift: one basket of gases, one unit (tCO₂e), one global atmosphere. A tonne avoided in Vietnam is worth exactly a tonne avoided in Valais. Nature has none of those properties — and the economy depends on it anyway. Pollination, fresh water, stable soil, fish stocks, flood protection: all of it is an input that never appears on a balance sheet.

The TNFD published its recommendations in September 2023, and made one deliberate design choice that matters enormously in practice: it mirrors TCFD's four pillars exactly — governance, strategy, risk & impact management, metrics & targets. Nature reporting therefore bolts onto the machinery a firm already built for climate rather than starting again.

The move that changes the analysis

Dependencies, not just impacts.

Climate reporting mostly asks what you emit. Nature reporting insists on both directions at once:

  • Impacts — what the business does to nature: land converted, water withdrawn, habitat fragmented, pollution released.
  • Dependencies — what the business silently needs from nature: the aquifer that cools the plant, the pollinators behind the crop, the stable river that carries the barge.

Dependencies are the genuinely new idea for an investor, because they are invisible until they fail. A beverage company with a flawless emissions record can still be uninvestable if its bottling plants sit in basins heading for water stress. And unlike carbon, nature risk is local: the same activity is benign in one watershed and ruinous in the next, so the analysis starts with a map.

Which is why TNFD's assessment process, LEAP, begins where it does:

Locatewhere do we touch nature?
→
Evaluatewhich dependencies & impacts?
→
Assesswhich risks & opportunities?
→
Preparerespond and disclose

Note that Locate comes first and has no analogue in climate work. You cannot assess nature risk from a spreadsheet of holdings alone; you need to know where the assets physically are.

So what do you tell the risk committee?

That climate belongs on the register — not as an ethical position but as a concentration. One €60m holding carries 78% of the sleeve's transition exposure, and the committee did not know that an hour ago. That is a risk finding, in the register's own language.

And that the honest answer to "how big is it?" is a range, under stated scenarios, not a point estimate. Give the CRO the four boxes: identified (transition, concentrated in one name), measured (116 kt financed emissions, WACI 1,360, with the scopes and vintage stated), managed (engage first, with an exit trigger), disclosed (TCFD's four pillars, which the Swiss ordinance already expects). Nature comes next — start with Locate, not with a metric.

A risk you can name, size and put a scenario around stops being a belief and becomes a line item. That is the whole job.
