The market that puts a price on the promise

Four weeks of definitions, systems, rules and risk. This week the money finally moves. The sustainable debt market is the largest and oldest corner of sustainable finance — roughly $1 trillion of new issuance a year and over $6 trillion outstanding — and it works by attaching a promise to an ordinary bond. Two very different designs compete: use-of-proceeds bonds (green, social, sustainability), which ring-fence what the money buys, and sustainability-linked bonds, which leave the money free and put the issuer's targets in the contract instead. You will learn both, price the label (the greenium), and test whether a claim would survive the EU Green Bond Standard. Léman's fixed-income desk is being asked to build a CHF 250m labelled-bond sleeve, and this is also the machinery behind the Apple green bond in your written assignment. Section 5.1 · The Hook

Two bonds, one issuer

Your decision

The same company. The same balance sheet. Four basis points apart.

Léman's fixed-income desk has two new issues on the screen from Léman Utilities — the same holding you met in Week 4. Both are eight-year senior unsecured bonds, same currency, same seniority, same covenants, same rating. The only differences:

Your German pension client wants the green one. Your credit analyst wants the conventional one and calls the difference "a tax on virtue". Before you decide, be clear about what you would actually be buying. Commit first:

Place your bet — then lock it
Pick the one you'd defend to the client.
Locked. The honest answer is the second one, and it is more interesting than it looks. In a default you rank exactly where the conventional holder ranks — pari passu, same claim on the same balance sheet. Green proceeds are not collateral, and failing to spend them greenly is almost never an event of default. What you actually buy for your 4 bp is a contractual promise to report — an allocation report, an impact report, usually a second-party opinion — and a reputational hostage: an issuer caught misusing green proceeds pays in market access, not in litigation. That is a real thing to buy. It is just not the thing most people think they are buying.
The whole week in one sentence

A labelled bond is an ordinary bond plus a promise.

Everything this week follows from that. The bond half is unremarkable: an issuer borrows, pays a coupon, repays the principal, and you rank as a normal creditor. The promise half is where all the design, all the regulation and all the arguing happens — and there are only two ways to write it:

  • Promise what the money buys. Proceeds are earmarked for eligible projects. This is the use-of-proceeds family: green, social and sustainability bonds. §5.2.
  • Promise what the company becomes. The money is general-purpose, but the coupon changes if the issuer misses a stated target. This is the sustainability-linked bond. §5.3.

Then two questions the market cannot stop arguing about. What is the promise worth? — the greenium, §5.4. And who checks it? — second-party opinions, certification and the EU Green Bond Standard, §5.5.

Why this market matters more than its size suggests

The oldest, largest and most testable part of sustainable finance.

The first labelled bond was issued by the European Investment Bank in 2007 (a "Climate Awareness Bond"), followed by the World Bank's green bond in 2008, which invented the template still in use: eligibility criteria, a second opinion — CICERO's — and impact reporting. Everything since is a variation on those three moves.

Today the labelled market is around $1 trillion of new issuance a year, with cumulative aligned volumes past $6 trillion and some 17,000 bonds outstanding from more than 3,300 issuers. Green bonds are roughly two-thirds of it. That makes it comfortably the biggest single expression of sustainable finance in the world.

But size is not why it matters to you. It matters because it is the one place where a sustainability claim is written into a financial contract and priced by a market every day. An ESG rating is an opinion. A green bond is a security with a term sheet, a reporting obligation and a yield you can compare against an unlabelled twin. If you want to know whether sustainability claims are worth anything, this is where you can actually measure it.

Everywhere else in this course, sustainability is a claim. Here, it has a price.

Section 5.2 · Use of Proceeds

Promising what the money buys

Three labels, one mechanism

Green, social, sustainability — the family tree.

All three work identically. The issuer publishes a framework naming the categories of project it will fund, raises the money, tracks it, spends it on those categories and reports what happened. Only the categories differ:

  • Green bond — environmental projects only: renewable energy, energy efficiency, clean transport, green buildings, sustainable water and waste management, biodiversity. Governed by ICMA's Green Bond Principles.
  • Social bond — social projects with a defined target population: affordable housing, access to healthcare and education, employment generation, food security. Governed by the Social Bond Principles. This is the label that grew fastest during Covid.
  • Sustainability bond — a mix of both, in one instrument. Governed by the Sustainability Bond Guidelines, which simply say: apply the green principles to the green part and the social principles to the social part.

The three are collectively "GSS", and with sustainability-linked bonds added they become GSSS or GSS+ — the acronym you will meet in every market report.

Two cousins you should recognise. A blue bond is a green bond whose proceeds go to ocean and water projects — a marketing sub-label, not a separate standard. A transition bond funds a hard-to-abate issuer's move to lower carbon without the projects being green today; ICMA covers it with the Climate Transition Finance Handbook. Both sit inside the use-of-proceeds family.
The market's constitution

The four core components of the Green Bond Principles.

ICMA's Green Bond Principles are voluntary process guidelines, not law — and yet essentially the whole market complies with them, because investors will not buy a bond that does not. Learn the four components; they are the most examinable list in this chapter.

#Core componentThe question it answers
1Use of ProceedsWhich eligible green project categories will the money fund, and what environmental benefit is claimed?
2Process for Project Evaluation & SelectionHow does the issuer decide a project qualifies — who signs off, and against what criteria?
3Management of ProceedsHow is the money tracked and kept separate until spent — a sub-account, a sub-portfolio, an internal ledger?
4ReportingAnnual allocation reporting (where the money went) and, where feasible, impact reporting (what it achieved).

Two key recommendations sit alongside them and are, in practice, obligatory: publish a Green Bond Framework, and obtain an external review — almost always a second-party opinion. Note that the four components describe a process, not a performance threshold. The GBP never say a project must be good enough; they say you must tell everyone what you chose, how, where the money went and what it did. That distinction is the whole reason the EU built a standard on top — §5.5.

The Principles do not define green. They define disclosure about green — and then let investors judge.

The thing to be honest about

Fungibility, refinancing, and why "additionality" is the hard question.

Money is fungible. If Léman Utilities was always going to build the solar farm, and it funds it with a green bond instead of a conventional one, the world gets exactly the same solar farm — and the issuer gets a label. The green bond financed a project; it did not cause one. That gap has a name: additionality.

It gets sharper still, because refinancing is explicitly allowed under the GBP: proceeds may repay debt on projects completed years ago. Issuers must disclose the split between new financing and refinancing, and a look-back period — but a bond that refinances a wind farm built in 2019 changes nothing about 2026's emissions.

Three defences are worth knowing, because you will have to make them:

  1. Signalling and internal discipline. Issuing a green bond forces a company to build a taxonomy of its own projects, get a board to approve criteria and commit publicly to reporting. Firms consistently report that the first green bond changed their internal processes more than their capital cost.
  2. Investor base. A labelled deal reaches mandated buyers who cannot touch the conventional one. That is scarce, sticky demand.
  3. Cost of the alternative. Without labelled bonds there is no per-project environmental disclosure in fixed income at all. Imperfect visibility beats none.

The honest position — and the one to write in your assignment — is that use-of-proceeds bonds are strong on transparency and weak on additionality, and that anyone claiming otherwise is selling something.

Section 5.3 · Sustainability-Linked

Promising what you become

A different bet entirely

The instrument that finally reached the hard-to-abate issuer.

Use-of-proceeds bonds have a structural blind spot: they only work for issuers with a pile of green projects to point at. A cement maker has almost none — its problem is the core business. Ring-fencing €500m for a new office roof does nothing about the kiln.

The sustainability-linked bond, which ICMA codified in 2020, inverts the logic. The proceeds are general corporate purposes — spend them on anything. What is written into the contract instead is the issuer's own performance:

KPIwhat we measure
→
SPTthe target, and by when
→
Observation datewhen we check
→
Step-upthe coupon penalty if missed

So an SLB finances a whole company rather than a project, and the sustainability claim is about trajectory, not spending. For the hard-to-abate sectors that dominate transition risk — cement, steel, shipping, chemicals — this is the only labelled instrument that speaks to the actual problem.

Five core components

What the SLB Principles require.

#Core componentWhat "good" looks like
1Selection of KPIsMaterial to the issuer's core business, measurable, externally verifiable, benchmarkable. Scope 1+2 intensity for a cement maker — not the office recycling rate.
2Calibration of SPTsAmbitious: beyond business-as-usual, beyond the issuer's own past trend, and ideally against a science-based or peer benchmark.
3Bond characteristicsA financial consequence that is meaningful — typically a coupon step-up, most commonly 25 bp.
4ReportingKPI performance published at least annually, with enough information to recompute it.
5VerificationIndependent external verification of the KPI at each observation date — mandatory, unlike the GBP's recommended review.

Component 5 is a genuine strengthening: for a use-of-proceeds bond an external review is recommended; for an SLB, verification of performance against the SPT is required. The design also runs both ways in some deals — a step-down if a stretch target is beaten, as in Slovenia's 2025 sovereign SLB framework — though the step-up penalty remains the norm.

Worked example — Rhône Cement

What does a missed target actually cost?

Meet Week 4's problem child again. Rhône Cement is the €60m holding carrying 78% of the industrials sleeve's financed emissions. Divesting would move Léman's number and nothing else, so the desk is looking at engagement instead — and Rhône Cement is issuing an SLB:

TermRhône Cement 4.25% SLB 2033
Size€500m, general corporate purposes
KPIScope 1 CO₂ per tonne of cementitious product
SPT−20% vs a 2020 baseline
Observation date31 December 2030
Step-up+25 bp on all coupons after the observation date
Maturity31 December 2033 — three coupons after observation

Now do the arithmetic that decides whether this instrument has any teeth:

annual penalty = €500m × 25 bp = €1.25m
total penalty = €1.25m × 3 remaining years = €3.75m

And the cost of actually hitting the target — a kiln retrofit plus an alternative-fuel conversion — is around €180m.

So the penalty for failure is 2% of the cost of success. A treasurer optimising purely for cost would take the step-up every time. That single ratio is the entire critique of the SLB market, and it is why the instrument fell out of favour.

If missing the target is cheaper than hitting it, the target is not a target. It is a disclosure.

Two structural traps to check on any SLB term sheet. First, the call date: if the bond is callable before the observation date, the issuer can simply refinance out of the penalty — the step-up never happens. Second, the observation-to-maturity gap: a target observed one year before maturity puts one coupon at risk instead of three, cutting the economic stake by two-thirds. Both are legal, both are common, and both are invisible unless you look.

Why the market turned

The SLB's rise, and its four-year fall.

SLBs went from nothing to a record ~$115bn in 2021, and then declined for four consecutive years — down to roughly $35bn by 2024–25, less than half the $80bn annual average of 2021–23. It is the only part of the labelled market to have shrunk that hard. Investors, not regulators, did it, and they had four reasons:

  • Unambitious SPTs. Targets set at, or below, the trajectory the issuer was already on. A target you would hit anyway is a coupon discount, not a commitment.
  • Immaterial KPIs. A company measured on something that is not its problem — the classic being an emissions-intensity KPI for a firm growing volume fast enough that absolute emissions rise while intensity falls.
  • Penalties too small to matter — the 2%-of-capex arithmetic you just did.
  • Structural escapes. Call dates before observation dates; observation dates late in the bond's life; targets restated after issuance.

Do not read that as "SLBs are bad". Read it as the market doing exactly what it should: pricing a promise, finding it hollow and refusing to pay. The instrument is the only labelled structure that speaks to hard-to-abate sectors, and a well-calibrated SLB — material KPI, science-aligned SPT, an observation date early enough to matter and a step-up sized against the capex — is more useful than a green bond refinancing a building someone already built. The problem was never the design. It was the calibration.

The SLB market did not fail its investors. Its investors failed it — in the good sense, and on purpose.

Section 5.4 · The Price

The greenium

A number you can actually measure

What the greenium is, and how it is found.

The greenium is the yield discount a green bond commands over an otherwise identical conventional bond. If it is positive, investors are accepting a lower return for the label — which means, symmetrically, the issuer borrows more cheaply. It is quoted in basis points, and it is almost always small.

Measuring it is harder than it sounds, because "otherwise identical" almost never exists. Three approaches, in ascending order of credibility:

  1. Matched pairs. Find a green bond and a conventional bond from the same issuer with similar maturity and seniority, and take the spread difference. Clean in principle, rare in practice, and sensitive to liquidity differences.
  2. Curve fitting. Fit the issuer's conventional yield curve and measure how far the green bond sits below it. This is what most sell-side research does.
  3. Twin bonds. The gold standard, and Germany's invention: issue a green bond with exactly the same coupon and maturity as a conventional Bund, exchangeable one for the other. Everything is held constant by construction, so the yield gap is the greenium. Denmark uses the same design.
greenium (bp)  =  yieldconventional  −  yieldgreen

How big, really

Small, unstable, and bigger where the market is thinner.

Be careful here, because the honest answer is unglamorous and the exam rewards precision. The greenium exists, but it is a few basis points at most, it varies by segment, and it moves over time.

  • Developed-market investment grade: typically 0 to 5 bp, and statistically indistinguishable from zero in many samples. Recent work finds around 4 bp for bonds carrying an external review — and essentially nothing for those without one.
  • Sovereigns: small and shrinking. Denmark's twin-bond greenium was about 1.5 bp in 2025; the German twins have spent long stretches near zero.
  • Emerging markets: larger, because labelled supply is scarcer relative to mandated demand — Romania's 2024 green bond priced roughly 10 bp through its curve.
  • Over time: it widened as ESG funds grew in 2019–21, compressed sharply as supply caught up, and several 2025 studies find it oscillating around zero. Some researchers now argue there is no reliable greenium once issuer characteristics are stripped out.

The pattern underneath is simple supply and demand. A greenium appears where mandated buyers exceed labelled supply — Article 9 funds, green bond indices, central-bank collateral preferences and insurers with sustainability mandates all have to buy labelled paper. Where supply is plentiful, it collapses. It is a scarcity premium, not a reward for virtue, and it behaves exactly as you would expect a scarcity premium to behave.

The greenium is not the market paying for the planet. It is the market paying for a bond that a certain kind of buyer is obliged to own.

Worked example — is the label worth issuing?

Léman Utilities does the sums.

Back to the two bonds from §5.1. Léman Utilities can issue €300m over 8 years at 3.64% conventional or 3.60% green — a 4 bp greenium. Its treasurer wants to know whether the label pays for itself.

What it saves:

€300m × 4 bp = €120,000 a year  ×  8 years  =  €960,000

What it costs — and this is the part issuers underestimate:

  • Second-party opinion, one-off: €50,000
  • Framework drafting, legal and structuring: absorbed in the deal, but real
  • Annual allocation and impact reporting, tracking, assurance: €60,000 a year → €480,000 over eight years
net saving = €960,000 − €530,000 = €430,000

Positive — but thin, and it would go negative on a smaller or shorter deal. The break-even greenium here is 2.2 bp; below that the label costs more than it saves. On a €100m five-year deal, break-even is over 7 bp, which is more than the market pays. That is why the labelled market is dominated by large, frequent issuers: the fixed costs of the label only amortise over size and repetition.

And yet issuers keep doing it — including when the arithmetic is negative. Because the coupon saving is not the point. Léman Utilities' green tranche was 3.2× oversubscribed against 1.9× for the conventional, drew 40% new investors, and priced with a smaller new-issue concession. In a stressed market, the labelled deal is the one that still gets done. Issuers do not buy 4 bp; they buy demand certainty. Write that in the assignment and you will be ahead of most of the sell-side research.

Section 5.5 · Credibility

Who checks the promise

Three layers of assurance

From an opinion, to a certificate, to a regulation.

If the label is a promise, someone has to stand behind it. The market built three layers, in increasing order of strictness — and it is worth being able to say exactly what each one does and does not test.

LayerWhat it checksWhat it does not check
Second-party opinion
Sustainalytics, ISS ESG, Moody's, S&P, CICERO		That the framework is aligned with the GBP or SLBP, and that the categories are plausibly greenWhether the projects perform. It is an opinion on a document, paid for by the issuer
Certification
Climate Bonds Standard		That assets meet science-based sector criteria, verified by an approved verifier pre- and post-issuanceAnything outside the certified assets — and it covers only sectors with published criteria
Regulation
EU Green Bond Standard		Taxonomy alignment of proceeds, reviewer independence and competence, mandatory templates, supervised by ESMAAnything the Taxonomy has not written criteria for — hence the flexibility pocket

Note the shape: each layer fixes the previous layer's main weakness. SPOs are issuer-paid opinions on documents, so certification added independent verification against science-based criteria. Certification covers only some sectors and remains voluntary, so the EU wrote a standard with a legal definition of green underneath it and a supervisor on top.

The strictest label in the world

The European Green Bond Standard.

Regulation (EU) 2023/2631 created the European Green Bond designation — "EuGB". It has applied since 21 December 2024. It is voluntary: nobody must use it. But the label is protected — call your bond a European green bond and every requirement below becomes mandatory and supervised.

  • The 85% rule. At least 85% of proceeds must fund activities that are aligned with the EU Taxonomy — Week 3's three tests: substantial contribution, DNSH, and minimum safeguards. This is the move nobody else made: it plugs the label into a legal definition of green rather than an issuer's own judgement.
  • The 15% flexibility pocket. Up to 15% may go to activities that meet the Taxonomy in every respect except that no technical screening criteria have been written for them yet. It exists because the Taxonomy is incomplete — not as a discretionary allowance for anything the issuer likes.
  • An external reviewer registered with ESMA. Not just any consultant: reviewers must register, meet competence and independence requirements, and are supervised, with fines from €20,000 to €200,000. The transitional regime for verifiers ran to 21 June 2026 — so from this summer, registration is the only route.
  • Mandatory templates. A pre-issuance European green bond factsheet reviewed before issuance; allocation reports every 12 months until proceeds are fully allocated; at least one impact report over the bond's life; all kept on the website for a year past maturity.

Uptake was expected to be slow, and it was — the bar is genuinely high, and Taxonomy-aligning a whole project pool is expensive. But it is now moving: corporates issued around €11bn of EuGBs in 2025, Denmark became the first sovereign with a €900m deal, and by 2026 more than 9% of European green bond volumes carry the label, including the first EuGB covered bonds. For a Geneva manager the practical point is simple: EuGB is the only green label in the world backed by a statutory definition of green and a financial supervisor. When your client asks which label to trust, that is the answer — with the caveat that it says nothing about additionality.

Switzerland, as usual, is next door rather than inside. Swiss issuers can and do issue EuGBs when they place into the EU, but there is no Swiss equivalent — the domestic route is ICMA alignment plus a second-party opinion, with the Swiss Climate Scores for transparency and AMAS self-regulation for asset managers. The Confederation's own first green bond, in October 2022, raised CHF 766m against an ICMA-aligned framework. Léman therefore has to be fluent in both books — exactly the position Week 3 left you in.

The four failure modes

How a labelled bond becomes greenwashing.

Week 1 taught you to spot greenwashing in a fund. Here is the fixed-income version. Almost every controversy fits one of four patterns, and naming the pattern is how you turn a suspicion into a question you can put to an issuer:

  1. The category is too loose. "Green buildings" that are ordinary offices scraping a certification; "clean transport" that includes fleet renewal for diesel vans. Ask: what threshold does a project have to clear, and who set it?
  2. The proceeds refinance the past. A large refinancing share with a long look-back period funds nothing new. Ask: what percentage is new financing, and what is the look-back period?
  3. The rest of the balance sheet contradicts it. The classic — an oil major's green bond for a solar portfolio while capex to expand production continues. Nothing in the GBP prohibits this, which is precisely the point. Ask: what is the issuer's total capital allocation, not the labelled slice?
  4. The target has no teeth. The SLB version: an unambitious SPT, an immaterial KPI, or a step-up too small or too late to matter. Ask: what does missing it cost, as a share of what hitting it costs?

Note that none of these is fraud, and almost none breaches the Principles. That is the uncomfortable finding of this week: the labelled bond market is largely honest about what it promises, and the promise is simply narrower than buyers assume. Which is why your job as an analyst is not to detect lying. It is to read the framework and know what was never promised in the first place.

The greenwashing in this market is rarely in what the document says. It is in what the reader assumes it says.

The case in your assignment

Apple, and how to structure the question.

Your written assignment (due Sunday 6 September) concerns Apple's green bonds. Everything you need to analyse it properly is now in your hands, so here is the shape of the programme and the questions worth asking of it.

IssueSizeContext
February 2016$1.5bnThe largest green bond by a US corporate at the time
June 2017$1.0bnIssued weeks after the US announced its intention to leave the Paris Agreement — deliberately timed as a signal
November 2019€2.0bnTwo euro tranches (0.000% due 2025, 0.500% due 2031), taking the programme to ~$4.7bn
ReportingAnnual Green Bond Impact Report; second-party opinion from Sustainalytics; use-of-proceeds examination by Ernst & Young

Eligible categories span low-carbon design and engineering, energy efficiency, renewable energy, carbon mitigation and carbon sequestration. The reported impact runs to hundreds of megawatts of installed renewable capacity and multi-million-tonne lifetime CO₂e reductions.

Five questions that will separate a strong assignment from a summary:

  1. Structure. Why use-of-proceeds and not an SLB? What does Apple's project mix make possible that Rhône Cement's does not?
  2. Additionality. Apple is one of the most cash-rich companies on earth and could fund every eligible project from its balance sheet. What, then, is the bond for? Is your answer signalling, supplier leverage, investor diversification — or nothing?
  3. Timing. The June 2017 issue landed just after the Paris withdrawal announcement. Read that as corporate strategy, not coincidence — what was being bought?
  4. Verification. An issuer-paid second-party opinion plus an auditor's use-of-proceeds examination. What does each one actually attest to, and what remains unattested?
  5. Counterfactual. If Apple had never issued a green bond, what would be different in the world? Answer that honestly and you have the additionality debate in a single paragraph.
Method note. Use the primary sources: Apple's Green Bond Framework, its Annual Green Bond Impact Report and the Sustainalytics opinion, all public. Quote the framework's own words when you assess whether a claim is met — an assignment that tests the issuer against its own stated criteria is far stronger than one that tests it against a general feeling about Apple.

Take it outside Léman Sustainable Capital

One bond, read properly — and it doesn't have to be Apple's.

The ideas in this course make sense inside the case and then evaporate on the way to your own work. So each week now ends with one small thing to do on material that is actually yours. It is not graded and nobody sees it — it exists because the step from "I followed that" to "I can do that" only happens on your own material.

Pick whichever one is closest to you

  1. Your own employer, or your bank. Search "[company name] green bond framework". Most large banks, utilities, insurers and property groups have one, and they are all public PDFs. Find the four core components in it — they will be there, in that order. Then find the one thing the framework does not promise.
  2. Your country. Nearly every European sovereign now has a green bond framework and an allocation report. Read the allocation report and ask the awkward question: how much of this was new spending, and how much was existing budget lines relabelled?
  3. A pension or fund you hold. If you own a bond fund, look up whether it holds labelled paper and what it says about it. Then check whether that claim is Article 8 or Article 9 under SFDR — Week 3's vocabulary, doing real work.

Then answer the three questions that matter more than the arithmetic: what exactly was promised — proceeds, or performance? · who verified it, and who paid them? · if the promise were quietly broken, what would actually happen to the issuer? That last question is the whole of §5.1, and it is the one that separates people who can read a term sheet from people who read the press release.

Bring what you find to the discussion forum if you would like a second pair of eyes on it — especially the frameworks that turned out to be weaker than the marketing. Those are the more interesting posts.
