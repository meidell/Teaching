Read this first

Three things that will save you marks

The acronyms are not interchangeable, and the exam knows it.
ESG, SRI, SFDR, CSRD, ESRS, TCFD, TNFD, ISSB, GBP, SLBP, PCAF, NGFS, EuGB. Several of them differ by one letter and describe completely different things. If you can only do one revision exercise, cover the right-hand column of this page and say what each acronym stands for and who it applies to.
Almost every term has a paired opposite.
Eligible/aligned, impact/dependency, inside-out/outside-in, physical/transition, use-of-proceeds/sustainability-linked, Article 8/Article 9. The "Same words, different jobs" card at the bottom of this page collects them. Learning terms in pairs is roughly twice as efficient as learning them singly.
A definition you cannot apply is not learned.
For each entry, the useful test is not "can I recite this?" but "could I decide a borderline case with it?" — is this project Taxonomy-aligned, is this fund Article 8 or 9, is this bond an EuGB. The classification labs in each week's lesson exist for exactly that reason.
Week 1 · Introduction to sustainable investing

The vocabulary of the field

Week 1 is mostly definitions — but they are the definitions every later week assumes you already have.

TermWhat it means — and the trapWhere
ESGEnvironmental, Social and Governance — the three groups of non-financial factors used to assess how sustainably a company or asset behaves. Trap: ESG is a set of factors, not a strategy and not a synonym for "ethical". A fund can use ESG data and still hold an oil major.§1.2
SRISocially Responsible Investing — the older umbrella term, usually built on excluding harmful sectors on ethical grounds. Trap: SRI starts from values; ESG integration starts from financial materiality. They can reach opposite conclusions about the same holding.§1.2
ESG integrationSystematically folding material ESG risks and opportunities into mainstream financial analysis and valuation. The largest strategy by assets. Trap: it makes no promise about outcomes — only about the process of analysis.§1.4
impact investingInvesting with the intention to generate a measurable positive social or environmental outcome alongside a financial return. Trap: intention and measurement are both required. Without measurement it is aspiration; without intention it is coincidence.§1.4
spectrum of capitalThe continuum from purely financial investing, through responsible and sustainable, to impact-first and philanthropy — positioned by intent and by the role of financial return. Trap: the axis is intent, not asset class.§1.3
stewardshipUsing ownership rights — voting and engagement — to influence how investee companies behave. Trap: it is the alternative to divestment, and the two have opposite effects on your reported portfolio metrics. See Week 4's exit-versus-engage tension.§1.4
PRIPrinciples for Responsible Investment — a UN-supported voluntary set of six principles; signatories commit to incorporate ESG and to report on progress. Trap: signing is a commitment to process and reporting, not to any outcome, and the PRI can delist signatories who fail to report.§1.5
greenwashingPresenting an investment or company as more sustainable than it really is — a label or claim without the substance, method or reporting behind it. Trap: most greenwashing is not lying. It is a true statement placed where a reader will over-read it. See Week 5's four failure modes.§1.6
Week 2 · Building a sustainable financial system

Market failures, actors and the plan

Why the market misprices sustainability in the first place, and who was assigned to fix it.

TermWhat it means — and the trapWhere
externalityA cost (or benefit) of an activity borne by someone other than the decision-maker — pollution paid for by society rather than the polluter. The classic reason markets misprice sustainability. Trap: the fix is to internalise it (a carbon price, a regulation, a liability), not to appeal to conscience.§2.2
tragedy of the horizonMark Carney's 2015 term: the worst climate costs fall beyond the horizon of business, political and central-banking decision-makers, so today's prices under-weight them. Trap: it is a statement about incentives and time, not about the science.§2.2
information asymmetryThe buyer cannot verify what the seller claims — the market failure that disclosure regulation exists to fix. Trap: disclosure rules do not make companies greener. They make claims checkable, which is a different and more modest goal.§2.2
asset ownerThe institution that owns the capital — a pension fund, insurer or sovereign fund. It sets the mandate that asset managers must follow. Trap: the asset owner is where sustainability preferences enter the system; a manager without a mandate cannot invent one.§2.3
fiduciary dutyThe legal duty to act in beneficiaries' best interests — now widely interpreted to require considering financially material ESG risks, not to forbid it. Trap: the old objection ("fiduciary duty prevents us") has been reversed by exactly this reinterpretation.§2.3
Sustainable Finance
Action Plan		The European Commission's 2018 plan, organised around three objectives: reorient capital toward sustainable investment, manage risk from climate and environmental change, and foster transparency and long-termism. Trap: everything in Week 3 — Taxonomy, SFDR, CSRD — is an output of this one document.§2.4
NGFSNetwork for Greening the Financial System — 100+ central banks and supervisors sharing methods and scenarios for climate-related financial risk. Trap: it has no legal power. Its influence comes from its scenarios, which supervisors then impose through stress tests.§2.4
ISSBInternational Sustainability Standards Board, housed in the IFRS Foundation — sets a global baseline for sustainability disclosure (IFRS S1/S2), consolidating the earlier TCFD framework. Trap: a baseline, not a ceiling: jurisdictions adopt it into law and may add to it.§2.5
Week 3 · Regulatory & policy environment

The rulebook — EU and Swiss

The heaviest week for terminology, and the one the exam mines hardest. Learn the pairs, not the singles.

TermWhat it means — and the trapWhere
EU TaxonomyRegulation (EU) 2020/852 — a classification system defining which economic activities count as environmentally sustainable, against six environmental objectives. The dictionary the rest of the rulebook points at. Trap: it defines activities, not companies and not funds.§3.2
Taxonomy-eligibleAn activity that is described by the Taxonomy, so criteria exist to assess it. Trap: eligibility says nothing about performance. It means the activity can be assessed — nothing more. A high eligibility number is not a green credential.§3.2
Taxonomy-alignedAn eligible activity that actually passes all three tests: substantial contribution to one objective, DNSH on the other five, and minimum social safeguards. Trap: aligned = it passed. This is the pair students most often collapse into one word.§3.2
DNSHDo No Significant Harm — to be green, an activity must substantially help one environmental objective while not significantly harming any of the other five. Trap: it is a veto, not a score. Failing DNSH once disqualifies the activity however good the contribution.§3.2
minimum safeguardsThe social floor in the Taxonomy: alignment with the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises and the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights. Trap: a purely environmental test can still be failed on social grounds.§3.2
SFDRSustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation (EU) 2019/2088 — requires funds and managers to disclose how they handle sustainability. Trap: it is a disclosure regime, not a labelling regime. The market turned Articles 8 and 9 into labels; the regulator never intended them as such.§3.3
Article 6SFDR baseline: a product with no sustainability claim; the manager discloses only how it integrates sustainability risk. Trap: Article 6 is not "bad". It is the honest label for a conventional product.§3.3
Article 8SFDR "light green": a product that promotes environmental or social characteristics but does not have sustainable investment as its objective. Trap: by far the largest and vaguest category — "promotes" is doing enormous work in that sentence.§3.3
Article 9SFDR "dark green": a product whose objective is sustainable investment — in practice held to almost 100% sustainable investments. Trap: the bar tightened after 2022, causing a wave of Article 9 → Article 8 downgrades. That reclassification is a favourite exam example.§3.3
PAIPrincipal Adverse Impacts — the negative effects of investment decisions on sustainability factors (carbon, biodiversity, board diversity…), reported on standard SFDR indicators. Trap: PAIs are the inside-out half of SFDR — impact on the world, not risk to the fund.§3.3
CSRDCorporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (EU) 2022/2464 — greatly widens which companies must report sustainability information, using the ESRS standards. Replaced the NFRD. Trap: CSRD governs companies; SFDR governs financial products. Different addressees.§3.4
NFRDNon-Financial Reporting Directive (2014) — CSRD's narrower predecessor, covering only large public-interest entities. Superseded. Trap: quoting NFRD scope as though it were current is a dated-answer giveaway.§3.4
ESRSEuropean Sustainability Reporting Standards — the twelve standards specifying what CSRD reporters must disclose: two cross-cutting, five environmental, four social, one governance. Trap: ESRS is the content; CSRD is the obligation to produce it.§3.4
double materialityCSRD's test: report a matter if it is material either as the company's impact on people and planet (inside-out) or as an effect on the company's own value (outside-in) — or both. Trap: it is an OR, not an AND. Either limb triggers reporting.§3.4
limited assuranceA moderate level of external audit assurance ("nothing came to our attention"), the initial CSRD requirement, expected to move toward fuller reasonable assurance over time. Trap: limited assurance is much weaker than a financial audit — and the difference is examinable.§3.4
OmnibusThe EU's 2025 simplification package for sustainable-finance rules. It delayed later CSRD waves ("stop the clock") and proposed raising size thresholds, cutting the number of companies in scope. Trap: it changes who reports and when — not the underlying concepts.§3.5
FINMAThe Swiss Financial Market Supervisory Authority. On sustainability its focus is preventing greenwashing — ensuring clients are not misled — and supervising climate and nature-related risk. Trap: FINMA supervises conduct; it does not run a Swiss taxonomy, because there isn't one.§3.6
self-regulationThe Swiss approach: industry bodies (AMAS for asset managers, the SBA for banks) set binding rules for their members instead of state legislation. The Federal Council backed this route in 2024. Trap: "self-regulation" here is binding on members — not voluntary best practice.§3.6
Swiss Climate
Scores		A voluntary set of transparency indicators (2022) showing how aligned a portfolio or product is with the Paris Agreement. Trap: Swiss best practice, not a binding label — the closest Switzerland comes to SFDR, and deliberately much lighter.§3.6
Ordinance on
Climate Disclosures		The Swiss ordinance requiring large Swiss companies to report along TCFD lines. Trap: Switzerland made TCFD mandatory for reporters while declining an EU-style taxonomy — light on classification, firm on disclosure.§3.6
Week 4 · Risk management & measurement

Identify, measure, disclose

Climate as a financial risk — and the metrics that try to size it, each with a blind spot worth naming.

TermWhat it means — and the trapWhere
physical riskFinancial risk from the climate itself. Acute: discrete events — floods, storms, wildfires. Chronic: sustained shifts — sea-level rise, heat, changed rainfall. Trap: the acute/chronic split is within physical risk. It does not apply to transition risk.§4.2
transition riskFinancial risk from the response to climate change, through four channels: policy & legal, technology, market, reputation. Trap: the more successfully the world tackles physical risk, the larger transition risk becomes. You cannot be exposed to neither.§4.2
liability riskThe risk of being sued over climate — by parties who suffered damage, or by investors who say they were misled about the damage or the transition. Trap: sometimes treated as a branch of transition risk; know both framings.§4.2
tCO₂eTonnes of carbon-dioxide equivalent — converts every greenhouse gas into the amount of CO₂ with the same warming effect, so they can be added. Trap: 1 kt = 1,000 t. Unit slips are the most common arithmetic error in this course.§4.3
Scope 1Direct emissions from sources the company owns or controls — furnaces, boilers, vehicles.§4.3
Scope 2Indirect emissions from purchased energy: the electricity, heat or steam generated elsewhere and consumed by the company.§4.3
Scope 3All other value-chain emissions, up and down — purchased goods, transport and, usually the giant, the use of sold products. Often 80–95% of the total. Trap: a firm reporting only Scopes 1 and 2 can look clean while its whole exposure sits in the omitted part.§4.3
PCAFPartnership for Carbon Accounting Financials — the standard defining how financial institutions measure financed emissions, using an attribution factor (holding ÷ enterprise value). Trap: PCAF is the method; financed emissions is the output.§4.3
financed emissionsThe emissions attributed to an investor: attribution factor × the company's emissions, summed across holdings. An absolute figure, in tonnes. Trap: it falls when you simply sell. Divestment flatters the metric and changes nothing in the atmosphere.§4.3
WACIWeighted average carbon intensity — the portfolio-weighted average of each holding's emissions per unit of revenue. An efficiency measure. Trap: comparable across portfolio sizes but silent about total tonnes — and revenue moves it without emissions changing at all.§4.3
scenario analysisTesting a portfolio against several coherent futures rather than forecasting one. NGFS families: orderly, disorderly, hot house world. Trap: the worst case for transition risk is disorderly — delay concentrates the repricing, it does not remove it.§4.3
aggregate confusionThe finding that ESG ratings of the same company correlate weakly across providers, because of differences in scope, measurement and weighting. Trap: not a reason to abandon the numbers — a reason to state which metric, whose data, and as of when.§4.3
TCFDTask Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures — created by the FSB in 2015, reported 2017, disbanded October 2023. Four pillars — governance, strategy, risk management, metrics & targets — and eleven recommended disclosures. Trap: the taskforce ended; the framework did not.§4.4
IFRS S1 / S2The ISSB's general (S1) and climate (S2) disclosure standards. IFRS S2 fully incorporates the TCFD recommendations. Trap: S2 applies financial materiality only — narrower than ESRS's double materiality. Same four pillars, narrower lens.§4.4
TNFDTaskforce on Nature-related Financial Disclosures — recommendations published September 2023, deliberately mirroring TCFD's four pillars so nature reporting bolts onto existing climate machinery. Trap: the pillars are the same; the analysis is not.§4.5
LEAPTNFD's assessment process: Locate your interface with nature, Evaluate dependencies and impacts, Assess risks and opportunities, Prepare to respond and disclose. Trap: it starts with Locate because nature risk is local — there is no analogue in climate work.§4.5
dependency
(vs impact)		What a business silently needs from nature — the aquifer cooling the plant, the pollinators behind the crop. TNFD's genuinely new idea for an investor. Trap: impacts are what you do to nature; dependencies are what nature does for you. Both are required.§4.5
Financed emissions
financed emissions = (holding ÷ enterprise value) × company emissions
the attribution factor, times the emissions — summed over every holding
Weighted average carbon intensity
WACI = Σ ( portfolio weight × company emissions ÷ company revenue )
an efficiency measure, in tCO₂e per €m of revenue — unchanged if you scale the whole portfolio
Week 5 · The sustainable debt market

Labels, structures and the price of a promise

A labelled bond is an ordinary bond plus a promise. Everything here is either the promise, its price, or its verification.

TermWhat it means — and the trapWhere
use-of-proceeds bondA bond whose proceeds are earmarked for eligible projects — the green, social and sustainability family. The promise is about what the money buys. Trap: earmarking is contractual and reputational, not a security interest. You do not rank ahead of anyone.§5.1
pari passu"On equal footing" — a green bondholder ranks exactly alongside a conventional holder of the same seniority in a default. Trap: this is the most common misconception in the market. The label buys reporting and reputational exposure, not priority.§5.1
green bondA use-of-proceeds bond funding environmental projects: renewable energy, energy efficiency, clean transport, green buildings, sustainable water and waste, biodiversity. Trap: "green bond" is often used loosely for the whole labelled market. Use "labelled bond" when you mean all four structures.§5.2
social bondA use-of-proceeds bond funding social projects for a defined target population — affordable housing, healthcare and education access, employment, food security. Trap: the target-population requirement is the thing the Social Bond Principles add. Naming it is often the mark.§5.2
sustainability bondA use-of-proceeds bond financing a mix of green and social projects. Trap: do not confuse it with a sustainability-linked bond. One mixes proceeds; the other links a coupon to a target. They are the most-confused pair in the chapter.§5.2
GBPICMA's Green Bond Principles. Four core components: use of proceeds; process for project evaluation & selection; management of proceeds; reporting. Plus two key recommendations: a framework, and an external review. Trap: the GBP govern process and disclosure, never a performance threshold.§5.2
SBPICMA's Social Bond Principles — the same four core components applied to social categories, plus the identified target population.§5.2
SBGICMA's Sustainability Bond Guidelines. They add no new components: apply the green principles to the green part and the social principles to the social part.§5.2
SLBSustainability-linked bond — proceeds are general corporate purposes; the coupon changes if the issuer misses a stated target. The promise is about what the company becomes. Trap: the only labelled structure available to a hard-to-abate issuer with no green project pool.§5.3
SLBPICMA's Sustainability-Linked Bond Principles. Five core components: selection of KPIs; calibration of SPTs; bond characteristics; reporting; verification. Trap: verification is mandatory here, where an external review is only recommended under the GBP.§5.3
KPI (in an SLB)The indicator the coupon is linked to. Must be material to the core business, measurable, externally verifiable and benchmarkable. Trap: an intensity KPI lets absolute emissions rise while the KPI improves, if volume grows fast enough. Look for both.§5.3
SPTSustainability performance target — the level the KPI must reach by the observation date. Required to be ambitious: beyond business as usual and beyond the issuer's own trend. Trap: a target you would hit anyway is a coupon discount, not a commitment.§5.3
observation dateThe date the KPI is measured against the SPT. Trap: its distance from maturity decides how many coupons are actually at risk — and a call date before it lets the issuer refinance out of the penalty entirely.§5.3
coupon step-upThe increase in coupon triggered by a missed SPT — most commonly 25 bp. Some deals also carry a step-down for beating a stretch target. Trap: judge it against the capex needed to hit the target. On the Rhône Cement deal it is 2% of it.§5.3
greeniumThe yield discount a green bond commands over an otherwise identical conventional bond — equivalently, the issuer's borrowing saving. Typically 0–5 bp in developed-market investment grade, larger where labelled supply is scarce. Trap: it is a scarcity premium driven by mandated buyers, not a reward for virtue — and several 2025 studies find it near zero.§5.4
bpBasis point = 0.01% = 0.0001. Trap: every arithmetic slip in this chapter is a factor of 100 in the wrong direction. €300m × 4 bp = €120,000, not €1.2m.§5.4
twin bondA sovereign green bond issued with exactly the same coupon and maturity as a conventional bond, and exchangeable for it — Germany's design, also used by Denmark. Trap: it is the cleanest greenium measurement that exists, because everything else is held constant by construction.§5.4
break-even greeniumThe greenium at which the label exactly pays for itself: total label cost ÷ (issue size × tenor) × 10,000. Trap: it falls with size and tenor, which is why the labelled market is dominated by large, frequent issuers. On €100m over 5 years it is ~7 bp — more than the market pays.§5.4
additionalityWhether the financing caused something that otherwise would not have happened. Trap: the hardest question for use-of-proceeds bonds, because money is fungible and refinancing existing projects is explicitly allowed. Strong on transparency, weak on additionality — say that, and say why.§5.2
SPOSecond-party opinion — an independent provider's opinion that a framework aligns with the relevant Principles. Trap: it assesses a document, not project performance, and the issuer pays for it. That gap is why certification and the EuGB exist.§5.5
Climate Bonds
Certification		The Climate Bonds Initiative's stricter route: assets must meet science-based sector criteria, verified pre- and post-issuance by an approved verifier. Trap: it covers only sectors with published criteria, and remains voluntary.§5.5
EuGBEuropean green bond, under Regulation (EU) 2023/2631, applying since 21 December 2024. Requires ≥85% of proceeds Taxonomy-aligned, an ESMA-registered external reviewer, a pre-issuance factsheet, annual allocation reports and at least one impact report. Trap: voluntary to use, mandatory once used — and the designation is only available to use-of-proceeds bonds.§5.5
flexibility pocketThe 15% of EuGB proceeds that may fund activities meeting the Taxonomy in every respect except that no technical screening criteria exist for them yet. Trap: it is a fix for an incomplete rulebook, not discretion to fund whatever the issuer likes.§5.5
allocation reportThe annual disclosure of where a labelled bond's proceeds actually went, by category, until fully allocated. Recommended by the GBP; mandatory for an EuGB.§5.2
impact reportThe disclosure of what the funded projects achieved — MWh generated, tCO₂e avoided, homes built. "Where feasible" under the GBP; at least one is mandatory over an EuGB's life. Trap: impact reporting methodologies differ between issuers, so the numbers rarely compare.§5.2
transition bondA use-of-proceeds bond for a hard-to-abate issuer whose activities are not green today but move it toward lower carbon. Covered by ICMA's Climate Transition Finance Handbook. Trap: not eligible for the EuGB label unless the activities are Taxonomy-aligned.§5.2
blue bondA green bond whose proceeds fund ocean, marine and water projects. Trap: a marketing sub-label inside the green bond family — not a separate standard with its own principles.§5.2
GSSS / GSS+Green, Social, Sustainability and Sustainability-linked — the collective name for the labelled market. Roughly $1 trillion of new issuance a year, cumulative aligned volumes past $6 trillion, some 17,000 bonds from 3,300+ issuers. Green bonds are about two-thirds of it.§5.1

Week 6 · The sustainable equity market

Screens, metrics and the vote

Equity gives you no contract — only a screen and a voice. Every term here is either a way of choosing what to hold, a way of measuring it, or a way of pushing the companies you already own.

TermWhat it means — and the trapWhere
exclusionary screeningRemoving sectors or activities from the universe — tobacco, controversial weapons, thermal coal above a revenue threshold. The oldest and simplest strategy. Trap: the threshold is the whole policy. "We exclude coal" at a 30% revenue threshold excludes almost nobody; at 5% it bites. Always read the number.§6.2
norms-based screeningExcluding issuers assessed as breaching international norms — UN Global Compact, ILO conventions, OECD Guidelines. Trap: it rests entirely on a data vendor's violation flag, so two managers using different vendors can hold opposite positions on the same company and both call it norms-based.§6.2
best-in-classHolding only the strongest ESG performers within each sector, keeping sector weights near the benchmark. Trap: designed for low tracking error, so it keeps every sector represented. You end up owning the best-run oil major — which is still an oil major.§6.2
ESG integrationFeeding ESG data into ordinary financial analysis as a source of risk and return. Trap: it promises no outcome. "Considered where material" has no threshold, no target and nothing to audit — the most over-claimed of the five strategies.§6.2
thematic & impactBuilding from a sustainability outcome — clean energy, water, health — with impact additionally requiring measurable additional effect. Trap: concentrated and volatile, and in listed secondary markets additionality is very hard to claim because your money went to the seller.§6.2
SFDR Article 8A product that promotes environmental or social characteristics — "light green". Trap: a single exclusion can satisfy it, which is why the category is enormous. The label alone tells a client almost nothing.§6.2
SFDR Article 9A product with sustainable investment as its objective — "dark green". Trap: when the standard was clarified, managers downgraded hundreds of billions of euros to Article 8 rather than defend the claim. Read the downgrade wave as disclosure working, not as funds getting worse.§6.2
WACIWeighted-average carbon intensity: Σ(weight × intensity), in tCO₂e per CHF m of revenue. Trap: it is an intensity, so it does not move when the fund grows — and it improves fastest when you simply sell your worst holding. It is not your share of absolute emissions; that is financed emissions (Week 4).§6.3
tracking errorThe standard deviation of portfolio return minus benchmark return, in bp a year — the price of a screen. Trap: it is not linear. Shallow exclusions are nearly free; the cost accelerates once you remove whole sectors and pick up unintended factor bets.§6.3
stewardshipUsing ownership rights to influence a company. Trap: it bundles two very different activities — voting (public, annual, on the record) and engagement (private, continuous, unverifiable). Conflating them is the commonest error in a client meeting.§6.4
proxy votingVoting your shares at the AGM without attending — on directors, pay, auditors and any resolution reaching the ballot. Trap: voting records are public. A manager claiming climate engagement while voting with management all year is the easiest inconsistency to expose.§6.4
say on climateAn advisory shareholder vote on a company's climate transition plan, modelled on say-on-pay. Trap: advisory means non-binding — its force is reputational, and a low approval rate matters more than the pass/fail.§6.4
escalation ladderThe ordered sequence when an ask is ignored: private letter → meeting → coalition → public statement → vote against the board → file a resolution → divest. Trap: an engagement that never escalates is indistinguishable from doing nothing. A credible policy names the rungs, the deadlines and the exit.§6.4
divestmentSelling a holding on sustainability grounds. Trap: on the secondary market it transfers shares and changes nothing physical — while improving your reported carbon intensity dramatically. Credible only when pre-announced as the consequence of a missed, dated target.§6.5
cost-of-capital channelThe serious mechanism behind divestment: at scale, a shrinking buyer pool raises a sector's cost of new capital until marginal projects stop clearing their hurdle rate. Trap: it is a market-level instrument. One investor cannot operate it alone, and pretending otherwise is the weakest argument in the field.§6.5
additionalityWhether your money caused something that would not otherwise have happened. Trap: plausible in primary issuance and private markets, weak in listed secondary markets. This is the word Week 7 is built around.§6.2
free floatThe shares actually available to trade, and therefore the denominator of your influence. Trap: your leverage is never your own stake — CHF 16m of a CHF 2,500m float is 0.64%. It is the coalition you can assemble behind one ask that decides an AGM.§6.4
Weighted-average carbon intensity
WACI = Σ ( weighti × carbon intensityi )
the sleeve: 112.81 tCO₂e / CHF m of revenue, of which Rhône Cement alone contributes 31.20 — 28% of the metric on 4% of the money
Excel: =SUMPRODUCT(weights, intensities)
Excluding a holding, and re-weighting
new WACI = ( old WACI − contribution ) ÷ ( 1 − weight excluded )
(112.81 − 31.20) ÷ 0.96 = 85.01, a 24.6% cut. Forgetting to divide by the remaining weight is the commonest error.
Engagement, by contrast
Rhône Cement 780 → 500 ⇒ WACI = 112.81 − 31.20 + (0.04 × 500) = 101.61
a 9.9% cut — less than half what divesting delivers, while being the only path that removes 280 tCO₂e/CHFm from the real world. This asymmetry is the whole of §6.5.
Your share of the register
stake % = holding ÷ free float
CHF 16m ÷ CHF 2,500m = 0.64%. To reach 15% of the register a coalition needs CHF 375m — about 23× Léman's own position.
Same words, different jobs

The pairs that cost marks

Every one of these has been confused by somebody in an exam. Learn them as pairs and the confusion disappears.

Taxonomy-eligible  vs  Taxonomy-aligned
Eligible = the Taxonomy has criteria for this activity, so it can be assessed. Aligned = it was assessed and passed all three tests. A company can be 90% eligible and 4% aligned, and frequently is.
Sustainability bond  vs  Sustainability-linked bond
Sustainability bond = use of proceeds, mixing green and social projects. Sustainability-linked bond = general-purpose proceeds, with a coupon linked to a target. One promises what the money buys; the other promises what the company becomes. The single most-confused pair in Week 5.
Green bond  vs  European green bond (EuGB)
A green bond is any bond an issuer labels green, normally against the ICMA Principles and a second-party opinion. An EuGB is a protected legal designation requiring ≥85% Taxonomy alignment and an ESMA-supervised reviewer. Every EuGB is a green bond; almost no green bond is an EuGB.
Financed emissions  vs  WACI
Financed emissions is a total in tonnes — it grows if you invest more and falls if you sell. WACI is an efficiency ratio — carbon per unit of revenue — comparable across portfolio sizes but silent about scale. They can move in opposite directions on the same portfolio.
Double materiality (ESRS)  vs  financial materiality (IFRS S2)
ESRS asks two questions: how does the company affect the world, and how does the world affect the company? IFRS S2 asks only the second, because its audience is investors. Same four pillars inherited from TCFD, narrower lens.
Impact  vs  dependency (TNFD)
Impact = what the business does to nature: land converted, water withdrawn, habitat fragmented. Dependency = what the business silently needs from nature: the aquifer, the pollinators, the navigable river. Dependencies are the genuinely new idea, and they are invisible until they fail.
Physical risk  vs  transition risk
Physical = the climate acting on you. Transition = the response acting on you. Note the inverse relationship: the better the world manages one, the larger the other becomes. A portfolio hedged against the transition is a bet on the physical scenario.
Article 8  vs  Article 9 (SFDR)
Article 8 promotes environmental or social characteristics. Article 9 has sustainable investment as its objective. Promotion is a marketing-and-disclosure test; an objective is a portfolio-construction test. The 2022–23 downgrade wave went 9 → 8 for exactly that reason.
SFDR  vs  CSRD
SFDR governs financial products and their managers — what a fund must tell you. CSRD governs companies — what an investee must publish. SFDR is downstream of CSRD: managers need company data to make their own disclosures, which is why the two were sequenced together.
Second-party opinion  vs  certification  vs  EuGB review
Three layers, each fixing the last one's weakness. SPO: an issuer-paid opinion that a document aligns with the Principles. Certification: independent verification that assets meet science-based criteria. EuGB review: an ESMA-registered, supervised reviewer confirming Taxonomy alignment, with fines for failure.
Greenwashing  vs  the additionality gap
Greenwashing is a claim without the substance behind it. The additionality gap is a claim that is entirely true and still changes nothing — a green bond refinancing a wind farm built in 2019. The second is far more common than the first, and it is not a rule breach. Naming which one you are looking at is the analyst's job.
exclusion vs divestment
Exclusion is a rule about what you will never buy — it shapes the universe before you invest. Divestment is the act of selling something you already hold, usually after an engagement failed. One is portfolio construction, the other is the last rung of the escalation ladder. Funds routinely describe the first and imply the second.
voting vs engagement
Both are "stewardship" and they behave completely differently. Voting is annual, public, binary and on the record — an outsider can audit it. Engagement is continuous, private and unverifiable from outside. That is why "we held 400 engagements" is a diary entry, while a voting record is evidence.
WACI vs financed emissions
WACI (Week 6) is an intensity — tCO₂e per CHF m of revenue — and does not change when the fund grows. Financed emissions (Week 4) are your share of absolute tonnes, and scale directly with how much you own. A fund can cut its WACI while doubling its financed emissions simply by getting bigger.
thematic vs impact
Thematic means the portfolio is built around an outcome — clean energy, water, health. Impact additionally claims your capital caused something that would not otherwise have happened. In listed secondary markets the first is easy and the second is very hard, which is why the words are used interchangeably by people who should know better.
