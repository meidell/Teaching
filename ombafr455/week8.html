Week 8 · 24–30 August · Chapter 8

Selling a tonne that never happened

Last week ended on a promise: an asset class whose entire product is a counterfactual. Here it is. A carbon credit is a claim that something did not happen — that a forest was not cleared, that a boiler was not built — and you are asked to pay for it, retire it, and let a client tell the world they are neutral. This week takes apart the two carbon markets, which are far less alike than the shared word suggests, then follows the same template into biodiversity credits and crowdfunding. By the end you will be able to price an offset properly: not per credit, but per tonne that is actually real. Plan 4–5 hours: ~two for this lesson, its videos and labs, then 2–3 for the homework.

By the end of Week 8 you will be able to

  • Explain why, in a capped market, buying an allowance changes who emits and not how much.
  • Describe how a voluntary credit is manufactured — baseline, additionality, permanence, leakage, MRV.
  • Name the four ways a credit fails, and diagnose which one a given project is exposed to.
  • Compute the effective cost per real tonne, and find the additionality share at which offsetting stops being cheap.
  • Place a claim on the mitigation hierarchy, and tell a compensation claim from a contribution claim.
  • Say why biodiversity credits cannot work exactly like carbon credits — and what the missing ingredient is.
  • Assess crowdfunding with the three-part test, and explain why it scores unusually well on contribution and badly on protection.

Section 8.1 · The Hook

1.85 million tonnes, at CHF 8 each

Your decision

A broker has an answer to Léman's carbon problem.

Rhône Cement — the holding that has followed you since Week 4 — emits about 1,850,000 tonnes of CO₂e a year. Léman's client wants to describe the sleeve as carbon neutral by the end of the year, and the fixed-income desk has been offered a portfolio of voluntary credits that would cover the lot.

The offerTerms
Volume1,850,000 credits, one tonne each
PriceCHF 8 per credit — total CHF 14.8m
TypeMostly avoided-deforestation and cookstove projects, issued under a recognised standard, third-party validated
For comparisonRhône Cement's own marginal abatement cost is about CHF 95 a tonne — kiln retrofit and alternative fuels

Everything about the paperwork is in order. The credits are real securities, registered and retirable. The question is what they represent.

Place your bet — then lock it

Of those 1,850,000 credits, what share do you think correspond to a tonne that would genuinely not have been emitted otherwise?

Pick your answer.
Locked. The last two answers are both defensible, and a serious analyst holds them together. Independent academic and journalistic assessments of major project types — avoided deforestation and cookstoves especially — have repeatedly found substantial over-crediting, sometimes concluding that only a small fraction of issued credits represented real, additional reductions. Those findings are contested by the standards bodies, methodologies have since been revised, and there is no audited market-wide figure. What is not contested is the direction: a credit is a claim about a counterfactual, and counterfactuals cannot be measured.

So do not guess the number — make it a variable. §8.4 gives you the arithmetic: at CHF 8 a credit, if 15% are genuinely additional you are paying CHF 53 per real tonne; below 8.4% you would do more for the atmosphere by simply handing Rhône Cement the money to fix its kilns. That break-even is computable, defensible, and it is the sentence to put in front of the investment committee.
The shape of the week

Two markets that share a word and almost nothing else.

§8.2 · Compliance market
The cap

A government sets a legal limit and issues allowances up to it. Trading decides who emits. The total is fixed by law, and no purchase changes it.

§8.3 – §8.4 · Voluntary market
The counterfactual

A project claims emissions were avoided against a baseline that never existed. There is no cap, no legal limit, and the product is an argument.

Both are called "carbon markets" and both trade something denominated in tonnes. Only one has a quantity that is actually enforced. Then §8.5 takes the same template into biodiversity, where the unit itself falls apart, and crowdfunding, where — unusually — the additionality is real and the investor protection is not.

A compliance allowance is a permit backed by law. A voluntary credit is a hypothesis backed by a methodology. Paying the same word for both is how a market ends up with an eight-fold price gap and no one able to explain it.

Section 8.2 · Compliance

The cap does the work

How a cap-and-trade system actually reduces emissions

Not by trading. By shrinking.

The EU ETS covers power, heavy industry and aviation across the EEA — roughly 40% of the bloc's greenhouse gas emissions. Each year the regulator issues allowances (EUAs) up to a cap, and every covered installation must surrender one allowance per tonne it emits. Emit more than you hold and the penalty is punitive.

Capset by law, falls annually
→
Allowancesauctioned or allocated free
→
Tradingdecides who emits
→
Total emissionsunchanged by any trade

This is the single most misunderstood point in carbon markets. If a Swiss utility buys an allowance, someone else now cannot use it. Aggregate emissions were set the moment the cap was, and the trade merely reallocates the right to emit toward whoever values it most — which is the efficiency argument for the whole design. The environmental outcome is a policy decision, not a market one.

MechanismWhat it doesWhy it exists
Linear reduction factorShrinks the cap by a fixed percentage each yearThe actual emissions policy. Everything else is plumbing.
Free allocationGives some allowances away rather than auctioning themProtects sectors exposed to competitors without a carbon price — and blunts the price signal for exactly those sectors.
Market Stability ReserveWithdraws allowances when the surplus is large, releases when tightThe 2008 crash left a surplus so big the price collapsed for years. The MSR stops a demand shock becoming a decade of cheap carbon.
CBAMCharges imports a carbon price at the EU borderAddresses carbon leakage — production moving to unpriced jurisdictions — and is what allows free allocation to be phased out.
Note the vocabulary trap. Carbon leakage here means production relocating to escape a carbon price — a policy problem. In §8.3 leakage means something different: a project displacing emissions next door rather than preventing them. Same word, two markets, two meanings, and both appear on the exam.

Section 8.3 · The Voluntary Market

Manufacturing a counterfactual

How a credit is made

Five claims, stacked, each of which can fail.

There is no cap here. A project developer builds a credit out of an argument, a standard validates the argument, and a buyer retires the result. Every step is a claim.

Baselinewhat would have happened
→
Additionalitythe project caused the difference
→
MRVmeasured, reported, verified
→
Issuancecredits into a registry
→
Retirementcancelled, claimed once

The baseline is the load-bearing element and it is unobservable. How fast would that forest have been cleared? How many households would have kept burning wood? The credit volume is the gap between reality and a scenario that by construction never occurred — so a developer with a financial interest in a large gap is being asked to estimate the thing that determines their revenue.

The four ways a credit fails

Almost every controversy fits one of these, and naming the pattern turns a suspicion into a question you can put to a seller. Compare the four failure modes for labelled bonds in Week 5 §5.5 — the structure is deliberately parallel.

  1. The baseline is inflated. The counterfactual assumes deforestation or fuel use far above what was really likely. Every tonne of exaggeration becomes a saleable credit. Ask: who set the baseline, on what evidence, and what happens to issuance if it is halved?
  2. The project was not additional. It was already economic, already legally required, or already funded. Ask: would this have gone ahead without credit revenue — and is there a financial test on the record?
  3. The reduction is not permanent. A forest can burn, be logged, or be cleared in ten years; the CO₂ returns and the credit was already retired. Ask: what is the buffer pool, who bears reversal risk, and for how long is the carbon guaranteed?
  4. The activity simply moved. Protect one concession and logging shifts to the next valley — leakage. Ask: over what boundary was leakage assessed, and was the deduction credible?
And a fifth, at the accounting level: double counting. If a host country also counts the reduction toward its own Paris target, the same tonne is claimed twice. The fix is a corresponding adjustment under Article 6 — the host adds the tonne back to its own books. Ask whether one applies; for a great many credits it does not.

None of this is fraud, and almost none of it breaches a standard. It is the same uncomfortable finding as Week 5: the market is largely honest about what it promises, and the promise is narrower than buyers assume.

Section 8.4 · The Arithmetic

Price per credit, or price per real tonne?

The gap that should bother you

Two prices for "a tonne", eight times apart.

A compliance allowance and a voluntary credit are both denominated in one tonne of CO₂e, and they trade at wildly different prices. That is not an arbitrage opportunity. It is a statement that they are different products.

EU allowance · compliance
tens of CHF

A legal permit to emit, backed by a cap and a punitive penalty. Its supply is fixed by statute. You cannot manufacture more.

Voluntary credit · avoidance
single-digit CHF

A claim that a tonne was avoided somewhere against a hypothetical baseline. Supply is limited only by how many projects can be developed.

Cheap supply is exactly what you would expect when the product is an argument rather than a permit. Which is why the price you should actually reason about is not the sticker price at all.

effective cost per real tonne  =  credit price ÷ share genuinely additional

Read the break-even carefully. It is the additionality share at which offsetting and abating cost the same per real tonne: share* = credit price ÷ abatement cost. Below it, the money does more good handed to the cement plant. It requires no view on whether credits are good or bad — only an explicit assumption about how many of them work, which is a far better argument to have in an investment committee than a values one.
The hierarchy, and the claim

Where compensation is allowed to sit.

Nothing above says credits are worthless. It says they belong in a specific place, and that place is last.

1
AvoidDo not create the emission. Redesign the process, drop the product, change the route.
2
ReduceEfficiency, electrification, materials. The cheapest real tonnes are almost always here.
3
SubstituteClean inputs — renewable power, alternative fuels, low-carbon clinker.
4
CompensateOnly for the residual you genuinely cannot yet abate — and preferably with removals rather than avoidance credits, since a removal takes carbon out rather than claiming it was never added.

The distinction that follows is the one your client will be judged on:

ClaimWhat it assertsStanding
Compensation
"carbon neutral"		Our emissions have been cancelled by creditsIncreasingly challenged by regulators and courts; several advertising authorities have ruled such claims misleading.
Contribution
"we funded X"		We reduced our own emissions by X and additionally financed climate action worth YDefensible, because both halves are separately verifiable and neither is cancelled against the other.
And note what the science-based target bodies do. Credits generally cannot be counted toward a company's own emissions-reduction target — reductions must happen inside the value chain. Offsets sit alongside the target, not inside it. A client who has bought credits to "hit their SBTi target" has misunderstood the instrument, and telling them so early is cheaper than telling them later.

Section 8.5 · Nature & Retail

The template, applied twice more

Biodiversity credits

The same design, missing the one thing that made it work.

Carbon markets function — to the extent they do — because of a property nobody notices until it is absent: a tonne of CO₂ is a tonne of CO₂ wherever it is. The atmosphere mixes. That fungibility is what allows a credit generated in Peru to offset an emission in Zurich.

Biodiversity has no such unit. A hectare of restored Amazon rainforest and a hectare of restored Swiss alpine meadow are not interchangeable — different species, different ecosystems, different irreplaceability. Nor can a species lost here be compensated by a species protected there. The market is therefore being built without its most important simplifying assumption.

ProblemIn carbonIn biodiversity
The unitOne tonne CO₂e — physically fungibleNo agreed unit. Proposals count hectares, condition scores, species-weighted area. None are interchangeable.
The baselineUnobservable counterfactualUnobservable counterfactual and contested measurement of the present state.
SubstitutionLocation broadly irrelevantLocation is nearly everything. Local loss is not repaired by distant gain.
Where it works bestGlobal, tradedLocal and compliance-driven — like England's biodiversity net gain regime, where development must deliver a measurable uplift, ideally on or near the site.

Biodiversity net gain is the most instructive live example precisely because it is not a global offset market: it is a planning requirement, with a defined metric, a preference for on-site delivery, and long maintenance obligations. That is a much narrower and much more defensible design than a freely traded biodiversity credit.

Crowdfunding

The one instrument this course has met where additionality is easy.

Retail capital, raised directly from many small investors through a platform, in four flavours: donation, reward, debt (peer-to-peer lending) and equity. For sustainable finance the last two matter.

Run it through Week 7's three-part test and something unusual happens:

TestHow crowdfunding scores
IntentionStrong. Investors self-select into a named, specific project — a community solar array, a local heat network. Nobody buys a crowdfunded solar bond by accident.
MeasurementMixed. Projects are small enough to measure precisely, and small enough that nobody funds the measurement.
ContributionGenuinely strong. This is primary capital going to projects that are typically too small for a bank or a fund. Very often the project does not happen without the raise — which is exactly what §7.4 said was hard to claim anywhere else.

And now the other side. The additionality that makes crowdfunding attractive comes from the same fact that makes it risky: these projects cannot get conventional finance. Illiquidity — often no secondary market at all. Concentration in a single asset. Failure rates typical of early-stage ventures. Disclosure far thinner than a prospectus, and platform due diligence that varies enormously. Regulation has tightened in the EU and Switzerland, but retail protection remains the weak point.

The honest summary for a client. Crowdfunding is one of the few places a small investor's money is unambiguously additional — and one of the few places they can lose all of it with no recourse and no exit. Both facts belong in the same sentence, and a platform that gives you only the first one has told you which of the two it is optimising for.

Take it outside Léman Sustainable Capital

One neutrality claim, taken apart.

Find a "carbon neutral" claim and audit it

An airline, a bank, a bottle of water, a delivery service — or your own employer, which is the most useful choice.

  1. Find the credits behind it. Which registry, which standard, which project type, which vintage? A claim that will not name its projects has answered the question already.
  2. Classify the project type against §8.3's four failure modes. Avoided deforestation and cookstoves carry baseline risk; renewable-energy credits in countries where renewables are already the cheapest option carry additionality risk; forestry carries permanence risk.
  3. Compute the effective price. Take the disclosed spend and volume, pick an additionality share you would defend in a meeting, and work out the cost per real tonne. Compare it with what abating in their own operations would cost.
  4. Place them on the hierarchy. Did they avoid, reduce and substitute first — with numbers — or did they go straight to compensation?

Three questions to answer in writing afterwards: Is this a compensation claim or a contribution claim, and which do the words actually make? What single disclosure would most improve it? And would you defend this claim to a regulator — if not, what would you tell the company to say instead?
