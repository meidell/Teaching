The running case · reference

The MekongPay data

Across eight sessions we say things about this data: three years of transactions, labels for only fourteen months, two analysts who disagreed, a class imbalance that decides the project. This page is the data itself — 25,034 real rows you can open, sort and count. Every problem we describe in the lectures is in here, and you can point at it.

Section 1

What you are looking at

MekongPay's warehouse holds roughly 180 million transactions. Nobody hands a project manager 180 million rows on day one — you get an extract, and you make decisions from it. This is that extract: 25,034 rows spanning 2023-08-01 to 2026-07-30, sampled across the full three years, with the real proportions preserved.

It comes as two files, because that is how it comes in reality. mekongpay-transactions.csv is the payment system's own record — every transaction, whether or not anyone ever looked at it. mekongpay-labels.csv is the fraud team's record: their verdicts, in their system, in their timezone, covering only the period they have worked through. Joining the two is the first real task of the project, and the join is where the problems start.

This is synthetic data built for teaching. The volumes, proportions and defects are set to match the MekongPay case exactly as described in the sessions — it is not real customer data, and no real e-wallet is represented here.

Section 3

The label file — and the row that should worry you

In Session 4 we call it risk #1: "confirmed-fraud labels exist for 14 months, not three years, and two analysts labelled them differently." Here is what that sentence looks like as data — one transaction, two verdicts:

txn_idlabelfraud_typelabelled_bylabelled_at_ictconfidence
TXN-2004664fraudaccount_takeoveranalyst_A2025-06-29 17:31:45low
TXN-2004664legitnullanalyst_B2025-07-03 18:31:45high

The same transaction. Analyst A called it account takeover with low confidence; four days later Analyst B called it legitimate with high confidence. Whichever row your training pipeline happens to pick up is what the model will learn as truth.

640 transactions were double-labelled as a quality check. The two analysts disagreed on 39 of them — 6.1%. That is your ceiling. No model can be more reliable than the labels it learns from, and this is how you put a number on it before you spend the GPU budget.

Section 4

Nine things wrong with this data

Each one is a claim we make in a lecture. Each one is now checkable. The tag says which session it bites in.

S4

The label cliff

Confirmed-fraud labels exist for 2025-06-01 → 2026-07-30 — 14 months of a three-year file. 47.1% of rows carry a label; the other 52.9% are unlabelled, not legitimate. Volume is not the same as usable.

S4

Two analysts, two standards

640 transactions were labelled twice as a QA check. The two analysts disagreed on 39 of them (6.1%), and the disagreements cluster on exactly the cases that matter: large amounts and small-hours activity. Your ground truth has a 6.1% error bar before you train anything.

S1

Almost no positives

23 confirmed frauds in 11,766 labelled transactions — 0.195%. A model that answers "legit" every single time is 99.805% accurate and worth nothing.

S2

merchant_cat is the messiest field you have

39 distinct strings for 10 real categories, plus 7.9% empty. S4 warns that if the model leans on merchant category and merchant category is the worst field you own, that is where the data-quality budget goes. Here is the evidence.

S1

Half the history has no device signal

device_id, device_os and is_new_device are empty for 45.7% of rows — capture only switched on 2025-03-01. "Add device-location data" is the mid-project scope change from Session 1, and this is what it actually costs: you cannot backfill the past.

S3

The timestamps are UTC. The analysts are not.

The transaction file is ts_utc; the label file is labelled_at_ict (UTC+7). Session 3's "suspicious 4 a.m. top-up versus normal night-shift behaviour" is a seven-hour mistake waiting to happen — a 4 a.m. Hanoi transaction is stamped 21:00 the previous day.

S2

Duplicate rows

34 transactions appear twice, byte for byte — payment retries that landed in the warehouse twice. 25,034 rows, 25,000 transactions.

S2

Negative and zero amounts

237 rows carry a negative amount (reversals) and 51 are zero (gateway artefacts). Sum amount_vnd without thinking and your fraud-loss baseline — the number the COO's 20% target is measured against — is wrong.

S4

A provincial skew you cannot explain

Merchants in Cần Thơ carry 4.4× the base fraud rate. That is 4 confirmed frauds in 989 transactions. Real pattern, or an artefact of who labelled what? You cannot tell from this file — and you are liable either way.
