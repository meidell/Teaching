Student showcase · Advanced Project Management in AI

Real AI projects, argued properly

On E1410, every participant brings an AI project from their own organisation and develops it across eight sessions — from the business problem and the state of the data, through budget, risk and governance, to how they would know it had stopped working. In the seventh session they defend it in front of a practitioner whose job is to find the weak joint. These are some of the results, published with their authors' permission.

The cases

Each one is a business case its author could take into their own organisation — and several of them did.

What they have in common

Read across a cohort's worth of these and the same three things separate the cases that survived their defence from the ones that did not.

  • The problem is stated without the technology in it. The strongest cases can be explained to someone who has never heard the phrase machine learning, and still sound urgent. The weakest start from a capability and go looking for somewhere to put it.
  • The data is described honestly, including what is wrong with it. Every one of these projects has a data problem — labels that disagree, history that only goes back three years, a field that changed meaning in 2022. Naming it is what makes the estimate believable.
  • There is a number attached to being wrong. Not just a budget: what it costs if the dominant assumption is off, and what cheap thing would test it first. That single move is the difference between a proposal and a plan.

How this was produced

The course. E1410 · Advanced Project Management in AI, on the IDEAS executive programme. Eight live sessions, each with an interactive lesson page, a workbook that feeds the participant's own project, and a self-check quiz. No modelling background is assumed.

The assignment. Each session's workbook fills one section of a written case study — context and problem, why AI and the data reality, the plan, resources and budget, risk, delivery and monitoring, responsible AI and governance, success criteria. Participants assemble it as they go rather than writing it at the end.

The defence. In Session 7 each project is presented for three minutes, challenged for four by a practitioner and a red team of peers holding specific roles — the CFO, the head of the affected team, the data protection officer, the sceptical engineer, the rival sponsor — and defended for two. The jury returns a verdict of fund, fund with conditions, or do not fund, and names the condition. It carries no grade.

Publication. Nothing appears here without its author's written consent. Where a project is commercially sensitive, the organisation is described by sector rather than named, and figures are rounded or indexed. Authors choose whether to be credited by name.
