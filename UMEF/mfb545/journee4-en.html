Section 4.1 · Block 1
🎤 Talk · 20 min

Why “trustless” works

The instructor’s talk. If it does not take place, read this section on your own, then do the sorting at the bottom of the page.

The problem

A ledger that nobody keeps alone

Your bank account is a line in the bank’s ledger. You believe the balance because you trust the bank, its auditor and FINMA. A blockchain replaces that third party with a ledger copied on thousands of computers, which anyone can reread from day one. Three questions must then be answered with no one in charge: who may spend? can the past be rewritten? in what order do transactions go through? Three ideas, one per question.

  • 1 · The signature — who can spend. Each account is a pair of keys. The private key signs; the public key (from which the address comes) lets everyone check the signature without ever knowing the private key. Without a valid signature, no computer on the network accepts the transaction. That is the sheet public key and private key.
  • 2 · The fingerprint — the past is not rewritten. Each block contains the fingerprint (the hash) of the previous block. Changing one comma in an old block changes its fingerprint, and therefore that of every block after it: the forgery shows immediately. That is the sheet what is a hash?.
  • 3 · Consensus — who writes the next block. Bitcoin draws lots by computation (proof of work, one block every ten minutes or so). Ethereum, since September 2022, by stake (proof of stake): validators each lock up 32 ETH; every 12 seconds one of them proposes a block and the others attest it. A validator who cheats loses part of its stake. After two “epochs” (about 13 minutes) the block is finalised: undoing it would cost at least a third of all the stake.

These three ideas are enough. The rest — smart contracts, DeFi, tokens — is code that runs on this ledger and inherits its guarantees, and its limits.

Section 4.3 · Block 2
🎤 Talk · 15 min

Lend, swap, stabilise — with no bank

The instructor’s talk, or read on your own.

Lending · Aave-type

A loan with no file: the collateral does everything

A lending protocol is a program (a smart contract) that holds a common pool. Nobody runs it day to day: lenders deposit tokens into it and earn interest; borrowers deposit collateral into it and borrow against that. The program knows no name, no income, no history: no score, no collection, only collateral worth more than the loan and a liquidation rule that applies by itself.

Below, two people who do not know each other, a lender and a borrower, use the same contract. At each step: who calls the contract, what the contract does, and the state of the pool. The figures are those of the tool in section 4.4 (LTV 80%, threshold 82.5%, bonus 5%).

Two users, one contract · step by step

The four rules the contract applies.

  • Maximum loan-to-value (LTV). At opening, you borrow at most 80% of the collateral’s value.
  • Liquidation threshold. If the debt exceeds 82.5% of the collateral’s value, the loan becomes liquidatable.
  • Health factor. Collateral value × threshold ÷ debt. Above 1, nothing happens; below 1, anyone can repay part of the debt in the borrower’s place and take some of their collateral with a 5% bonus. Automatic, public, no judge.
  • The rate. It follows the pool’s utilisation (the share lent out): the more of the pool is lent, the higher the rate, to attract deposits and slow borrowing. The interest borrowers pay goes to the depositors, less a reserve the protocol keeps.
Health factor = collateral × liquidation threshold ÷ debt

What it is used for, in practice.

  • Liquidity without selling. Moussa needs 3,000 USDC for six months but wants to keep his ETH: he posts it as collateral instead of selling it, and gets it back as it was. No sale, no realised gain, no file, about twenty seconds.
  • A return on idle cash. Awa places stablecoins she is not using; the rate is public, it moves every block, and she withdraws when she wants, as long as the pool is not fully lent out.
  • Leverage. Borrow USDC against ETH to buy more ETH, and deposit that too: the position rises faster, and gets liquidated faster.
  • What it is not. Credit for someone who has nothing: you must already hold more than you borrow. The rate is not fixed, the contract can have a bug, and a crash faster than the liquidators leaves bad debt that the protocol has to absorb.

Aave’s real parameters vary from one asset to another and from month to month; the ones on this page are orders of magnitude, chosen for the calculation.

The exchange · Uniswap-type

No order book: a formula

An automated market maker (AMM) is a pot holding two tokens, for example 100 ETH and 300,000 USDC. The program accepts any swap that leaves the product of the two quantities unchanged: x × y = k. The price is the ratio of the two reserves (300,000 ÷ 100 = 3,000 USDC per ETH). The more you buy at once, the more you empty the pot on one side and the higher the average price: that is price impact. Each swap pays a fee (often 0.3%) to the pot’s depositors. If the pot’s price drifts from the market’s, arbitrageurs bring it back by swapping — and earn the gap.

Received = y × Δx × (1 − fee) ÷ (x + Δx × (1 − fee))
Stablecoins

A token worth one dollar — provided that…

  • Backed by reserves. A company issues one token for each dollar received and places the dollars in cash and Treasury bills (Circle’s USDC, Tether’s USDT). The value holds as long as the reserves exist and can be reached. In March 2023, 3.3 billion dollars of USDC’s reserves were stuck at Silicon Valley Bank: USDC fell below 0.90 dollar for a weekend.
  • Backed by crypto-assets. DAI is created by borrowers who deposit more collateral than they issue, with automatic liquidation — Aave’s mechanism, applied to a currency.
  • Algorithmic. No reserves: the peg holds through a swap mechanism with a second token. TerraUSD (UST) worked this way; in May 2022 a wave of selling sent both tokens to almost zero, and tens of billions of dollars vanished within days.

Section 4.6 · Block 3
🎤 Talk · 20 min

Tokenisation and central bank digital currencies

The instructor’s talk, or read on your own.

Tokenising

A token is a ledger entry that represents a right

Tokenising a building, a bond or a fund unit means recording the ownership right in a shared ledger rather than in a custodian’s. What you gain: transfers settled in minutes instead of two days, smaller units, rules (who may buy, when the coupon is paid) written into the token. What you do not gain: the blockchain does not know that the building exists. The link between the token and the asset remains a contract, a company, a custodian and an applicable law — exactly as for a share.

Switzerland

A law, an exchange, a wholesale currency

  • The DLT Act (the federal act adapting federal law to developments in distributed ledger technology, in force in 2021) creates ledger-based securities (art. 973d CO): a security that exists only in an electronic register, transferable on that register with the same legal certainty as a paper security. It also creates a licence for a DLT trading facility and protects clients’ crypto-assets if the custodian goes bankrupt.
  • SDX (SIX Digital Exchange), the SIX group’s digital exchange, authorised by FINMA in 2021, issues and settles digital bonds.
  • Helvetia: the SNB makes a wholesale central bank digital currency available to banks on SDX’s platform, to settle tokenised bonds in central bank money. In pilot operation since December 2023, extended by the SNB until at least June 2028. A digital currency for the public (retail) is not planned by the SNB.
WAEMU

Studies, and no decision

  • A BCEAO digital currency? On 22 July 2026, presenting its annual report, the BCEAO said that a study phase is under way and that no deployment decision has been taken. The “eCFA” launched in Senegal in 2016 was not central bank money: it was electronic money issued by a commercial bank, on a start-up’s technology.
  • Crypto-assets. The BCEAO points out that they are not money and are not regulated in WAMU; a regulatory framework is being prepared. Neighbouring Nigeria launched a retail digital currency, the eNaira, in 2021: its adoption has remained very low.
Central bank digital currency: retail, wholesale, or not at all?

Section 4.8 · Block 4
🎤 Talk · 15 min

MEV, three hacks, MiCA

The instructor’s talk, or read on your own.

The “sandwich” attack

Your order is public before it is executed

A transaction waits in the public queue (the mempool) before it enters a block, and whoever builds the block chooses the order. A bot sees your purchase of 30,000 USDC of ETH on the pot from section 4.4. It buys just before you (the price rises), your purchase goes through at the higher price, and it sells just after. It gains exactly what you lose, within your slippage tolerance: with 1%, you agree to receive 1% less than expected, and that is what the bot takes. This gain that the order of transactions makes extractable is called MEV (maximal extractable value).

In this example the bot puts 1,586 USDC in front of you, you receive 8.9754 ETH instead of 9.0661, and it sells back with a 318 USDC gain, pot fees included. The simulator “The dark forest” ↗ shows it block by block.

Three hacks

The code, the keys, the screen

  • The DAO, June 2016 — the code. An investment fund run by a smart contract. A flaw allowed a withdrawal to be requested again before the contract had updated the balance (re-entrancy): about 3.6 million ETH diverted. Ethereum rewrote history through a split (hard fork); those who refused it carried on under the name Ethereum Classic. Lesson: “code is law” applies to bugs too.
  • Ronin, March 2022 — the keys. The bridge of the game Axie Infinity required 5 signatures out of 9 validators. The attackers obtained 5 keys and withdrew about 625 million dollars; the FBI attributed the attack to North Korea. Lesson: a secure chain does not protect badly kept keys.
  • Bybit, February 2025 — the screen. The platform signed its transfers from a multi-signature “cold” wallet. The signing interface had been hacked: the signers approved what they saw on screen, not what they were signing. About 1.5 billion dollars of ETH, the largest theft in crypto-asset history, also attributed to North Korea. Lesson: check what you sign, on a second device.
The code · the keys · the screen: where does each risk come from?
The framework

MiCA in the EU, FINMA in Switzerland, the BCEAO

  • European Union · MiCA (Regulation 2023/1114). Since 30 June 2024 for stablecoins, since 30 December 2024 for the rest: an issuer of a stablecoin backed by a single currency (an e-money token) must be a credit or e-money institution, with segregated reserves and a right to redemption at par; a provider (platform, custodian, adviser) needs an authorisation, valid across the Union. The national transitional periods ended on 1 July 2026 at the latest.
  • Switzerland. No MiCA: FINMA applies existing law according to the token’s function (payment, investment, utility), the DLT Act, and the AMLA to every provider that transfers crypto-assets on behalf of others.
  • WAEMU. No framework specific to crypto-assets so far (see section 4.6).
Section 4.9 · Block 4
🛠 Project and game · 75 min

Prove the money is there · DeFi Millionaire

A · 15 min · the proof of inclusion

Each customer checks that their units are counted, without seeing anyone else’s

After FTX’s bankruptcy (November 2022), platforms began publishing a “proof of reserves”. Its “liabilities” half is a Merkle tree: the fingerprint of each account (identifier, balance, and a secret salt that prevents guessing the others’ balances) is combined two by two up to a single fingerprint, the root, published on the same day for everyone. Each customer receives their path — three fingerprints here — and recomputes the root themselves. If their balance had been left out or reduced, the root would not match. Here are eight customers of the app, in fund units.

The Merkle tree of the eight customers · SHA-256 computed in your browser

The root proves that your balance is counted. It does not prove that the units exist: that is the other half — an attestation from the custodian and the auditor that the assets held cover the sum of the balances. A blockchain can serve to publish the root with a timestamp and unalterably; it replaces neither the custodian nor the auditor.
