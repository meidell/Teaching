Fintech, or the unbundled bank

A universal bank sells six services in a single bundle: account, payment, credit, savings, investment and compliance. Fintechs, bigtechs and decentralised protocols take them over one by one. This day takes the bundle apart: your own account openings examined step by step, the unbundled value chain, a radar of a dozen Swiss, European and African players, the numbers of Wave and TWINT redone by hand — then the launch of your project.

Section 1.1 · Block 1
🎤 Talk · 25 min

The unbundled bank

Talk by the instructor. If it does not take place, read this section on your own, then do the classification at the bottom of the page.

What a bank does

Six building blocks, sold in a single bundle — and what each one earns

Block 1
Account
Holding the money, keeping the ledger. Earns little in fees; gives access to cheap deposits.
Block 2
Payment
Moving money around: card, transfer, foreign exchange. Very profitable (fees, FX margin), almost without capital.
Block 3
Credit
Lending out the deposits: maturity transformation. Earns the interest margin; requires capital and a licence.
Block 4
Savings
Savings accounts, retirement provision (the 3rd pillar in Switzerland). Earns the difference between what the bank pays and what it invests.
Block 5
Investment
Advice, wealth management, brokerage. Earns management fees (as a % of assets) and brokerage fees.
Block 6
Compliance
Identifying the customer (KYC), monitoring flows (AMLA), reporting. A cost — which regtechs now sell as a service.

The universal bank cross-subsidises one block with another: the “free” account is paid for by payment fees, the FX margin and the interest margin. A new entrant does not need to do everything: it takes the block that is most profitable for the capital it requires — historically payments and foreign exchange — and does it better, cheaper, on a phone.

Why the bundle is coming apart now

Three mechanisms

  • The cost of distribution collapses. A branch costs rent, tellers and opening hours; an app, a server. Remote account opening (below) removed the last mandatory visit to a branch.
  • The blocks can be bought. A start-up rents a banking licence and an account from a partner bank (banking-as-a-service), identification from a regtech, card processing from a processor, hosting from a cloud — each block through an API (Day 2). It only writes the customer journey.
  • Regulation becomes proportionate. In Switzerland, the fintech licence (art. 1b of the Banking Act, 2019) allows a firm to accept deposits from the public up to 100 million francs, provided it neither lends them out nor pays interest — with lighter requirements and no deposit insurance. In the EU, the payment institution (PSD2) and electronic money statuses; in the WAEMU, the BCEAO’s electronic money issuer licence.
Who takes over the blocks

Three kinds of new entrants — and the bank that remains

  • Specialised fintechs: one block, one journey, one app (Wise for foreign exchange, Wave for mobile transfers, Klarna for buy-now-pay-later).
  • Bigtechs: Apple, Google, Alibaba, Tencent. Their advantage is the loop the Bank for International Settlements calls DNA — Data (the platform’s data), Network (hundreds of millions of users already there), Activities (the financial service is grafted onto commerce, messaging, the phone). Each turn of the loop enriches the data, which improves the service, which attracts users (Frost et al., 2019).
  • Decentralised protocols: lending, exchange, stablecoins run by programs on a blockchain, with no company in the middle (Day 4).
  • And the bank: it keeps what requires a balance sheet — credit, large deposits — and often becomes the invisible supplier of fintechs. The opposite movement also exists: super-apps (Alipay, M-Pesa) re-bundle everything into a single app (Day 6).
The mechanism · opening an account without a branch

Remote identification (eKYC), step by step

In 1.2, you will compare your own account openings with these seven steps. In Switzerland, the framework is FINMA Circular 2016/7 “Video and online identification”. Each step answers a question: is the document genuine, is the person alive, is it the same person, are we allowed to accept them?

The seven steps of an onboarding

The ten-minute account is no less checked than the branch one: the checking has been automated, and each step has an error rate that can be tuned.

Six segments, and how to document a player

Talk by the instructor, or self-study.

The radar’s segments

The market cut into six — with, for each, the mechanism that makes money

Segment 1
Payments
Fee per transaction, FX margin. TWINT, Wise, Stripe, Wave, Flutterwave.
Segment 2
Credit
Interest margin or merchant fee (buy-now-pay-later). Klarna, swisspeers, Baobab.
Segment 3
Savings and wealth management
Management fees as a % of assets, brokerage fees. VIAC, True Wealth, Yuh, Trade Republic.
Segment 4
Insurtech
Premiums, distribution commissions; often a traditional insurer’s policy sold differently. Lemonade, Wefox, Turaco.
Segment 5
Regtech
Subscription or price per check, sold to institutions. PXL Vision, IDnow, Smile ID.
Segment 6
Infrastructure and BaaS
Price per account or per API call, sold to other fintechs. Solaris, Swan, Onafriq, Paystack.

The names are starting points, not a closed list: a player you know from your own country is worth more than a name from this page.

The standard profile

Five questions, and where to find each answer

  • Problem solved — in one sentence, from the customer’s point of view, without the name of the technology. Source: the home page, then customer reviews (app stores).
  • Customer — who pays, and who uses (not always the same: at TWINT the user pays nothing, the merchant pays). Source: the price list.
  • Revenue — the exact mechanism: “1% on transfers”, “0.5% of assets per year”, “CHF 9 per month”. Source: the pricing terms, the annual report if published.
  • Funding — funds raised, investors, profitable or not. Source: press releases, specialised press (Finextra, TechCabal, Fintech News Switzerland), Crunchbase.
  • Regulator — who authorises it, under which status. Source: the regulator’s register (FINMA: list of authorised institutions; BCEAO: list of electronic money issuers; EU: national registers and the EBA register), and the website’s legal notice.

A profile is judged on its revenue mechanism. “Makes money from fees” is not an answer; “charges 1% on every transfer, deposits and withdrawals free” is one.

How a fintech charges

Five revenue mechanisms

  • Commission or fee per transaction — a percentage or a fixed amount on each operation (merchant fee, transfer fee).
  • Interchange — the share of each card payment received by the bank that issued the card. This is how neobanks make money on a “free” card (Day 2).
  • Margin — on foreign exchange (converting at a worse rate than the mid-market rate) or on interest (lending at a higher rate than one’s own funding cost).
  • Subscription — a monthly price for a premium offer.
  • Management fees — an annual percentage of assets under management.
Network effects

Why the first to reach critical mass often wins everything

Direct effect — each new user makes the service more useful to the others. Among n users, the number of pairs that can send each other money is n(n − 1) / 2: 45 pairs for 10 users, 4,950 for 100, 499,500 for 1,000. The value grows much faster than the number of users.

Indirect, or two-sided, effect — two groups that attract each other: the more customers pay with TWINT, the more merchants accept it, the more customers adopt it. The starting problem is the chicken-and-egg problem; the usual solution is to subsidise one side — free P2P at TWINT, free withdrawals at Wave — and charge the other.

Unit economics

Four formulas for Block 3

ARPU = revenue for the month ÷ active users for the month
Contribution margin per user = ARPU − variable costs per user
Break-even (in users) = fixed costs for the month ÷ contribution margin per user
Take rate = revenue ÷ volume that flows through

Variable costs follow each user (agent commissions, processing, SMS, fraud); fixed costs are there even with no users (salaries, technology, compliance, head office). A fintech loses money as long as it is below its break-even point, and each user above it brings in their contribution margin.

The “Build your fintech” project

Talk by the instructor, or self-study.

40% of the grade · replaces the midterm exam

Two teams of three, one project: a pension-savings app for Senegal, Congo and their neighbours.

The app lets people who have no pension, or a very small one, save for their old age by paying small amounts every month until 65, invested in Swiss blue-chip equity funds, among the most stable on the market. The app and the company must be transparent and earn trust: that is the condition for anyone to hand over their money for thirty years. Each team picks its country (Senegal, Congo, DRC, Côte d’Ivoire…) and its customer. Each day adds a piece to the project, at the end of the afternoon, using what has just been learned. A good starting point

A problem, not a solution

  • A specific customer: “self-employed seamstresses in Dakar”, not “Africans”.
  • What they do today: the current workaround (cash, tontine, a transfer through a cousin) — that is your real competitor.
  • What it costs them: in money, in time, in risk. A figure, even a rough one.
  • Why now: what has changed (a phone in every pocket, instant payments, a new regulation).

The word “blockchain” or “AI” does not appear in a problem statement. The technology is chosen on Day 4, once the problem is understood.
