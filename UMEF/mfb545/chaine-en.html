Round 0 · 5 min · read

You are a node

Your bank account is one line in the bank’s database. One copy, one keeper; you believe your balance because you trust the keeper. A blockchain replaces that single keeper with identical copies held by strangers who do not trust each other — and with rules that every copy checks for itself.

In this room: your browser keeps its own copy of the ledger, checks every block and every payment it receives, and refuses what is false. The site is only the postman: it carries messages between the nodes, it decides nothing. The last round proves it: the instructor will tamper with the postman’s copy, and every node will refuse it.

The words. Distributed database: N copies, none of them “the real one”. Hash (fingerprint): the digital fingerprint of a content, which changes completely if one letter changes. Private key / public key: signing without revealing your secret. Mining: the lottery by computation that picks who writes the next block. Consensus: the rule everyone applies to choose the same chain. Trustless: you need to believe nobody, only to verify.

B · sign, verify

Sign a message, then try to forge it

Signing means running the message’s hash through the private key. Verifying means running the signature through the public key: anyone can do it, nobody can produce the signature without the private key.

Change one letter of the message (“10” → “100”) without signing again, then verify. Then verify with another node’s public key.

Round 2 · 10 min · hash and block

A block, its hash, and why the past is not rewritten

A block is a text: its number, the previous block’s hash, the miner’s address, the time, the list of payments, and a freely chosen number, the nonce. Its hash is SHA-256 of that text. Change anything below and watch the hash.

Why it holds. Block 2 contains the hash of block 1. Changing a payment in block 1 changes its hash; block 2 then points to a block that no longer exists; and so on to the last one. Forging the past means redoing every later block — and round 3 shows what “redoing a block” costs.
B · mine by hand

Find a nonce such that the hash starts with 0

The mining rule: the block’s hash must start with a certain number of zeros. The only way is to try nonces until you hit one — a one-in-16 chance per try for one zero. Change the nonce above, and count your tries.

Round 3 · 10 min · everyone mines

Who writes the next block? Whoever finds the nonce first

Every node assembles the same next block (chain head + waiting payments), with its own address as miner, then searches for a nonce. The first to find one publishes its block; all the others verify it, add it, and throw their work away to start on the block after. The winning miner receives — UMF: it is the only way to get coins.

How someone wins. At random, in proportion to computing speed: a fast laptop makes more tries per second than a phone. Look at the “blocks won” column on the room screen. To win alone, you would have to compute faster than all the others together — the “majority” the 51% attack is about.
Round 4 · 10 min · pay

Send coins to someone in the room

A payment is a signed message: “from my address to their address, amount”. It goes into every node’s queue (the mempool); it only really exists once a miner has put it in a block. Every node checks the signature and the balance; a payment without balance is simply ignored.

Round 6 · 15 min · cheat

A · Rewrite the past in my copy

Pick an already confirmed payment and change its amount in your copy. Nothing is sent: you edit your own ledger, like a banker correcting the books at night.

Why it fails. The altered block’s hash no longer matches; every later block points to a hash that no longer exists; your node announces a head nobody else has, and the others carry on without you. To impose your version you would have to re-mine every block since the change, faster than the rest of the room adds new ones.

B · Spend the same balance twice

Send your whole balance to two different people, one right after the other. Both payments enter the queue with a valid signature. Watch which one gets into a block, and what the nodes do with the other.

and
Why it fails. The miner takes the payments in order and recomputes the balance after each one; the second exceeds the balance and is dropped. A node that accepted it would build a block every other node refuses. Without a blockchain, the bank prevents the double spend; here, everyone does.

C · Lend your private key

Show your private key (round 1) and dictate it to a neighbour. They paste it below: they can now spend your coins, and nothing tells their payments from yours.

The word. “Not your keys, not your coins.” The three hacks of Day 4 — The DAO, Ronin, Bybit — break neither the hash nor the signature: they steal the key, or fool whoever holds it.

D · The instructor tampers with the central ledger

The site that carries the messages also keeps a copy of the blocks. The instructor, with the administrator password, changes an amount in that copy. If the blockchain were “the site’s database”, the amount would change for everyone. Watch your node.

Trustless. Your node verified this block when it arrived and keeps its copy. The altered version hashes to something other than its key: the node refuses it and keeps its own. Nobody needed to trust the site — or the instructor.
Round 7 · 10 min · for the project

And the pension fund units?

Your app promises a customer in Dakar or Brazzaville that their payments have become units of a Swiss fund. What you just built guarantees three things and does not guarantee a fourth:

  • A signed entry cannot be forged on the customer’s behalf (round 1).
  • A past entry cannot be rewritten without everyone seeing it (rounds 2 and 6 A).
  • No single keeper — neither your company nor the site — can alter the ledger (round 6 D).
  • But nothing proves the units exist at the custodian: the chain guarantees the entry, not the real world. A custodian’s attestation is still needed — that is section 4.9.

Answer in section 4.1 B of Day 4: which entry of your app would deserve this ledger, which one does not need it, and who the nodes would be.
