Section 3.1 · Block 1
🎤 Talk · 20 min

How a credit score works

Instructor’s talk. If it does not take place, read this section on your own, then do the sorting exercise at the bottom of the page.

The lender’s question

How much will this loan cost me, on average?

Expected loss (EL) = PD × LGD × EAD
  • PD — probability of default: the probability that the borrower stops paying (usually: more than 90 days late) over the life of the loan or over one year. This is what the score estimates.
  • LGD — loss given default: the share of the amount the lender does not get back, after collection. For unsecured consumer credit, often 40 to 70%.
  • EAD — exposure at default: the amount owed at the moment of default.

A CHF 5,000 loan, PD 12%, LGD 50%: expected loss 0.12 × 0.50 × 5,000 = CHF 300. The price of credit must cover this loss, the cost of money, servicing costs and the capital tied up. This is risk-based pricing: two customers of the same product can pay two different rates.

The mechanism · from data to decision

Seven steps between the application and the app’s “yes”

An application for a CHF 3,000 loan over 24 months
Measuring a score

AUC and Gini: does the score rank the good before the bad?

A score does not say who will default; it ranks. It is judged on loans it has never seen. The AUC is the probability that a randomly drawn bad payer gets a higher PD than a randomly drawn good payer: 0.5 = a coin toss, 1 = perfect ranking. Banks rather report the Gini = 2 × AUC − 1. A consumer-credit score typically has a Gini between 0.5 and 0.7; above 0.9 on test data, suspect a mistake (a variable that “knows” the answer).

The threshold. The model gives a PD; a person sets the threshold above which applications are refused. If a repaid loan earns a margin m (as a % of the amount) and a default costs the LGD, a loan is worth making as long as (1 − PD) × m > PD × LGD, that is:

Theoretical threshold: PD < m ÷ (m + LGD)

With a 15% margin and a 50% LGD: 0.15 ÷ 0.65 = 23.1%. Accepting more widely raises volume and defaults; accepting more tightly turns away good customers. The simulator in the next section shows the trade-off on real loans.

The framework

What the law requires of a score, in Switzerland, the EU and WAEMU

  • Switzerland. The Consumer Credit Act (KKG/LCC) requires a creditworthiness assessment and a check with the Consumer Credit Information Centre (IKO); the ZEK is the lenders’ private credit register. Maximum annual effective rate in 2026: 10% for a cash loan, 12% for an overdraft or a credit card.
  • European Union. The new Consumer Credit Directive (2023/2225, applicable from 20 November 2026) requires a creditworthiness assessment based on relevant data, forbids using sensitive data (health, origin…) and social-media data in it, and gives a customer refused by an automated decision the right to an explanation and to human intervention. The AI Act classes credit scoring of individuals as a high-risk AI system. And the Court of Justice ruled in 2023 (SCHUFA, C-634/21) that the score itself can be an automated decision under the GDPR when the lender relies on it decisively.
  • WAEMU. The usury rate set by the WAMU Council of Ministers caps the cost of credit: 14% a year for banks since 1 June 2026 (15% before), 24% for microfinance institutions and financial institutions. Credit information bureaus (BIC) licensed by the BCEAO share repayment histories.

Section 3.3 · Block 2
🎤 Talk · 15 min

The economics of a neobank

Instructor’s talk, or self-study reading.

Where the money comes from

Five revenue lines, all small per customer

  • Interchange (Day 2): the neobank is the issuer; it earns a share of every card payment. In the EU, capped at 0.2% on debit: CHF 800 of monthly spending brings in CHF 1.60.
  • FX: a margin on the rate, often zero on weekdays up to a cap, then marked up (at weekends, above the limit, on illiquid currencies).
  • Subscriptions: a few percent of customers pay for a plan (travel insurance, more free FX, a metal card).
  • Interest margin: deposits placed with the central bank or in government securities earn interest; the neobank passes part of it on. With rates at zero (Switzerland 2015–2022), this line disappears.
  • Credit and investing: overdrafts, loans, buy now pay later, brokerage. The most profitable lines — and the ones that need a banking licence and a score.
The mechanism · unit economics

What a customer is worth, and what they cost to acquire

ARPU = average revenue per active customer per month
Customer lifetime value (LTV) = ARPU × contribution margin ÷ monthly churn
Payback period = CAC ÷ (ARPU × contribution margin)

The contribution margin is what remains of ARPU after variable costs (card processing, support, fraud, compliance per customer). Churn is the share of customers who leave — or go inactive — each month: at 2% a month, a customer stays 1 ÷ 0.02 = 50 months on average. CAC is the cost of acquiring a customer (advertising, referral bonuses, onboarding and identity-verification costs). The industry rule: LTV ≥ 3 × CAC, and CAC recovered in under 12 to 18 months.

Four cases

Revolut, N26, neon, Yuh: why some lose money with millions of customers

  • Revolut (London, 2015): 52.5 million customers at the end of 2024, revenue £3.1 billion, profit before tax £1.1 billion — a fourth profitable year. The engine: many products per customer (FX, subscriptions, credit, trading, interest) and global scale on a single platform.
  • N26 (Berlin, 2013): 4.2 million “revenue-relevant” customers at the end of 2023, revenue €313.5 million, net loss €102.4 million. Growth held back from 2021 to 2024 by BaFin, which had capped new account openings over anti-money-laundering weaknesses.
  • neon (Zurich, 2019): about 250,000 customers at the end of 2025; no banking licence of its own (accounts are held by Hypothekarbank Lenzburg), a low-fee, subscription model.
  • Yuh (2021, Swissquote and PostFinance; 100% Swissquote since July 2025): profitable at the end of 2024, just under 400,000 accounts at the end of 2025. Revenue comes largely from investing and the interest margin rather than from cards.

Sources: the companies’ annual reports and press releases (Revolut, Annual Report 2024; N26, press release of 13 Nov. 2024; Swissquote, Annual Report 2025; PostFinance, “Yuh 2024” release). The figures change every year: the workshop asks you to look up the latest published ones.

Crowdfunding, peer-to-peer lending, buy now pay later

Instructor’s talk, or self-study reading.

Four forms of crowdfunding

What the person who puts in money gets back

  • Donation: nothing in return (a school to rebuild, a cause).
  • Reward: a product or a perk, often the first batch — it is a pre-sale (Kickstarter, in Switzerland wemakeit). An “all or nothing” campaign: if the target is not reached, nobody is charged.
  • Lending (crowdlending): individuals lend to an SME or to another individual, with interest.
  • Equity (equity crowdfunding): backers become shareholders; they hope for a capital gain on resale, in five to ten years, or nothing if the company fails.

The framework. In the EU, Regulation 2020/1503 (ECSP) gives lending and equity platforms an EU-wide licence, for offers of up to €5 million per project over twelve months, with a standard key information sheet and a knowledge test for non-sophisticated investors. In Switzerland, professionally accepting funds from more than 20 people normally requires a banking licence; platforms use the exceptions in the Banking Ordinance (funds on a settlement account passed on within 60 days) or the fintech licence (Day 5).

The mechanism · a peer-to-peer loan

Six steps, from application to repayment

An SME borrows CHF 300,000 from 600 individuals
Expected return ≈ (1 − PD) × rate − PD × LGD − platform fees

The advertised rate is not the return. A loan at 18% with an 18% PD and a 70% LGD yields, after 1% fees, about 1.2% — less than a loan at 9%. And one default in a portfolio of two loans is a disaster; in two hundred, a statistic.

Split payment · Buy Now, Pay Later

“Pay in 4, interest-free”: who pays, and is it credit?

  • The mechanism. At checkout, the provider (Klarna, Afterpay, PayPal Pay in 4, in Switzerland also Powerpay or invoices payable in 30 days) runs a check in a few seconds, pays the merchant at once, and is repaid by the customer in several instalments, often every two weeks.
  • Who pays. The merchant: a fee of a few percent, higher than a card, which it accepts because split payment raises basket size and conversion. The customer pays nothing if they pay on time — and late fees otherwise.
  • The status. In the EU, the old directive excluded interest-free credit repayable within three months; the new one (2023/2225) brings it in from 20 November 2026, with a creditworthiness assessment. In the UK, the FCA has regulated it since 15 July 2026. In Switzerland, the KKG/LCC excludes credit repayable within three months at most; the old “four instalments within twelve months” exception disappeared with the 2016 revision.

Section 3.8 · Block 4
🎤 Talk · 15 min

Digital credit in Africa: effects and abuses

Instructor’s talk, or self-study reading.

The founding case · M-Shwari, Kenya, 2012

A savings account and a loan in the M-Pesa menu

  • The product. Launched by Commercial Bank of Africa and Safaricom: a savings account and a 30-day loan, from 100 shillings, opened from the M-Pesa menu, with no branch and no paper. The price: a facilitation fee of 7.5% of the amount, not an interest rate.
  • The score. No credit file for most customers: the credit limit is computed from the M-Pesa history (tenure, deposits, transfers, savings on M-Shwari). The first limit is small; it grows with on-time repayments.
  • The measured effect. Bharadwaj, Jack and Suri (Journal of Development Economics, 2021) compare customers just above and just below the eligibility threshold: a third of those eligible borrow; households with access to the loan are 6.3 points less likely to forgo essential spending after a shock (illness, crop loss). But neither savings nor productive assets rise: digital credit helps absorb a blow, it does not lift people out of poverty.
In West Africa

Credit and savings through the mobile wallet

  • Baobab (a microfinance group, formerly MicroCred) launched M-Baobab in Senegal in 2022 with Orange Finances Mobiles: fully digital credit and savings, from the Orange Money app or the USSD code #144#. Baobab+ built a score from the payment history of solar kits paid for by mobile money.
  • The WAEMU framework. An e-money institution cannot lend (Day 2): the credit is extended by a partner bank or microfinance institution, under the usury rate (14% for banks, 24% for microfinance institutions). That constraint is what sets the West African market apart from Kenya.
The abuses

What went wrong, and what regulators did

  • The hidden price. A “per loan” fee over 30 days looks modest; annualised, it often exceeds 90%.
  • Over-indebtedness and the blacklist. In Kenya, borrowers were reported en masse to credit bureaus for defaults of a few hundred shillings, which then shut them out of bank credit.
  • Debt shaming. Lending apps read the phone’s contact list and wrote to the late borrower’s contacts.
  • The response. Kenya put digital lenders under central-bank supervision (Central Bank Act amended in 2021, Digital Credit Providers Regulations of March 2022): licence required, access to the contact list banned, threats and publication of data banned, no reporting to the credit bureau below 1,000 shillings. In 2025 the Kenyan press still reported harassment by licensed lenders.
  • The opaque score. A refused customer does not know why, and so can neither challenge it nor improve.
