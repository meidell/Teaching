    What a recruiter’s software really does
    
Many companies receive applications in an applicant tracking system (ATS: Workday, SAP SuccessFactors, Greenhouse…). It does three things. It extracts the text of the CV, section by section — a two-column CV, with icons or text inside an image, may arrive scrambled or empty. It files that text into boxes (experience, education, skills). And it lets the recruiter search and filter by words: “Canva”, “Google Sheets”, “Wolof”. Your measurement above does the same thing, more simply: it looks for each requirement’s words in your CV.

    
Two consequences. Write the skill with the offer’s word (“Google Sheets”, not just “spreadsheet”). And keep a simple layout: one column, ordinary section headings, no text in images. You often read that “75% of CVs are rejected by robots”: that figure circulates with no known study behind it. What is certain is that a recruiter searching for “Canva” will not find a CV that does not contain the word.

Why these checks? A recruiter reads a message in a few dozen seconds. “Passionate, dynamic, detail-oriented” appears in almost every AI-generated message — and proves nothing. What sticks is a requirement of the offer followed by evidence: “You want someone at ease with Google Sheets: I kept the family shop’s sales file for three months.”

    Your notebook · the follow-up table
    

      
1.One line per application: date sent · company · role · to whom · reminder date · answer.

      
    

    
Why a table? After ten applications nobody remembers who replied, or when to send a reminder. The simplest rule: one reminder, polite and short, seven days after sending, in the same thread (“I am following up on my application of 10 November…”). No second reminder the same week. And one e-mail per company: never ten companies in the same mailing, nor in copy — each would see the others, and the message would look like advertising.
