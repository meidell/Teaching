What we almost always observe
  • The text is always well written, whatever the quality of the content. Form says nothing about substance: that is the first thing to unlearn.
  • Two systems give two answers to the same question, and the same system answers differently if you ask again. Not a bug: the answer is drawn at random among likely continuations.
  • The images are beautiful and wonky: six-fingered hands, unreadable text in the picture. The model learned what an image “looks like”, not what it represents.
  • It never says “I didn’t understand”. The transcription invents words where it cannot hear; the summary smooths over what it did not grasp.

Step 1. In an assistant, type as you would have yesterday: “write me an email asking for an extension”. Read the answer. Step 2. Fill in the five boxes below — the structure that comes back every session — copy the assembled prompt and send it to the same assistant. Step 3. Your verdict.

The five-part prompt · fill in, copy, test
Your assembled prompt
—
Compared with the question tossed in, the answer is…

An AI does not read your mind: it completes what you wrote. What you do not say, it makes up. The five parts are there so that nothing is left to invention.

Three more reflexes — try them now
  • Ask it to ask questions first: “Before answering, ask me the three questions you need.” It does this well, and you discover what you forgot to say.
  • Critique, do not start over: “Too long, too formal, keep the second paragraph.” The second answer is almost always the right one.
  • Give an example of what you like (an email you received, a text you find good). One example is worth ten adjectives.

Section 1.4 · Ten minutes of vocabulary

Three words, and one difference that explains everything

The three circles

AI contains machine learning, which contains deep learning. Click each circle.

Click a circleThree definitions, from the widest to the narrowest.
The real boundary

Rules written by a human, or rules learned from examples?

The spam filter, version 1: a programmer writes if the message contains “free” and a link, then junk — and spammers write “fr33”. Version 2: we show ten thousand e-mails already labelled junk / normal, and the program finds by itself what sets them apart. That is machine learning, and it is behind everything you have used since 13:30.

Classical program: rules + data → answers  ·  Learning: data + answers → rules
Written rules · or · learned from examples?

A learned rule is only as good as the examples were. That is the key to every failure you will see in this course.

The four words for what you just saw
  • Training data — the images you showed, each with its right answer. It is everything the model knows about the world.
  • Generalising — recognising a mug never seen. That is the goal; without it a model is only a memory.
  • Data bias — examples that do not represent the reality where the model will be used: every mug on a white background. The learned rule reproduces the imbalance.
  • Out of domain — what the model has never seen. It picks the nearest class, with a confidence that means nothing — exactly like the assistant in section 1.3.
“The model did not learn what you wanted to teach it. It learned whatever, in your examples, let it give the right answer.” — the sentence to remember from this session.

Hand-in 1, concretely

Half a page, before 20 October, on the Swiss UMEF platform.

  • Who — you, or the two of you (names and programmes).
  • The problem, in one sentence, without the word “AI”: who does what today, by hand, how often.
  • What it would change if a machine did it — for whom, measured how.

Leads, to get started: sorting a shop’s customer reviews and flagging the urgent ones · answering frequent questions about the study regulations · preparing a sector’s weekly press review · spotting a small company’s abnormal invoices · generating a club’s campaign visuals and texts · an assistant that prepares your revision cards from your notes · an app that computes a quote. A good project is small, real and verifiable.
