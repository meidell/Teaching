The browser’s speech synthesis · the point of comparison

This voice stitches recorded sounds together, piece by piece: it does not know what it reads. Today’s voices (ElevenLabs, Google’s or OpenAI’s) are neural networks trained on thousands of hours of speech: they predict the next sound, as the text model predicts the next word — with the same consequences. A number, an acronym, a foreign name: it predicts the most probable pronunciation, which is not necessarily yours.

Try it here · 1 · what the machine hears

Speak. The scrolling picture is the spectrogram: time left to right, low sounds at the bottom, high ones at the top.

Live spectrogram · your microphone
Say a sentence, then whistle: the whistle is a thin line, at the top.

A transcription model (OpenAI’s Whisper, Google’s inside Gemini) does not receive words: it receives this picture, a hundred columns a second, and predicts the most probable text tokens for each piece of picture — as the text model predicts the next word. Hence its errors: a name it has never seen written becomes the common word that looks most like it; a word of Wolof becomes French syllables; the noise of the street becomes words that do not exist. And hence the remedy: a microphone near the mouth, a quiet room, and the proper names spelled once in the follow-up.

Try it here · 2 · counting the errors

The counter. One click per wrong word, in the right column. The error rate computes.

Transcription error rate

Professionals measure a transcript by its word error rate (WER): wrong words divided by words spoken. Under 5% you correct by hand in a minute; between 5 and 15% you correct names and numbers before any summary; above that, you re-record closer to the microphone. What you count here is also what a client will pay for: one error on a figure in a report costs more than ten typos.

Your turn · which link?

Six errors at the end. At which link of the chain were they born?

Microphone → transcription → summary → translation. An error born early travels through every link after it: that is why you correct right after the transcription, never at the end.

What to look for on an image generated in 2026. Six-fingered hands and unreadable signs were 2023; Gemini, Ideogram and Canva hardly make them any more. What remains is quieter, and dearer: an invented detail (a price, a number, an address, an opening time you did not give — the tool fills in what is missing, like the text model); a place from nowhere (a “Dakar boutique” that looks like every boutique in the world, a “wax” fabric that is nobody’s); a character whose face changes from one image to the next, because each image starts again from noise; a borrowed logo or face (a tick that looks like Nike’s, a person who looks like a singer — and you do not have the rights); and a format that does not fit the channel. None of these is visible to a hurried eye: you look at the image with the list.

Try it here · 3 · where the image comes from

A generator starts from noise and removes it, step by step, guided by your text. Do it by hand.

Denoising · from step 0 to step 50

The mechanism, in three lines. In training, you take millions of captioned images, add noise to them in small doses, and teach the network to remove one dose knowing the caption. In generation, you start from pure noise and apply that “removal” fifty times; at each step your prompt (turned into numbers by a language model) pulls the result toward the images that carried those words. Nothing is searched or pasted: the image is invented from the noise, which is why two generations are never identical — and why one word of your prompt can change everything.

Why assemble rather than generate? Video generators (Veo, Sora, Kling, Runway) produce, in 2026, five-to-ten-second shots, often superb, often wrong — an impossible gesture, melting text, an object that changes shape — and their free tiers are counted in seconds a day. For a shop, an edit of real images with a real voice is more credible, faster, and you control every second of it. When you need a generated shot, you will put it inside this edit, as one shot among four.

Sign of generation · proves nothing

What about “AI detectors”? They give a percentage, and that percentage is worth nothing: the same tools declare hand-written texts “generated” (the Universal Declaration of Human Rights has been flagged) and images generated the day before “human”. The reason is mechanical: a detector is a model trained on yesterday’s generators, and tomorrow’s generators are trained to fool it. What works is provenance: where the file comes from, who published it first, and — more and more — the invisible signature serious tools write into the file (the C2PA Content Credentials of Adobe, Google, OpenAI; the SynthID watermark in Gemini’s images). Your images from 3.3 carry one.
