Section 2.2 · Done together

The same prompt on three assistants — who wins, and why

Together · 25 min

Paste your prompt from 2.1 into three assistants. Score each answer on five criteria, 0 to 2.

Name your three assistants in the headers. Click a cell to cycle the score: 0 failed · 1 half · 2 yes. The total computes, the winner shows. Three things to look at closely: what is invented (a figure, a name, a rule you did not give), the requested format (is the table a table? are the 40 words 40?), and completeness (did it handle every case, or the first three?).

The grid · five criteria × three assistants

The prompt was the same. The difference is the model — and it changes every two months. Never swear by an assistant: swear by your grid.

What we almost always observe
  • The ranking depends on the task. The best at writing is not the best at tables, nor at arithmetic. A professional has two or three assistants and knows which serves what.
  • Format is the criterion most often failed — and the easiest to fix: a one-line follow-up is enough (section 2.3).
  • “Nothing invented” is the criterion worth money. An assistant that adds a case number that does not exist loses you a customer; one that writes “I don’t know” wins you one.

Section 2.4 · Ten minutes of mechanism

How the machine writes — and why your five parts work

What happens when you press Enter

Your prompt is cut into tokens; the model computes, for the next token, a probability for every possible token; it draws one; and starts again.

prompt → tokens → network → probabilities of the next token → draw → one more token → … → the answer

Four words, and each explains something you have seen since 13:30. Tokens: the model sees neither letters nor words, but pieces of words. Next word: it searches for nothing, it continues the most probable text — your examples included, since they are part of the text. Temperature: the draw has an adjustable share of chance. Context window: everything it “knows” of your conversation must fit in a fixed number of tokens; beyond that, the beginning falls off.

1 · Tokens

Type a sentence. See it as the model sees it.

Tokenisation · approximation

This is why an assistant miscounts the letters in a word and gets a seven-digit multiplication wrong: it has never seen isolated letters or digits, only pieces. And it is why a rare word costs more than a common one: it is cut into more tokens. The split above is an imitation; each model has its own split, learned from its data — on average an English word is about 1.3 tokens.

2 · The next word, and the temperature

An unfinished sentence, five possible continuations, a probability each. Set the temperature, then draw.

“The capital of Switzerland is …”

The bars are illustrative, the arithmetic is the real one. A model gives a score to each of its ~100,000 tokens; the temperature divides those scores before they are turned into probabilities. Low, it always lets the favourite win; high, it gives the outsiders a chance. The free assistant you use runs around 1: that is why two answers differ, and why, once in twenty, it writes “Zurich”.

3 · The context window

Let the conversation run. Watch what falls off.

What the model reads at every answer

The model has no memory: at every answer it re-reads the whole conversation, and only what fits in the window. Hence three practical consequences: your opening instructions must be repeated in a long conversation (or put in the “custom instructions”, which stay); a long pasted document eats the window; and a new conversation knows nothing of the previous one, unless the tool has a memory feature — which is a note it pastes back to itself.

Your turn

Why does it happen? Six phenomena, four mechanisms.

Tokens · next word · temperature · window

Role, context, examples: all of it is text before the answer, and the model continues the text. You explain nothing to it — you put in front of it the beginning of a document whose continuation is what you want.

Section 2.6 · Block 2 · Done together

Your prompt library for your programme

Together · 40 min

Five recurring tasks — in your project, your programme, your job. Five prompts, five parts each. Test at least two.

A prompt library is what a professional really keeps from AI: not answers, but requests that work, reusable by changing two lines of context. Write each prompt in full (role, context, task, format, examples), even if it is long — especially if it is long. Tick “tested” once you have sent it and read the answer. The five prompts and their answers make up hand-in 2.

Leads by programme. Management: summarise a report in five decisions · prepare a meeting agenda from e-mails. Finance: explain a balance sheet to a non-financial reader · spot the abnormal lines in a statement. Marketing: three versions of a post for three networks · ten customer objections and their replies. HR: a job ad from a job description · the questions of a structured interview. International relations: a briefing note on a UN report · two opposing readings of the same event. Hospitality: reply to a negative review · a translated menu with allergens. Studies: revision cards from your notes · ten exam questions with answers.
Section 2.7 · Done together

The prompt duel

Together · 20 min

Give your prompt no. 1 to your neighbour, without a word of explanation. They send it, read the answer, and score you.

Exchange the prompts in writing (a message, a piece of paper). The other runs it as is in their assistant and fills in the grid below on the answer obtained. Then compare: does the answer look like the one you expected? Where it differs, a part of the prompt was missing — or clear only to you.
