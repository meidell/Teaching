Suggested structure (you may adapt)

Executive summary (~150 words). The strategic question, your 2×2 axes, your headline finding so far.
System scope and boundary (~200). What you are analysing — geography, time horizon, decision being supported. What you are explicitly leaving out.
Stakeholders and drivers (~400). The actors that matter and why; the forces shaping their behaviour; one or two feedback loops.
Sustainability challenge framed through your scenario matrix (~400). Your two critical uncertainties, the four worlds they generate, what each implies for the strategic question.
Indicators and datasets (~300). The metrics that would tell you which scenario is unfolding; the sources you will use in Phase 2.
Limitations and next steps (~150). Honest acknowledgment of what your framing leaves underspecified and how Phase 2 closes the gaps.

The 2×2 matrix itself is not the Phase 1 deliverable. It is the framing through which you select stakeholders, draw boundaries and choose indicators. Students who treat the matrix as an appendix produce weaker reports than students who let it discipline the whole document.

The orthogonality test · a cohort-wide pitfall

Make the second axis a driver, not an outcome

A matrix a student drafted last year illustrates the most common failure mode in scenario design. The two axes were: Axis 1 — European coordination and investability (integrated, rules-based, financeable ↔ fragmented, protectionist, insecure); Axis 2 — delivery capacity and system resilience (high delivery / resilient ↔ low delivery / fragile).

The thinking behind both is sound. Each is a real critical uncertainty. But put together they fail the independence test. A fragmented, protectionist Europe will, in the real world, tend to produce lower delivery and a more fragile system; an integrated one, higher delivery. If the axes correlate, two of your four cells collapse — the off-diagonal scenarios become hard to construct without an explicit causal story for why they should exist. This pattern — a "policy" axis paired with what is effectively a "policy outcome" axis — also fails Schoemaker's plausibility test (Pitfall 3, p. 39).

The general fix: make sure your second axis is a driver, not an outcome. Drivers that pair well with a policy or coordination axis:

  • Pace of cost decline — Wright's Law continues vs stalls
  • Supply-chain geopolitics — open vs weaponised, especially China
  • Capital-cost environment — rates stay elevated vs fall back
  • Demand-side electrification speed — mass vs partial
  • Speed of grid build-out — held up vs unblocked

The worked example — climate-policy strength × renewable cost-decline speed — is chosen precisely because the two are reasonably independent. A strong-policy world might still see slow cost decline if supply chains seize; a weak-policy world could still see fast decline if Chinese manufacturers keep flooding the global market.

Self-check before you commit your axes

Write a one-sentence narrative for each of the four cells. If the off-diagonal cells need lengthy contortions to make plausible, your axes are correlated and you should fix one before you commit.

If cells 2 and 3 took you three times as long as 1 and 4, that is the test failing. Post the final axes in Monday's forum and I will tell you by Tuesday.

Pressure-test the draft · before you lock it

Three questions to put to your own 2×2

A draft matrix can pass the cell-by-cell test above and still fail the report. Last term's on-campus cohort brought a full draft to this point. Their four worlds were coherent, numbered and traceable, and the draft still had the three problems below. Put each question to your own draft before Monday's post.

1 · What kind of thing have you built?

A recall from Week 4, now applied to your own draft. Schoemaker (1995) sets scenario planning apart from three things that also get called "scenarios": a forecast, a sensitivity analysis and a contingency plan. Week 4's first pitfall, best / base / worst on one axis, is the sensitivity analysis. Open your Week 4 axes and scenarios and decide which of the four you have.

Called a scenarioWhat it isWhat it is good for
ForecastOne best estimate of one future: one line, one numberThe short term. It fails under deep uncertainty.
SensitivityOne input varied at a time ("gas +20%")Testing a model. It is not a scenario.
ContingencyA plan for one bad thing: the "downside case"Better than nothing, but it still assumes one future.
ScenarioSeveral coherent futures built on different axesStress-testing a strategy
Sort them · which of the four is each one?

2 · Is one world the official future in disguise?

Week 4 listed Schoemaker's pitfalls in building a scenario set. These are three more to look for once the set is drafted:

  • Equal-probability worlds. The four worlds are presented as equally likely. They are not. You prepare as if any of them could happen, but that is not the same as calling them equally likely.
  • The official future in disguise. One world matches the company's plan or the policy target. The other three exist to flatter it.
  • Scenarios as forecasts. Every world ends in a point estimate, and the discussion turns into which estimate is right. The four worlds collapse back into one number.
The check, done on last term's draftTheir best world, "Green Acceleration", delivered 250+ GW of EU offshore wind by 2040. The member states' non-binding 2040 goal, as updated in December 2024, is 259–261 GW. These are not two numbers in tension; they are the same number. If your best world is the policy target, ask what the other three are for. Do the same check on yours: put your highest-deployment world next to the official target (EU sea-basin goals, Ørsted's or TotalEnergies' stated plan) and see whether they match.

3 · Are your axes really independent, or is the second axis hiding?

A draft built on Cost × Policy, the pair this course uses as its worked example, still has to show that the axes are independent. Policy reaches cost through at least four channels:

ChannelHow policy moves cost
AuctionsCfD strike prices set project IRR; auction design shapes financing cost and therefore LCOE
PermittingPermit duration changes carrying costs and the development-risk premium before a single MW is built
Carbon priceThe ETS price moves power prices, and so the price wind has to beat
Industrial policyThe Net-Zero Industry Act, local-content rules and US IRA spillovers reshape turbine-maker economics

Cost × Policy survives only if you define the two axes narrowly. For example, the cost axis could cover global technology learning (Wright's Law, Chinese manufacturing) and the policy axis EU ambition, so that each captures an uncertainty the other cannot.

The second risk is a hidden axis: a constraint that cuts across all four cells of your matrix. Last term it was supply-chain capacity. Neither cost nor policy decides whether the vessels, cables, magnets and people exist. The Kühne Climate Center (January 2026) estimates that the installation fleet outside China could install about 175 GW of offshore wind by 2030 with €2.5–4 billion a year of new vessels, and about 140 GW without them. China's fleet alone covers about 225 GW. A 250 GW world that assumes a fleet nobody has ordered is a wish list, not a scenario.

Option A · keep your pair. Sharpen the definitions until each axis captures a different uncertainty, and show in one paragraph why their interactions do not collapse the four worlds. This is the conservative option: most of your draft stays.
Option B · replace the outcome-like axis with supply-chain capacity (e.g. Cost × Supply chain). This is the most orthogonal pair and the biggest rewrite.
Option C · make cost an output. Policy × Supply chain, with cost low where both deliver and high where both fail. Last year's online cohort made this choice (Week 4). The causality is cleaner and the rewrite moderate.

For B and C, define the supply-chain axis as a driver. "Supply chain open ↔ weaponised" or "vessels, cables and magnets available ↔ scarce" are drivers: they can go either way whatever Brussels decides. "Delivery capacity high ↔ low" is an outcome, the same correlated axis the orthogonality section above warns against. Run the four-cell test above again on the new pair.

Then test each world's number against the constraints

Before a deployment figure goes into your report, ask what would have to be true for it, and whether that is plausible in that world. As a worked example, here are last term's four worlds (not the ones from Week 4) and the question each one had to answer:

World2040 claimThe question it must survive
Green Acceleration250+ GWDo the vessels, cables and ports allow that build rate? If the draft never confronts the constraint, the world is a wish list.
Price-Driven Growth150 GWIf high prices mean high returns, why does capital not eventually fix the permitting bottleneck? Trace the causality.
Policy-Constrained Deployment200 GWWhat happens to Ørsted's auction IRR at a €55/MWh strike price? Does 200 GW survive its hurdle rate?
Stalled Transition130 GWThat is about six times the EU's 21 GW today. Is "stalled" the right name for a sixfold expansion, or is it "sub-target"?

Now do it for your own four worlds, the ones in your Week 4 consulting file (Exercise 2). For each world, write its 2040 number and the one question that could break it. A number that fails the question gets changed, and so may the world's name. The result goes into the "Sustainability challenge framed through your scenario matrix" section of your Phase 1 report.

From scenario matrix to system map

Three ways the 2×2 disciplines the map

1 · Boundary. The two uncertainties you commit to determine which actors and flows belong inside your system boundary. If you have chosen "EU coordination" as an axis, EU institutions, member-state regulators and capital markets are inside; the consumer-side electrification of European households may be outside — a different system, even if it touches yours.

2 · Indicators. Each of your four scenarios should have at least three quantitative anchors that distinguish it from the others. Those anchors are your indicators. They give you a list — not a comprehensive list, a discriminating one — of what you will need data on for Phase 2.

3 · Feedback loops. The reason scenarios diverge is that systems contain reinforcing and balancing loops, and small differences in starting conditions amplify. Your map should show at least two loops — typically a reinforcing loop driving deployment (cost decline → adoption → scale → cost decline) and a balancing loop constraining it (deployment → grid congestion → curtailment → revenue compression → slower investment). Sterman Chapter 1 is the source for how to draw them.

Build a loop · mark each link + (same direction) or − (opposite), and read off R or B

The rule from Sterman: count the minus signs around the loop. Even (including zero) → reinforcing. Odd → balancing. Two loops from the wind system, with the signs left for you.
