Learning curves and their limits

Last week you chose your indicators, your method and your hypothesis. This week you apply the method to your own data. Most of you will use Wright's Law: the rule that every doubling of the total capacity ever built cuts the unit cost by the same percentage. The rule has no upper limit built into it, and every optimistic 2040 wind forecast relies on it. Between 2021 and 2024 the cost of offshore wind went up, not down. So this week has two halves: fit the curve to your data, then find out where it bends and why.

Core concepts · 1 and 2

Fitting your curve, and producing three projections

Two terms first. Cumulative installed capacity is the total capacity ever built, in gigawatts (GW): not what is built this year, but everything built up to this year added together. LCOE, the levelised cost of electricity, is the price per megawatt-hour (MWh) at which a power plant exactly pays back the cost of building and running it over its life; it is the one number that lets you compare a wind farm with a gas plant.

By hand, before the formula. Global offshore wind: about $203 per MWh in 2010 when 3.1 GW had been built, about $79 per MWh in 2024 when 83 GW had been built (rounded IRENA and GWEC figures, the fitter's default rows). From 3.1 GW to 83 GW the cumulative capacity doubled 4.74 times (because 24.74 = 26.8 = 83 ÷ 3.1). The cost fell to 79 ÷ 203 = 0.389 of its 2010 value. Spread over 4.74 doublings, that is 0.3891/4.74 = 0.820 per doubling: each doubling left 82% of the cost, so it removed 18%. That 18% is the learning rate.

Wright's Law writes the same idea as a formula: Cost(Q) = Cost(Q₀) × (Q ⁄ Q₀)−b. Q is cumulative capacity now, Q₀ the cumulative capacity at your starting point, Cost(Q₀) the cost at that starting point, and b the learning parameter. The learning rate is 1 − 2−b; a learning rate of 15% means b = 0.234, and every doubling of Q leaves 85% of the cost.

Published learning rates for offshore wind LCOE fall between 9% and 18%, depending on the years and the countries in the sample. Onshore wind is usually 8% to 12%. Solar photovoltaics has run above 20% for four decades, which is why every forecaster who extended solar's cost in a straight line was wrong. You are not choosing one of these published numbers. You are fitting a rate to your series and then saying what it means.

Three checks before you trust your fit

  • Look at the plot. The fitter below draws cost against cumulative capacity with both axes in logarithms. Wright's Law is a straight line on that plot. If the dots curve away from the line, the learning rate is changing over time and Wright's Law is the wrong model for the series.
  • Look at the residuals. A residual is the distance between a dot and the line. If the dots fall above the line for the early years and below it for the late years (or the other way round), something besides capacity is moving the cost: interest rates, steel prices, a supply-chain crisis. Name it in your post.
  • Check the 2040 number against the world. An offshore LCOE below $30 per MWh in 2040 means your assumed 2040 capacity is too high or your early data too expensive; above $120 per MWh means the opposite. Both should send you back to the rows.
By hand, a second time, on the last four years only. 2020: $86 per MWh at 34.4 GW. 2024: $79 at 83 GW. Capacity doubled 1.27 times (83 ÷ 34.4 = 2.41 = 21.27); cost fell to 0.919 of its value; per doubling 0.9191/1.27 = 0.935, a learning rate of 6.5%. The whole series learns at 18% a doubling; the last four years learn at a third of that. A fit that gives every year the same weight hides this. Report both numbers.

Core concepts · between the fit and the limits

Where the curve bent, 2021–2024, and the three mechanisms that bend it

Your Phase 2 report needs more than a fitted line, because the line has just been contradicted. Here is what happened to offshore wind costs in the last four years, with sources, and then the three mechanisms that explain it. Each mechanism is a reason your central projection could be wrong, and each has a number you can put in your report.

On a Wright's Law plot that is a point far above the line. Your fit must either explain it or admit that it cannot.

2 · Mechanism one: the cost of capital

A wind farm is almost all up-front cost: the turbines, foundations and cables are paid for before the first MWh is sold. That is why the interest rate matters more for wind than for a gas plant, whose main cost is fuel bought year by year. The formula makes the effect visible:

LCOE = (installed cost per kW × capital recovery factor + annual O&M per kW) ÷ MWh produced per kW per year

The capital recovery factor (CRF) turns a one-off investment into an equal annual payment over the plant's life, exactly like a mortgage: CRF = r(1+r)n ⁄ ((1+r)n − 1), where r is the cost of capital and n the life in years. O&M is operation and maintenance, the yearly running cost. MWh per kW per year = 8.76 × the capacity factor, because a kilowatt running every hour of the year would make 8.76 MWh, and a wind farm runs at a fraction of that (its capacity factor, about 45% to 52% for new offshore farms).

By hand. Installed cost $2,852 per kW (IRENA 2024), life 25 years, O&M $90 per kW per year, capacity factor 45%. At a cost of capital of 4%: CRF = 0.0640, so the capital costs $182.6 per kW per year; add $90 of O&M = $272.6; divide by 8.76 × 0.45 = 3.94 MWh: LCOE = $69 per MWh. At 7%, with nothing else changed: CRF = 0.0858, capital $244.7, total $334.7, LCOE = $85 per MWh. Three percentage points on the interest rate add 23% to the cost of electricity, with the same turbines on the same site. Between 2021 and 2023, long-term rates in the US and Europe rose by about three points. That is most of the UK row.
Cost of capital and LCOE · change one input, read the result

The result is compared with the same plant at a 4% cost of capital, the level European offshore projects financed around 2020. Try 4%, 7% and 9%, then try the 2010 installed cost of about $5,400 per kW at 4% to see why learning and cheap money together produced the 2010–2020 fall. Inputs are IRENA 2024 for the installed cost; the O&M and capacity-factor defaults are round mid-range values for you to replace with your project's.

3 · Mechanism two: the supply chain and the quality of what it delivers

Wright's Law assumes that building more makes each unit cheaper. In 2021–2023 the industry built more and each unit got more expensive, for three reasons you have already met. Turbine makers raced to 13–15 MW machines and some of the new platforms failed early: Siemens Gamesa's €1.6 billion repair bill is the price of learning too fast. Installation vessels able to lift a 15 MW nacelle are few, and Week 5 showed that the non-Chinese fleet cannot carry the 2030 pipeline. Steel, copper and shipping prices rose with inflation after 2021. Week 6's turbine timeline showed the rating curve flattening at 13–15 MW from 2023 to 2027: when the machine stops growing, one of the two engines of the learning curve (bigger turbines spread fixed costs over more MWh) stops running.

4 · Mechanism three: learning itself slows

The second by-hand calculation above is the evidence: 18% a doubling over 2010–2024, 6.5% over 2020–2024. Learning rates are not constants of nature. They are high while a technology moves from prototypes to factories, and they fall as the easy improvements are used up. Two things to test on your own series: fit the first half and the second half of your years separately and compare the two rates; and check whose capacity your Q column counts. Most of the 83 GW of global offshore wind at the end of 2024 was built in China, at Chinese prices. A European cost series fitted against a global capacity series borrows learning that European projects may never see.

5 · What this does to your three projections

Add the mechanism to the scenario, as a second line under each learning rate. The conservative world is not only "10% learning"; it is 10% learning and a cost of capital of 7%, which the table above turns into dollars. The aggressive world is 20% learning and 4% capital and a vessel fleet that is built in time. Write your central projection with its bend named: which mechanism, how large, and what evidence would tell you by 2030 that it has started.

Core concepts · 3

Where the curve breaks — planetary boundaries

Rockström and colleagues (2009) defined nine planetary boundaries. Richardson and colleagues (2023) found six of them crossed: climate change, biosphere integrity, biogeochemical flows, land-system change, freshwater change and novel entities. European wind presses on several of them. Tap each boundary to read how:

The nine boundaries · which does wind press on, and how?
Tap a boundary. Red = crossed in the 2023 assessment.

The pattern to state in your report: wind relieves one boundary (climate) partly by pressing on others (biosphere, novel entities, freshwater). That is not an argument against building it. It is the trade-off a consultant writes down, so that the client decides with it in view. The same applies to the AI you use: Luccioni (Week 6) gave you its energy and carbon; Li et al. give you its water.

Rare earths: how much, and where it binds

The offshore turbines being built today are mostly direct-drive machines with permanent magnets: no gearbox, and a generator ring of magnets made of neodymium and praseodymium, hardened with dysprosium, all rare-earth elements. The IEA (2021) expects these machines to be about 95% of the offshore market by 2040, and rare-earth demand from wind to more than triple by 2040 in its sustainable-development scenario. Its chart (p. 64) draws the rare-earth content of a direct-drive permanent-magnet turbine between 150 and 300 kg per MW; a gearbox turbine with an induction generator, the onshore standard, uses almost none. Put Week 6's build rate through that:

Rare earths for Europe's offshore build · tonnes a year, and the share of the world's supply

11 GW a year is what the member states' 2030 goal needs (Week 6). The world mined 390,000 tonnes of rare-earth oxides in 2024 (USGS, Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025), of which China 270,000 tonnes (69%); Burma, the main source of the heavy rare earths such as dysprosium, fell from 43,000 to 31,000 tonnes between 2023 and 2024. China also does about 90% of the processing that turns ore into oxides, metals and magnets (IEA 2021). The price of neodymium oxide fell from $134 per kg in 2022 to $56 in 2024 (USGS), so the market shows no shortage today.

Read the result as a consultant would: Europe's whole offshore build needs about half a percent of what the world mines each year. The boundary does not bind through tonnage. It binds through where the processing happens (one country, about 90%), what it leaves behind (the thorium-bearing tailings and acid waste at Bayan Obo in Inner Mongolia and at the Lynas plant that treats Mount Weld ore, which is the "novel entities" boundary), and the one element whose supply is genuinely thin, dysprosium. That is the sentence Forum 14 asks you to write for your own two boundaries.

Core concepts · 4

The water you did not measure last week

Last week's AI audit estimated the energy and carbon of your Phase 1 work. It did not include water. Li et al. (2025) measure it in two places. On-site water evaporates in the cooling towers that keep the servers cool; they express it as a water-usage effectiveness, WUE, in litres per kWh the servers use (0.55 litres per kWh for an average US data centre, Table 1). Off-site water is what the power plants use to generate the data centre's electricity, mainly steam cooling at gas, coal and nuclear plants; they express it as EWIF, litres per kWh of electricity (3.14 litres per kWh for the average US grid, Table 1), applied to the data centre's whole draw, which is the server energy times the PUE (power-usage effectiveness, 1.17: for every kWh the servers use, the building uses 1.17). Their headline figures: training GPT-3 once in an average US data centre used 5.4 million litres, 700,000 of them on-site; and GPT-3 "drinks" a 500 ml bottle for 10 to 50 medium-length responses, 29.6 responses in the US average, 10.5 in Washington State, 70.4 in Ireland.

Water footprint of your project to date · Li et al.'s formula with their Table 1 factors
Wh per turn:

On-site litres = turns × Wh ÷ 1,000 × WUE. Off-site litres = turns × Wh ÷ 1,000 × PUE × EWIF. The two presets are the two honest answers to "how much energy does one turn use": Li et al. assume 4 Wh per GPT-3 response (0.004 kWh, from OpenAI's 2020 figure of 0.4 kWh per 100 pages); Google, OpenAI and Epoch AI published 0.24–0.34 Wh for a short prompt to a 2025 assistant (Week 6). They differ by a factor of 13, and your report should say which one you used and why. For the place: Washington State's factors are WUE 0.95 and EWIF 9.5, Ireland's 0.02 and 1.48 (Table 1); a data centre on a wind-heavy grid has an EWIF near 0.1. Look up the region you think your provider routes through on WRI Aqueduct ↗ to see whether that water is scarce there.

Why this belongs in a recommendation about wind: if you argue that wind helps decarbonise Europe and you built the argument with an assistant whose data centre draws water in a dry region, your client is entitled to know. Li et al. project that AI will withdraw 4.2 to 6.6 billion cubic metres of water worldwide in 2027. Say what your share was, in litres, and whether a wind-powered data centre would have changed it.
