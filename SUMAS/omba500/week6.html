Core concepts · 1

From Phase 1 narrative to Phase 2 numbers

Phase 1 was a system map. Phase 2 is a stress test.

Your Phase 1 said something like: "In a Climate-First + High-China-Integration scenario, costs fall fastest and deployment accelerates." Phase 2 has to answer the harder question: how would I know if that is the scenario actually unfolding right now? What number is moving, in which direction, by how much?

That is why the indicators you proposed in Phase 1 matter so much. They are now your measurement instruments. The next three weeks depend on having two or three that are:

  • Trackable — published regularly by credible sources (IRENA, BloombergNEF, WindEurope, IEA, Ember)
  • Discriminating — they move differently across your scenarios; if all four worlds produce the same number, the indicator is useless
  • Forecastable — you can project them to 2040 with some method, however imperfect

Most Phase 1 submissions name 3–5 indicators. For Phase 2, narrow to 2–3 you will actually analyse quantitatively. The rest stay in your bibliography.

Core concepts · from signals to signposts

Turn each early signal into a signpost

Week 4 asked each world for 2–3 early signals. Phase 2 needs those signals in a form that a published number can confirm or rule out. A signpost is an indicator with four parts written down in advance: what is measured, a checkpoint date, the value each world expects at that date, and the band of uncertainty around a reading. With all four fixed before the data arrives, the data can disagree with you.

1 · Start from the baseline, and from the official future

Take the indicator most proposals use: installed offshore wind capacity in the EU-27.

ReadingValueSource
Installed at the end of 2024, EU-27about 21 GWWindEurope, 2024 statistics (Europe including the UK and Norway: 37 GW)
Connected during 2024, all of Europe2.6 GWWindEurope, against 3.8 GW expected at the start of the year
Member states' 2030 goal, updated December 202486–89 GWNon-binding sea-basin agreements; the January 2023 version said about 111 GW

From 21 GW to 88 GW in six years means about 11 GW a year, more than four times what all of Europe connected in 2024. That is the official future, already lowered once. A world that reaches it has to say where four times today's build rate comes from; Week 5's vessel figures show why that is hard. Note also the definitional trap: Europe and the EU-27 differ by 16 GW of offshore capacity, more than five years of the 2024 build rate. Decide which one your indicator counts, and say so in the proposal.

2 · Give each world a value at the checkpoint

A worked example on Week 4's matrix, EU policy ambition × cost decline. The 2030 values are illustrative, set for this example; yours come from the narratives in your Phase 1 report.

WorldEU-27 offshore, 2030Build rate needed, 2025–30Why this value
Renewable Renaissance50 GW4.8 GW a yearBoth engines fire; vessels, ports and grid connections bind
Subsidised Transition42 GW3.5 GW a yearThe state pays through contracts for difference; volume follows budgets
Market-Driven Greening36 GW2.5 GW a yearCheap enough to win corporate power contracts, with no push from auctions
Stalled Transition30 GW1.5 GW a yearCancellations and deferrals; only projects already financed are built

Build rate = (2030 value − 21 GW) ⁄ 6 years. Even the most ambitious world here stays far below the 86–89 GW goal: that is a finding to state, not a mistake to hide.

3 · Test whether the indicator can tell the worlds apart

A reading discriminates between two worlds only if their expected values differ by more than the uncertainty in the reading. Offshore capacity is lumpy: a 1–1.5 GW farm commissioned in January instead of December moves a year's figure by that much, and data providers revise their numbers. A band of ±3 GW at 2030 is a reasonable start. Two worlds can be told apart when the gap between their values is larger than the two bands together.

Signpost check · does a 2030 reading tell the worlds apart?

Enter your own worlds' values and your own band. The check is the same for any indicator: four expected values, one band, one reading.

With the defaults, the reading separates the outer worlds clearly, but neighbouring worlds are only 6–8 GW apart, so their bands touch or nearly touch. The deeper problem is that capacity reads both axes at once. Subsidised Transition (strong policy, slow cost decline) and Market-Driven Greening (weak policy, fast cost decline) sit 6 GW apart, and a slightly different set of assumptions would swap them. A reading of 39 GW would not say which axis moved.

4 · Pair it with one indicator per axis

The fix is to add indicators that each read one axis only:

AxisIndicatorWhat a reading tells you
Cost declineAuctions without state revenue support: are bids made at all, and at what price per MWh?Whether offshore wind's cost has fallen below what the power market pays
Policy ambitionCapacity offered in auctions each year, and whether revenue support (contracts for difference) is includedHow hard governments are pushing, and whether they will pay for it
A real reading, December 2024Denmark offered about 3 GW at three North Sea sites with no state subsidy and received no bids (Danish Energy Agency, 5 December 2024). Developers pointed to high costs and power-price risk. On the cost axis, that reading points away from the two fast-decline worlds. In January 2025 the government cancelled the tender and announced that the next one may include state support: on the policy axis, a move toward Subsidised Transition rather than Stalled. One event, read on two axes, says more than a year of capacity figures.

5 · Write your signpost table

For each of your 2–3 indicators, write the checkpoint year, the value in each of your four worlds, the band, and the source. Run the check above on each. An indicator whose four values fall inside one band is not discriminating, and it leaves the proposal. Check, too, that between them your indicators read both of your axes. Methods you might use

You met these in Week 3. Now you commit to one or two:

MethodWhat it doesWhen to use it
Regression / curve fittingFit a function through historical dataWright's Law on LCOE, learning-rate estimation
Time-series forecastingProject future values from past valuesEU wind capacity to 2040, generation growth
Monte Carlo simulationVary input drivers, see the distribution of outcomesCapital-cost sensitivity on LCOE, scenario probability bands
ClusteringGroup similar things togetherSegment EU offshore markets by maturity, cost, policy support

You don't need to build any of these from scratch. You need to know which one your indicators call for, and how to use Claude / ChatGPT / Perplexity to apply it.

A Monte Carlo in one paragraph, since the table mentions itTake your LCOE formula. Instead of one value for the cost of capital, give it a range — say 5% to 9% — and let a spreadsheet draw a random value 1,000 times, recomputing LCOE each time. The 1,000 results are a distribution: "LCOE is $54/MWh" becomes "LCOE is between $48 and $66 in 90% of draws, and the cost of capital explains most of that". It is the honest way to put a band around a number when the uncertainty is in an input, not in the data. Ask your assistant for the spreadsheet formula; it is =NORM.INV(RAND(), mean, sd) and a copy-down.
Core concepts · 3

The AI you are using has an energy cost

By the end of Phase 2 you will have spent significant compute on this project. Luccioni and colleagues quantify what that costs. Some numbers to hold in your head while reading:

  • What Luccioni et al. measured. Open models of up to 11 billion parameters, 1,000 queries each on one GPU. Text generation averaged 0.047 Wh per query; image generation 2.9 Wh per image on average and 11.5 Wh for the least efficient model (Stable Diffusion XL), about half a smartphone charge.
  • Large assistants, as published in 2025. A typical short text prompt costs about 0.24–0.34 Wh: Google reports 0.24 Wh for the median Gemini text prompt (August 2025, measured in production); OpenAI states 0.34 Wh for an average ChatGPT query (June 2025, no method published); Epoch AI estimates about 0.3 Wh for GPT-4o (February 2025). Long documents, long answers and "reasoning" modes cost several times more, and a median hides that tail.
  • A working session. 100 text turns at 0.3 Wh is about 30 Wh; ten images from a large image model add up to about 115 Wh more.

That is small per person. At scale — millions of users, several sessions a day — it becomes a measurable load on the grid. Some of that grid is wind. Some of it isn't.

Your AI footprint · the calculation Activity 2 asks for

Defaults: 0.3 Wh per turn is the 2025 published range for a short prompt to a large assistant (0.24–0.34 Wh); raise it to 1–3 Wh for turns with uploaded documents or long answers. 2.9 Wh per image is Luccioni et al.'s Table 2 mean for image generation (2.9 kWh per 1,000); use 11.5 for the least efficient model they tested; 220 gCO₂/kWh is a European mid-point (150–300). Use Electricity Maps ↗ for the data-centre region you think your provider routes through. The point is not precision; the point is that the number exists and you can estimate it within an order of magnitude.

Why this matters for your consulting recommendation: if you are going to claim that wind helps decarbonise European electricity, you should also be able to estimate whether AI tools — growing fast, run in data centres, increasingly sited near cheap renewable supply — are net helpful or net harmful to that decarbonisation story. Week 7 returns to this with the water footprint paper.

Activities · this week

The proposal, the audit, and an optional timeline

Activity 1 — Phase 2 proposal · on Moodle by

Submit a ~600-word Phase 2 proposal that I can approve before you begin the data work. It must include:

The central quantitative question your Phase 2 will answer. One sentence, derived directly from your Phase 1 scenarios. Example: "How fast must offshore wind LCOE fall for the Hyper-Scale Transition scenario to remain plausible by 2040?"
The 2–3 indicators you will track. For each: what it measures, why it discriminates between your scenarios, the source (with URL), and how often it is updated.
The analytical method(s) you will apply, and why. Be specific. "I will fit a Wright's Law curve to historical offshore wind LCOE 2010–2024 using log-log regression, then project to 2040 under three learning-rate assumptions (10%, 15%, 20%)." Not "I will analyse the data."
One paragraph on what could go wrong. Data gaps, definitional inconsistencies (Europe vs EU-27 — a student flagged this well last year), method failure modes, anything that might invalidate your conclusions. Acknowledging this in advance is what separates analysis from advocacy.

Activity 2 — Your AI audit. Estimate the energy and carbon footprint of producing your Phase 1 report.

  1. Review your Phase 1 chat history with Claude, ChatGPT or whichever assistant you used. Count the substantial conversational turns (a turn = a question + answer).
  2. Apply the per-query estimates from Luccioni et al. (or her 2024–2025 follow-up figures). Distinguish text turns from image generations if you used those. The calculator above does the arithmetic.
  3. Convert energy to CO₂-equivalent with a reasonable grid factor — for European data centres serving European users, ~150–300 gCO₂/kWh is a rough mid-point.
  4. Post 250–350 words: your estimate, with the assumptions you made · one number that surprised you, and what it surprised you about · whether you would report this AI footprint in your final consulting recommendation, and why or why not · whether reading Luccioni changes how you will use AI in Phase 2 (be honest).

Week 7 preview · Learning curves and their limits

Next week you take the Wright's Law work from Week 3 and apply it to your own indicators. But you also confront the limit: Rockström and colleagues defined the planetary boundaries the global economy is pressing against — rare earths, steel, biodiversity, freshwater. Learning curves quietly assume scaling is unbounded. The planet says otherwise. And Li et al. (2025) extend the thinking to AI itself: every query also has a water footprint. Bring: an approved Phase 2 proposal, the historical series for at least one indicator, and a working hypothesis about where the cost curve might bend or break.
