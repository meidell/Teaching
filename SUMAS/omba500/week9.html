Read

Wack, P. (1985) Scenarios: Uncharted Waters Ahead. Harvard Business Review, 63(5), 73–89.
hbr.org ↗ (use the library if the page asks you to pay). Pierre Wack led scenario planning at Shell in the early 1970s. His scenarios prepared Shell's managers for a sudden rise in oil prices, which then happened in 1973. Read the article for four ideas, each explained below on this page:
· First-generation scenarios: scenarios that describe possible futures but do not change any decision.
· Second-generation scenarios: scenarios that make managers see their present situation differently, so that they change what they decide. Wack calls the picture of the world that a manager carries in his or her head the manager's microcosm; a second-generation scenario changes it.
· The two failures Wack warns against: scenarios that only confirm what the executives already believe, and scenarios so far from their experience that they dismiss them.
· Predetermined elements: developments that will happen in every scenario because they are already under way (for example, a wind farm already under construction will be running by 2030). Separate them from the uncertainties, the developments that could go either way, which are the axes of your scenarios.

Use Wack's distinction as a test of your own report in the next section.

Core concepts · 1

An analyst describes the possible futures; an advisor tells the client what to do in them

For eight weeks you have worked as an analyst: you described the system, measured indicators and built scenarios, and you could leave all four scenarios open. An advisor has to go one step further. An advisor tells the client which position to take, says which scenario he or she thinks most likely, and says which scenario would do the most damage if the client ignored it. A client hires a consultant for that commitment, so your report must make it.

Apply Wack's test to your four scenarios. A first-generation report describes four worlds; the executive finds them plausible and decides nothing differently. A second-generation report shows the executive something about the present that he or she had not seen, for example that the company's plan only works if interest rates fall back to their 2021 level.

The test for your Phase 3: write down one decision that the chief executive of Ørsted or of TotalEnergies would change after reading your scenarios. If you cannot name one, your report is first-generation; go back to the scenario that worries you most and say what it would cost the company.

Core concepts · 2

The robust-strategy test: how each company fares in each of your four scenarios

In Week 4 you made a first version of this table (exercise 3). Now redo it with your Phase 2 evidence. For each company and each scenario, choose one of four results: the company thrives (it grows and earns well), survives (it keeps its business), struggles (it loses money or market share) or fails (it leaves European offshore wind or needs rescuing). Base each choice on a number from your work, such as the LCOE or the capacity you projected for that world.

Two terms describe the pattern you get. A strategy is robust when the company survives or thrives in at least three of the four scenarios (the Week 4 rule). A strategy is a high-variance bet when the company thrives in one or two scenarios and struggles or fails in the others: the result depends on which world arrives. A robust company may earn less in the best world than a company that made a bet.

Score it · type the names of your four scenarios, then rate each company in each

The page counts Thrive as 3 points, Survive 2, Struggle 1 and Fail 0, out of 12. Do not read the total alone: 9 points made of Thrive, Thrive, Thrive, Fail is a bet, and 9 points made of Survive, Survive, Survive, Thrive is robust. The two suit different clients.

Which company is "better positioned" depends on what the client wants. A pension fund, which must protect the money it will pay out to pensioners, prefers the robust company. A growth investor, who accepts losses in some worlds for large gains in others, may prefer the bet. A government building an industry at home weighs jobs and supply chains as well as profit. So name the client in your recommendation, for example: "For a long-term institutional investor whose first aim is to protect its capital, [company] is better positioned because…".

Two possible arguments, one per company — test them against your own scores; you do not have to agreeFor Ørsted: it is a specialist in building and running offshore wind farms. If the main limit on growth in Europe is the ability to build farms on time and on budget, rather than access to new sites, a specialist is in a strong position. The weak point is its own record: on 1 November 2023 Ørsted stopped its Ocean Wind 1 and 2 projects in the United States and took DKK 28.4 billion of impairments (write-downs of the value of its assets), citing supplier delays and higher US interest rates (Week 7). Your scores should say in which worlds that risk returns.
For TotalEnergies: it is an integrated energy company. It earns most of its cash from oil and gas and reports electricity, including wind, in its Integrated Power segment. It can therefore keep investing in wind when wind earns little, and it can sell customers a bundle (wind power plus gas-fired or stored power for the hours without wind) under long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs: contracts in which a buyer agrees to purchase a farm's electricity at a fixed price for many years). The weak point is that the board can move money back to oil and gas whenever those earn more. Your scores should say in which worlds that happens.
Core concepts · 3

Early-warning signals: the numbers that tell the client which scenario is arriving

A recommendation that holds only in some scenarios is useful only if the client can see which scenario is arriving. An early-warning signal is a published number, with a threshold and a date, that would tell the client a particular scenario is becoming the most likely one within the next two to three years. For each of your four scenarios, write two or three signals.

You have already built most of them. In Week 6 you turned your indicators into signposts: an indicator, a checkpoint year, the value you expect in each world and a band around it. In Phase 2 you measured where each indicator stands today. A signal is that signpost written as a sentence for the client: "if the indicator is above this value by this date, this scenario is arriving". In Week 7 you also found that each extra percentage point on the cost of capital adds about $5 per MWh to the cost of offshore electricity ($69 at 4 % and $85 at 7 %, with the Week 7 inputs). Use that figure to turn an interest-rate signal into a cost signal.

Three examples, each with a source the client can check:

  • "If the European offshore LCOE that IRENA publishes each summer is still above its 2024 level of $80 per MWh in the reports of 2027 and 2028, the costs are not falling again, and the Stalled Transition becomes the central case."
  • "If a Chinese manufacturer is chosen to supply the turbines of more than one EU offshore wind farm by the end of 2027, the High-China-Integration scenario is arriving." (Wind farms are won in auctions by developers such as Ørsted; the turbine makers then win supply contracts from those developers.)
  • "If the return on average capital employed of TotalEnergies' Integrated Power segment stays below 10 % for two years in a row, its strategy of investing in power alongside oil and gas is failing." The return on average capital employed (ROACE) is the segment's operating profit divided by the capital invested in it; TotalEnergies reports it in its results. Check the current value before you choose a threshold.

Link each signal to your recommendation: say what the client should do if the signal is reached (for example, "reduce the position in Ørsted" or "nothing changes"). A client should be able to check your signals once a quarter and know what to do.

Writing the report

How to divide the 4,000–5,000 words of the Phase 3 report

SectionWordsWhat goes here
Executive summary~400One page that a busy executive can read alone: your recommendation, the conditions under which it holds, and the signals to watch.
The system~700A shorter version of Phase 1: the stakeholders, the drivers, the feedback loops and the scenario matrix, corrected with what you have learned since.
The evidence~900A shorter version of Phase 2: your indicators, your learning curve, your three projections and your cross-check, with the charts that support your recommendation.
The scenarios~700Your four 2040 worlds, 150–180 words each, each showing what it would mean for the two companies (Wack's second generation).
The robust-strategy test~800The table of scores from this page, the evidence behind each score, and the pattern: which company is robust and which is a bet.
Recommendation and signals~600Your recommendation, the client it is for, the conditions under which it holds, and the early-warning signals with what the client should do when each is reached.
Limitations~300What you do not know, what could make the recommendation wrong, and what further work would answer it.
  • Do not copy paragraphs from Phase 1 or Phase 2. Shorten and rewrite them so that each one supports the recommendation.
  • Use about five charts. Keep a chart only if the reader needs it to follow the argument; the Phase 2 chart of your projections with its uncertainty band is usually one of them.
  • Assume the reader has 20 minutes. In that time they read the executive summary, look at the scenarios and one chart, and decide whether to trust you. Put the most important points where they will look.

What to include

  • The seven sections above, or another order that you can justify
  • The charts from Phase 2 that the argument needs, and any new chart that sums up the scenario scores
  • One reference list with the sources of all three phases
  • A short AI declaration at the end: which assistants you used and for what, and the energy and water footprint you estimated for your own use in Weeks 6 and 7

Activities · this week

Post a draft, challenge two classmates, hand in the report

Activity 1 — Draft recommendation (post in Forum 17 by Wednesday). The draft lets two classmates test your argument before you write the full report. In 400–500 words, write: your recommendation in one sentence; the client it is for (an institutional investor, a government, a pension fund…); the conditions under which it holds; your two or three strongest pieces of evidence, with their numbers; and the strongest argument against your recommendation that you can think of, with your answer to it.

Activity 2 — The peer challenge (reply in Forum 17 by Friday). On Thursday morning I post in Forum 17 who challenges whom: each of you challenges two classmates and is challenged by two. Each challenge is a reply of 200–300 words with three parts: the weakest step (which step of their argument is easiest to attack, and why); the counter-evidence (which number or scenario, if true, would make their recommendation wrong); and the hardest question a sceptical client would ask them. I grade the quality of your challenge, not whether the author agrees with it. Strategy consultancies test their recommendations this way before a client sees them.

How to write a challenge. Criticise the argument, not the author. A good challenge is precise about the weakness and polite to the person: quote the sentence you challenge and say what evidence would change your mind.
