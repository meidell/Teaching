How AI works — and how to investigate without being fooled

About 90 minutes. Bring your problem in one sentence, and keep one AI assistant open in another tab.

Phase 2 asks you to investigate your problem with AI. To do that well you need to know what these tools actually do when they answer: they predict text. That single fact explains why they are fluent, why they are useful, and why they sometimes invent a study that does not exist. This session opens the machine, then gives you the four habits of a good investigation: the right tool, a precise prompt, a checked source and a real number.

✅ Block 1

What a language model does: the next token

Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini are large language models. Underneath the chat window, each one does one thing, again and again: given the text so far, it computes a probability for every possible next piece of text, picks one, adds it, and repeats.

Step by step

  1. Tokens. Text is cut into pieces called tokens — whole words, parts of words, punctuation. A model has a fixed vocabulary of tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand of them. "Bangkok's" might be two tokens: Bangkok's.
  2. Training. The model is a neural network with billions of adjustable numbers (parameters). In pre-training it reads an enormous amount of text and, for each position, is scored on how well it predicted the token that actually came next. The parameters are adjusted, trillions of times, to predict better.
  3. Fine-tuning. Afterwards it is trained further on examples of helpful conversations, and on human (and AI) ratings of which answers are better. This is what turns a text predictor into an assistant that answers questions.
  4. Answering. Your prompt is turned into tokens. The model computes the probability of each possible next token, one is chosen, appended, and the whole process runs again — hundreds of times for one answer.

What follows from this
  • The model writes what is probable, not what is checked. Most of the time these coincide; not always.
  • It has a training cut-off: without a search tool it knows nothing that happened after its training data was collected.
  • It has a context window: a maximum amount of text it can take into account at once — your conversation, plus any documents you paste.
  • The same question can get a different answer twice, because the choice of each token involves chance.
✅ Block 2

Why a model invents a study

A reference has a very regular shape: authors, year, title, journal, volume, pages, DOI. A model has seen millions of them, so it is extremely good at producing text with that shape. If it has also read the real paper many times, it may reproduce the real reference. If it has not, the most probable continuation is still a reference-shaped string — plausible authors, a plausible journal for the topic, a plausible year, a DOI in the right format. Nothing in the next-token mechanism checks that the paper exists. This is called a hallucination.

Part of the referenceWhy it looks right
AuthorsCommon surnames in the field, in the usual order and format.
TitleBuilt from the words of your question: "PM2.5", "school", "Delhi", "monitoring".
JournalA real journal that publishes on this topic.
DOIThe real prefix of that publisher (10.1016/ for Elsevier, 10.1038/ for Nature) followed by a plausible number. It may lead nowhere — or to a different, real paper.

The second case is the dangerous one: the link works, so a hurried check passes. Block 5 has one of each.

✅ Block 3

Assistants that search: how the citations get there

Perplexity, and Gemini or ChatGPT when they search the web, add a retrieval stage before the model writes. This is often called retrieval-augmented generation. The pipeline:

  1. Rewrite the question into one or several search queries.
  2. Search the web (or an index of it) and take the top results.
  3. Read those pages, cut them into passages, and rank the passages by how relevant they are to the question.
  4. Write the answer with the language model, giving it the best passages as context, and attach a citation number to sentences that draw on a passage.

The citations are now to pages that exist — a real improvement. But each stage can still fail:

StageWhat can go wrongWhat you check
SearchThe top results are a news summary or a blog, not the original study or dataset.Follow the citation to the original source.
ReadA passage is taken out of context — a number for another city, or another year.Find the number on the page; check place, year, unit.
WriteThe model blends two sources, or attaches a citation to a sentence the source does not support.Read the cited passage: does it say exactly this?
The sourceThe page itself is wrong, old or biased.Who published it, when, and with what data?
✅ Block 4

Four tools, four strengths

All four are free at entry level, and all four can be wrong. The pathway uses them as a set and picks the tool that fits the task. The strengths below are the pathway's starting guide; the tools change every few months, so test them on your own problem.

Claude · AnthropicLong, careful reasoning. Structured analysis. Writing and editing reports that hold together.
ChatGPT · OpenAIBrainstorming. Drafting fast. General-purpose ideas.
Gemini · GoogleReal-time information. Images, maps, multimodal input. Connected to Google Search and YouTube.
Perplexity · Perplexity AIWeb research with sources you can click. Built for fact-checking.

✅ Block 7

Where real numbers come from

An AI assistant is a way to find data, not a source of data. The number you put in the essay must come from the organisation that measured it. Open sources by topic:

AirOpenAQ — open air-quality measurements from government and research monitors worldwide. WHO air pollution data — city annual means and the WHO guideline values.
WasteWorld Bank · What a Waste — waste generation and treatment by country and many cities. Your city's own annual environmental report.
WaterWRI Aqueduct — water stress, drought and flood risk on a map, down to river basins.
Heat, land, satellitesCopernicus Climate Change Service and NASA Earthdata — temperature, land cover, satellite imagery.
ForestsGlobal Forest Watch — tree-cover loss and deforestation alerts from satellite data.
BiodiversityGBIF — species occurrence records; iNaturalist — observations, including your own.
