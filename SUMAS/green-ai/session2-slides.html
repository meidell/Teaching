Part 1 · What a language model does

An AI assistant writes its answer one small piece at a time, by predicting what comes next

Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini are large language models: computer programs trained on huge amounts of text to continue a text in a likely way.

Step 1 · text is cut into tokens

The model reads and writes text as tokens, small pieces from a fixed list

InBangkok'scanals,thewaterlevel

Token: a piece of text the model treats as one unit. It can be a whole word, part of a word, or a punctuation mark. A model's list holds tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand tokens.

"Bangkok's" may be two tokens: "Bangkok" and "'s". The exact cut depends on the model.

Step 2 · training

In training, the model guesses the next token of real texts and is corrected each time

  1. The model reads a text from its training data, up to a point
  2. It guesses which token comes next
  3. It is scored on how close its guess was to the real next token
  4. Its parameters are adjusted a little, so the next guess is better

Parameters: the billions of numbers inside the model that training adjusts, trillions of times.

Step 3 · fine-tuning

Further training on good conversations turns a text predictor into an assistant

After the first training, the model is trained again on examples of helpful answers, and on ratings by people (and by other AI models) of which answer is better. This is why it answers your question instead of just continuing your sentence.

Step 4 · answering

The model gives every possible next token a probability

In November the air in Delhi is often …
polluted34 %
hazy22 %
smoggy17 %
unhealthy12 %
cold8 %
beautiful4 %
purple3 %

Invented numbers for this demonstration, not from a real model. One token is picked and added, and the step runs again, hundreds of times per answer.

How the token is picked

A setting called temperature decides how often the model takes an unlikely token

Low temperature (0.3)

  • "polluted" 73 %
  • "hazy" 17 %
  • "purple" almost 0 %
  • Answers are predictable and repetitive

High temperature (1.8)

  • "polluted" 25 %
  • "hazy" 19 %
  • "purple" 6 %
  • Answers vary more, and odd words appear

Same invented numbers as the last slide. Try it yourself on the Session 2 page, Block 1: press "Pick the next token and add it" and watch the sentence grow, one token at a time.

What follows for your investigation

Four facts about the machine that change how you use it

  • It writes what is probable, not what is checked. Usually the two agree; not always.
  • It has a training cut-off: the date its training texts end. Without a web search it knows nothing after that date.
  • It has a context window: the most text it can take into account at once, your conversation plus anything you paste.
  • The same question can get a different answer twice, because each token is picked with some chance.
Part 2 · Why a model invents a study

A model can write a reference to a study that does not exist, and it looks exactly like a real one

This is called a hallucination: the model states something false in the same confident way it states something true. In Session 1 you heard about a student whose AI gave four sources on Milan's air; two were invented.

Each part of a reference has a regular shape

The model has seen millions of references, so it writes the shape perfectly

PartWhy the invented one looks right
AuthorsCommon surnames in the field, in the usual order
TitleBuilt from the words of your own question: "PM2.5", "school", "Delhi"
JournalA real journal that publishes on this topic
DOIThe publisher's real prefix (10.1016 for Elsevier, 10.1038 for Nature) and a plausible number

DOI (digital object identifier): a code that points to one exact study. Paste it after https://doi.org/ to open the study.

Two kinds of invented reference

An invented DOI either leads nowhere, or leads to a different real paper

Easy to catch

doi.org answers "DOI not found". Crossref has no such title.

Hard to catch

The link opens a real paper, but on another topic, by other authors. A check that only clicks the link passes it.

Crossref (search.crossref.org): a free index of published research. Search the title there; if it is not found, be suspicious.

Part 3 · Assistants that search the web

Search assistants find real pages before they write

Perplexity does this every time; Gemini and ChatGPT do it when they search the web.

  1. Rewrite your question into one or more search queries
  2. Search the web and take the top results
  3. Read those pages, cut them into passages, rank the passages
  4. Write the answer from the best passages, with a citation number on each sentence

This is called retrieval-augmented generation: the model first retrieves (finds) real texts, then writes its answer from them.

The citations are real pages, but each stage can still fail

Check each citation against what the page really says

StageWhat can go wrongWhat you check
SearchThe top result is a blog or news summary, not the studyFollow it to the original source
ReadA number for another city or another year is usedFind the number on the page: place, year, unit
WriteTwo sources are blended, or a citation does not support the sentenceRead the cited passage: does it say exactly this?
The sourceThe page itself is old, wrong or one-sidedWho published it, when, with what data?

Part 5 · An investigation prompt

A precise prompt with five parts gets an answer you can check

  • 1 RoleWho should answer: "an environmental scientist who works with city air data"
  • 2 ContextYour city and your problem, in one or two sentences
  • 3 TaskExactly what you want: "list the public datasets that measure…"
  • 4 ConstraintsHow it must answer: "only sources with a link; say when you are not sure"
  • 5 FormatThe shape of the answer: "a table with source, what it measures, years, link"
One example, with all five parts
You are an environmental scientist who works with city air-quality data. I am a student in Delhi studying fine dust (PM2.5) at school gates on winter mornings. List the public datasets that measure PM2.5 in South Delhi, hour by hour, since 2022. Give only sources with a working link, and write "not sure" when you are not sure. Answer as a table: source, what it measures, which years, link.

The vague version, "Tell me about pollution in Delhi", gets a general essay with no sources you can check.
