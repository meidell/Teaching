Your talking points — 45 minutes, slide by slide

Bullet points for each of the 17 slides of session3-slides.html. The session sums up the project and gets every student started on essay 1, due Friday 4 December, 23:59 CET.

How to use this

If students join live

Present the slides and say each bullet in your own words. Run the two activities (slides 13 and 16) and read answers from the Zoom chat.

If no one joins

Record yourself talking through the bullets, advancing the slide at each new heading. At each pause, tell viewers to stop the video, do the task, then come back.

Length: about 30 minutes of talking, two activities (5 + 3 minutes) and questions ≈ 45 minutes. Rough split: slides 1–7 the summary (about 12 minutes), 8 the calendar (3), 9–13 essay 1 and activity 1 (15), 14–15 what comes after (5), 16–17 activity 2 and close (10). Audience: students aged 15–17 in several countries, some working in a second language. Before you start: open the course page, the Phase 1 hand-in page and the Session 3 page in tabs. The lines in italic (grey) are directions to you. The footprint numbers on slide 7 are computed from the two sources on the Session 3 page (300 × 0.26 ml = 78 ml; 300 × 0.24 Wh = 72 Wh; 300 × 10–50 ml = 3–15 L).

Slide 1Title: the whole project in one view, and how to start your first essay today
  • Welcome them to the last live session; it is recorded, so anyone absent can watch it later.
  • Say the aim in one sentence: by the end of these 45 minutes, every student knows what to hand in, when, and what to do this week.
  • Ask them to open two tabs: the course page (janerikmeidell.com/SUMAS/green-ai) and the Journal page.
Keep slide 1 up while students join.
Slide 2What these 45 minutes give you
  • Summary first: the project and what the first two sessions taught them.
  • Then one new idea: AI uses energy and water, and AI can also protect people; their report must show both sides.
  • Then the calendar and the first essay in detail, because it is due on Friday 4 December.
  • Two short tasks: they write their essay plan, and they write the next three things they will do.
Slide 3The project in one sentence
  • Read the sentence slowly: a problem in your own city, AI as the tool, and an honest record of how you used it.
  • "Your city" means a problem they can see: the air on the way to school, waste in the street, heat, flooding, water.
  • "Show how" means the AI Process Journal: every time they use AI, they write it down.
Slide 4Four steps, five pieces of work
  • PICK: they choose the problem and prove with numbers that it is real. That is essay 1.
  • INVESTIGATE: they find the data and the science: what causes the problem, how it is measured. That is essay 2.
  • PROPOSE: they compare solutions used elsewhere and recommend one for their city. That is essay 3.
  • PITCH: they revise the three essays into one report of 4,500 words and present it in 3–5 minutes with 10 slides.
  • Stress: each essay is a draft of one third of the final report, so nothing is wasted.
Slide 5What Sessions 1 and 2 gave you: three habits
  • Habit 1, precision: a problem sentence names what, where, and one number. Give the Delhi example from Session 1.
  • Habit 2, checking: in Session 2 they saw that an AI can invent a study with real-looking authors and a DOI. Explain DOI once: the permanent code of a scientific article; Crossref says whether it exists.
  • Habit 3, recording: every AI use goes into the Journal, which is handed in with each essay.
  • Ask: who has already made at least one Journal entry? Live: hands up or chat.
Slide 6AI costs water and energy, and AI can also save lives
  • The cost: training GPT-3 is estimated to have used about 5.4 million litres of water, once the water used by the power plants is counted. That is about two Olympic swimming pools.
  • Using it costs too: about one 500 ml bottle of water for every 10 to 50 answers, according to the same study.
  • The benefit: Google's AI flood forecasts warn people up to 5 days before a river floods, even on rivers with no measuring station. By November 2024 they covered 100 countries with about 700 million people.
  • The point for their project: if they propose an AI solution in essay 3, they must also say what it costs. An honest report shows both sides.
Slide 7Your project's own footprint
  • Suppose a student uses AI 300 times for the whole project.
  • With Google's measurement for its Gemini assistant (0.26 ml and 0.24 Wh per prompt): 300 × 0.26 = 78 ml of water, about a glass, and 300 × 0.24 = 72 Wh of electricity, about one hour of a laptop under load.
  • With Li and colleagues' estimate for GPT-3 (10 to 50 ml per answer, power plants included): 3 to 15 litres.
  • Why so different: a different model, a different year, a different data centre, and Google counts only the water used on site.
  • Their report needs one sentence on this: the footprint of their own project, and which estimate they used. The Session 3 page has a calculator for it (Block 2).
Slide 8The calendar: five deadlines
  • Read the five dates. All deadlines are at 23:59 Swiss time (CET); tell them to convert it to their own time zone (India: 04:29 the next morning; Dubai: 02:59 the next morning; Bangkok: 05:59 the next morning).
  • Essay 1 is due Friday 4 December: next week.
  • Essay 2 on Friday 8 January and essay 3 on Friday 29 January.
  • The final report and the 10 slides on Sunday 28 February.
  • The presentation is with the instructor alone, at a time agreed with each student in February or March.
  • SUMAS counts the final report as the main assessment; each essay is a graded step of the work in progress.
Slide 9Essay 1: the problem is real, local and measurable
  • Essay 1 has one job: to convince the reader that the problem exists, in their city, and how big it is.
  • It ends with a question that essay 2 will investigate.
Slide 10Essay 1: five sections and a word budget
  • Walk through the five sections and their word counts: 200, 400, 300, 350, 250, which add up to 1,500.
  • Section 2 is the heart: three numbers or more. Each one says where it comes from, which year, and the unit (for example "178 micrograms of fine dust per cubic metre of air, winter 2024, Centre for Science and Environment").
  • Section 5 is one precise question, for example "Which hours and which roads cause the highest fine-dust levels at my school gate?"
  • The budget can change; the five sections should all be there.
Slide 11Before you hand in essay 1: six checks
  • Read the six checks; these are what the essay is read against.
  • The rule on writing: AI can search, brainstorm, suggest a structure, comment on a draft. The sentences are theirs.
  • Every reference must open. An invented source is the most common and the most serious mistake.
  • The Journal page prints the Phase 1 entries as an appendix: they press Print and save as PDF, or add it to the end of the essay PDF.
Slide 12How to hand in
  • One PDF: the essay plus the Journal appendix.
  • The file name: their surname, then GreenAI, then Phase1, for example Sharma_GreenAI_Phase1.pdf.
  • The hand-in page is linked from the course page. They sign in with the same name and password as on the course pages.
  • Show the upload on screen if possible (open handin.html), and point out the "Received" line.
  • If anything fails, they send the file through the message panel before the deadline, so it counts as on time.
If live, share the screen and open the hand-in page for Phase 1.
Slide 13Do this now: your essay 1 plan in five lines
  • Give them five minutes to write one line per section.
  • An empty line is fine: it tells them what to find this week.
  • Live: ask two or three students to read their line 1 and line 5 aloud, or to paste them in the chat; comment on whether the place and the question are precise.
Pause

Activity 1 · 5 minutes. Students write the five lines of their essay 1 plan. Recording: tell viewers to pause the video now.

Slide 14Essays 2 and 3 in one view
  • Essay 2, due 8 January: the data and science that answer the question from essay 1, with at least one table or chart, and one AI error they caught.
  • Essay 3, due 29 January: solutions tried elsewhere, compared on six criteria (fit, evidence, who acts, cost, speed, footprint and fairness), each scored 0, 1 or 2; then their own proposal.
  • A solution without AI is allowed, if it is the best one; they say why.
  • Both are explained step by step on the Session 2 and Session 3 pages, Blocks 4 and 5.
Slide 15The final report and the presentation
  • The final report is the three essays, revised with the feedback, plus a new introduction (about 300 words) and a new conclusion (about 500 words).
  • It ends with the references and the whole Journal.
  • The presentation: 10 slides in about four minutes, so about 25 seconds a slide. One idea per slide.
  • Both are due on Sunday 28 February at 23:59 CET.
Slide 16Do this now: your next three actions, each with a day
  • Three actions, each with a day before Friday 4 December.
  • Read the example: one search logged in the Journal, two sections written, references checked.
  • Live: they type them in the chat; pick two and make them more precise if needed.
Pause

Activity 2 · 3 minutes. Students write three actions with a day each. Recording: tell viewers to pause the video now.

Slide 17Help, and close
  • Messages: the 💬 panel on every course page; the instructor answers there.
  • Coaching: a short online meeting, on request, about the problem or a draft. They ask in the 💬 panel.
  • All three session pages stay online; this recording too.
  • Close: the first deadline is Friday 4 December at 23:59 CET. Thank them; take questions.
