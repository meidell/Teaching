If no one joins

Length: about 45 minutes of talking plus two short activities ≈ one hour. Audience: students aged 15–17, in several countries, some working in a second language — so the language is plain and every term is explained the first time. Tone: warm, direct, unhurried. The lines in italic (grey) are directions to you, not words to say.

Slide 1Title — Pick a problem in your city

Hello, and welcome to Green AI. My name is Jan Erik Meidell, and I will be your instructor for this project. This is the first of three live sessions, and it is being recorded — so if you are watching this later, or if you need to hear something again, everything I say today stays available to you.

Here is the whole project in one line: pick a problem in your city, use AI to crack it, and show how. That is what you will spend the next few months doing. Today I will explain exactly what that means, and by the end of this hour you will already have chosen a first problem to work on.

Slide 2What this hour gives you

Let me tell you where we are going in this hour, so nothing comes as a surprise. First, I will explain what Green AI asks you to do, and what gets graded. Then I will show you the four steps of the project and every important date between now and February. I will show you how three short essays grow into one longer report. We will talk about what makes a good problem to work on — and this is the part that matters most for your grade. I will introduce something called the AI Process Journal, which is your proof that the work is yours. And I will tell you exactly what to bring to our next session.

Two of the things on this list you will actually do today, with me, during this hour. So keep a notebook or a document open while you watch.

Slide 3Why this course exists

Before any of the rules, let me tell you why this course exists at all. When people say the word "sustainability", a lot of you probably picture melting ice caps, or polar bears, or a graph of the whole planet getting warmer. Those things are real. But they are also far away, and very big, and it is hard to feel that you can do anything about them.

So we are going to turn the camera around. This course is not about the planet in general. It is about the place where you actually live. It is about the air you breathe on the way to school. The heat you already plan your summer around. The water that comes out of your tap. The rubbish that leaves your street and goes… somewhere. That is where sustainability becomes something you can see, measure, and change — and that is where your project will live.

Slow down here. This is the heart of the "why". Let it breathe.
Slide 4Delhi — 178 µg/m³

Let me show you what I mean with three real numbers. Here is the first one.

One hundred and seventy-eight. That is the average amount of fine dust in Delhi's air, measured across the winter of 2024, by an organisation called the Centre for Science and Environment. The unit is micrograms per cubic metre of air — think of it as how much dust is floating in a box of air one metre on each side.

Now, is 178 a lot? The World Health Organization — the United Nations' health body — says that over a year, to stay healthy, that number should sit around 5. So Delhi's winter air was roughly thirty-six times higher than the level considered safe.

And this dust has a name: PM2.5. That means particles smaller than two-and-a-half micrometres across — far thinner than a human hair. They matter because they are small enough to slip past your body's defences, go deep into your lungs, and even pass into your blood. If some of you live in or near Delhi, you already know this air. You have felt it in your throat. That feeling is the number on this slide.

Slide 5Dubai — 142 mm in a day

Here is the second number, and this one is for those of you in Dubai especially. One hundred and forty-two millimetres. That is how much rain fell on Dubai in a single day — the sixteenth of April, 2024. The UAE's own national weather service said it was the heaviest rainfall the country had seen since records began in 1949. Roads turned into rivers. Homes flooded. The airport — one of the busiest in the world — had to stop flights.

Think about how strange that is. Dubai is a desert city. It was built for a place where it almost never rains. And then it got more than a year's worth of rain in one day. That is what a changing climate looks like up close: not a distant graph, but the street outside your building under water. A problem like "my neighbourhood floods, and the drains cannot cope" is exactly the kind of thing this project is made for.

Slide 6India — 160,000 tonnes of waste a day

And here is the third number. One hundred and sixty thousand tonnes. That is how much household and street waste India's cities throw out every single day, according to India's Central Pollution Control Board. About half of that gets treated or recycled. But nearly a third of it is simply never properly accounted for — meaning nobody is quite sure where it ends up.

I like waste as an example because it is the most visible problem of all. You do not need a scientific instrument to study it. You can walk out tomorrow with your phone and photograph it: the pile by the market, the plastic in the canal, the bin that is never emptied. That photograph can become the first piece of evidence in your report.

Slide 7What those three facts share

Now look at what those three numbers have in common, because this is the bridge into your whole project. Each one is visible — you can see it, or breathe it, or photograph it. Each one is local — it is a specific city, not "the Earth". And each one is measurable — there is a number attached, so you can track whether it gets better or worse.

Visible. Local. Measurable. Hold on to those three words. In a few minutes I am going to ask you to use them to choose a problem of your own.

Slide 8The project and its four steps

So here is the project, stated plainly. You will pick a real environmental problem in your city, use AI tools to understand it and to explore solutions, and show — openly — how you used those tools. It runs in four steps, and they always happen in this order.

Step one is PICK: choose a real problem you can see and touch. Step two is INVESTIGATE: use AI — tools like Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini or Perplexity — to dig into the data, the science, and what is happening in your own city. Step three is PROPOSE: again using AI, you explore what solutions have been tried elsewhere, and you choose one that could work where you live. And step four is PITCH: you bring it all together into a final report and a short presentation.

PICK, INVESTIGATE, PROPOSE, PITCH. Four words. That is the shape of the next few months.

Let me make those four steps real with one quick example, so you can picture the whole journey. Imagine you choose this problem: the canal behind your school fills with plastic rubbish every Monday, after the weekend market. That is your PICK — it is visible, it is local, and you could literally count the bags. In INVESTIGATE, you would use AI to help you find out how much waste a market like yours produces, what plastic does to water and to fish, and whether any nearby city has measured the same thing — and then you would check every fact the AI gave you against a real source. In PROPOSE, you would ask AI what has actually worked elsewhere — a deposit paid on bottles, a net stretched across the canal, a different collection day — and you would weigh those up and choose the one that fits your city. And in PITCH, you would write it all up and stand in front of me for four minutes and say: here is the problem, here is what I found, and here is what I would do about it. One problem, four steps. You can already see the whole shape of it.

Slide 9Why AI sits at the centre

Let me be honest with you about the AI part, because this is unusual. In most schoolwork, using AI is treated as cheating. In this project, it is the opposite: using AI is the method. You are expected to use it, a lot. But you have to be clever about it, and here is why.

AI assistants are genuinely brilliant at some things. They search and summarise faster than any of us. They explain hard ideas in simple words. They can read a messy table of data and sort it out. They help you draft and organise your thoughts.

But they also do something dangerous: they are sometimes confidently wrong. They will invent a scientific study that does not exist. They will make up a number and present it as fact. They sound just as certain when they are guessing as when they are right. And they often miss things that are true only in your particular city. So the second half of this project — the "show how" — means you write down every step you take with AI, and you check every claim it makes. We will come back to exactly how.

Slide 10What you hand in, and the grade

Let me show you what you actually hand in, because I know that is on your mind. You do not sit down one day and write one giant report from nothing. Instead, you write three short essays, one for each of the first three phases. Each one is about 1,500 words — that is a few pages. After each one, you get feedback from me.

Then those three essays join together, with your improvements, into one final report of about 4,500 words. That final report is the main thing that is graded. On top of that, there is a short presentation — three to five minutes, with ten slides — that you give to me.

For those of you who like to know how the marks work: the final report is worth 25% of this part of your grade, the presentation is worth 10%, and together they make up the Applied Project, which is 35% of your whole Year 2 grade. But honestly — do not think about percentages today. Think about picking something you actually care about. The marks follow good work; they do not lead it.

Slide 11Three essays, one report

I want to make sure you really understand how the essays and the report fit together, because students worry about this. The three essays are not three separate pieces of homework that you do and forget. Each essay is a first draft of one third of your final report.

Essay one is about the problem: what it is, where it is, how big it is, and who it hurts. Essay two is about the investigation: the data and the science, checked against real sources, and what they show about your city. Essay three is about the proposal: what solutions have been tried elsewhere, and the one you recommend. Then the final report is simply those three essays, rewritten using the feedback I gave you, and joined into one clear argument with a single introduction and a single conclusion.

The reason we do it this way is kindness, really. You hand in each piece early, so that when I point out a weakness, you still have plenty of time to fix it — instead of discovering it the night before the final deadline.

Write to me there any time you are stuck, and I will answer. The exact deadline times, down to the hour, are on the hand-in page of the site.

Slide 13Phase 1 · PICK

Right — that is how the project works. Now we get to the real work, and the most important decision you will make in this whole course: choosing your problem.

I am not exaggerating when I say everything rests on this. If you choose a good problem, every later step gets easier — the data is there, the solutions are findable, the report almost writes itself. If you choose something too big, or too vague, you will fight it for four months. So let me give you the tools to choose well.

Slide 14Five properties of a good problem

A good project problem has five properties. Let me walk through them, and for each one I will tell you what goes wrong if your problem is missing it.

First, it must be visible — you can see it, photograph it, or measure it yourself. If it is invisible to you, your report stays abstract and you have no first-hand evidence. Second, it must be local — in your city or neighbourhood, not "the planet". "Climate change" is far too large to investigate or solve in 4,500 words. Third, it must be measurable — some number describes it: micrograms of dust, tonnes of waste, degrees of heat, litres of water, a count of birds. Without a number, phase two has nothing to analyse. Fourth, it must be documented — real data or studies exist, for your city or a similar one. This one is important with AI: if no real information exists, the AI will happily invent some, and you will have nothing solid to check it against. And fifth, it must be actionable — someone could actually do something about it: a school, a city office, a company, the residents. Otherwise phase three, your proposal, has no one to propose anything to.

Let me make those five concrete with one example, so they are not just words. Take the air at a school gate. Visible: you can stand there and photograph the haze. Local: it is your gate, not "India" or "the planet". Measurable: there are sensors that give you a PM2.5 number in micrograms. Documented: newspapers and scientists have written about your city's air, so real data exists to check against. And actionable: your school could change its drop-off times, or plant a hedge of trees along the fence. Five ticks. That is a problem worth four months of your time.

Visible, local, measurable, documented, actionable. If your problem has all five, you have chosen well.

Slide 15Narrow beats broad

Now, the single most useful piece of advice I can give you today. Make your problem narrow. Much narrower than feels comfortable.

Look at the two on the screen. On the left: "air pollution in my city." That is not a problem — that is a topic. It is enormous. You could write a library about it. On the right: "the PM2.5 level at my school's gate during the morning drop-off, compared with a quiet street two blocks away." That is a problem. Do you see the difference? The narrow one tells you exactly what to measure, exactly where, and exactly what to compare it with. It practically hands you your investigation.

So whatever area you choose, keep asking yourself: can I make this smaller? Can I tie it to one street, one school, one market, one river? Narrow is not a weakness here. Narrow is how you win.

Slide 16Where to look

If you are not sure where to start, here are six areas where good problems often hide. Air — the dust and traffic fumes near your school. Waste — where your rubbish actually goes, and who pays for it. Water — shortages, or flooding, or some neighbourhoods getting clean water while others do not. Biodiversity — the trees, birds, or green spaces that are quietly disappearing. Urban heat — the districts with no shade that become ovens in summer. And flooding — the streets that go under water every storm or monsoon.

And if none of those spark anything, try these three questions. What do you walk past every single day that nobody seems to be fixing? What did your grandparents have — clean air, a river you could swim in, a forest behind the house — that you do not have any more? And what in your neighbourhood would you not want your own future children to grow up with? Somewhere in your honest answers to those three questions is your project.

Slide 18The Phase 1 essay

Welcome back. Now that you have a problem in mind, let me show you what your first essay needs to do, so that December deadline is not a mystery. It is about 1,500 words, and it has to do five things.

One: name the problem, the place, and the people affected — precisely, not vaguely. Two: give at least three numbers about it, and for each number, say where it came from, what year it is from, and what the unit is — and I mean numbers you have checked yourself, not just taken from an AI. Three: explain why it matters — is it about people's health, about money, about nature, about fairness? Four: say what is already being done about it, and by whom. And five: end with the exact question you will investigate in phase two. The page gives you a suggested word budget for each part, which you can adjust.

Write it in your own words. That matters. The AI can help you find things and check things, but the sentences in the essay are yours.

Slide 19The AI Process Journal

This next part is the heart of how we use AI honestly, so listen closely. It is called the AI Process Journal. The rule is simple: every single time you use an AI tool for this project, you write down three things.

One, the prompt — the exact words you typed to the AI. Two, the output — what it gave you back. And three, your reflection — what you did to check it, and what you decided to keep or throw away. Three things, every time.

You hand this journal in together with every essay, and with the final report. And here is why it is so valuable to you: it is your proof that the work is genuinely yours. When you can show the whole trail — here is what I asked, here is what the machine said, here is how I checked it and made it my own — nobody can accuse you of letting a machine do your thinking. The journal is what lets you use AI openly and proudly, instead of hiding it. There is a Journal page on the website that stores your entries, saves them to your account, and prints them out neatly as an appendix.

Here is what one entry actually looks like, so it is not a mystery. The prompt: "Find studies on PM2.5 air quality in Mumbai between 2018 and 2024." The output: the AI listed four studies. The reflection: "I checked all four reference codes on Crossref; two led to real papers and two led nowhere, so I kept the two real ones and threw the others away." That is the whole entry — three short lines. If you write one of those every single time you open an AI tool, two good things happen: your appendix basically writes itself, and your honesty is right there on the page for anyone to see.

Slide 20Verify everything — the Milan story

Let me tell you a true story about why the checking matters. A student was working on air quality in Milan, in Italy. They asked an AI to find them scientific studies on the subject. The AI gave back four sources, each looking perfectly professional — titles, authors, reference codes, the lot.

Two of them were real. Two of them were completely invented. The authors did not exist. The reference codes led nowhere. The AI had simply made them up, because inventing a convincing-looking source is exactly the kind of thing these tools do.

Now, how did the student catch it? Every real scientific study has something called a DOI — think of it as a permanent web address that points to that one exact paper. You can paste that DOI into a free, trusted index called Crossref, and it will either show you the real paper or come up empty. The student checked all four DOIs. Two led to real papers. Two came up empty. That is how you catch a hallucination — that is the word for when an AI confidently makes something up. The lesson for your whole project: never, ever cite a source an AI gave you until you have opened it and confirmed it is real, with your own eyes.

Slide 21The five rules for using AI

So let me gather all of that into five simple rules for using AI in this project. Rule one: AI is part of the method — use it to search, to brainstorm, to explain, to read data, to check your work — and record every use in your journal. Rule two: the writing is yours — the AI can comment on a draft, but it does not write the essay for you. Rule three: verify every source before you cite it — open the link, check the DOI. Rule four: cross-check what matters — if a claim is important, check it against a second AI tool and against a real source, not just one. And rule five, which is about keeping you safe: no personal data in your prompts — never type your home address, never type classmates' names, never upload photos of people. Treat an AI chat box as if it were public.

Follow those five, and you can use these tools as much as you like, with a completely clear conscience.

Slide 22Do this now · your first journal entry

Here is your second task, and it is a short one — about five minutes — but it starts a habit you will use all term. Open any AI assistant you like, and ask it this exact question: "What are the three most serious environmental problems in" — and then your city — "with a source for each?"

Read what it gives you. Then open the Journal page on the website and save this as your very first entry: the prompt you used, the answer it gave, and — this is the interesting part — whether the sources it named are actually real. Click on them. See if they exist. You may be surprised. And whatever you find, that is a perfect first journal entry, because it is exactly the kind of checking you will be doing all project long.

Slide 23Before Session 2

Welcome back — and well done, because you have now done real project work, not just listened. Before I set your task for next time, let me answer the three worries I hear most, because one of them is probably in your head right now. The first worry: "What if there is no data for my exact street?" Then use the nearest place that does have data — a bigger road, a nearby district, a similar city — say openly that that is what you have done, and your report is actually stronger for being honest about it. The second worry: "Which AI should I use?" Any of them — Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity. In fact, using two of them and comparing their answers is better than trusting just one. The third worry: "What if I am not good at writing?" You do not have to be a brilliant writer. You have to be clear and honest, and you have four months and three rounds of my feedback to get there. Nobody expects a perfect first essay — that is exactly why we hand in drafts.

So, before our next session, on Friday the thirteenth of November, I would like you to bring three things: your chosen problem written out in a single clear sentence; three numbers about it, each with a source; and at least two entries in your journal.

And let me tell you what is coming, so you are curious rather than nervous. Session two is all about how AI actually works on the inside — why it is so fluent, why it invents sources the way it did in the Milan story, and how you can investigate with it without ever being fooled. And session three turns the question around and looks at AI's own environmental footprint — because these tools are not free to the planet. Just one number to leave you with: a short conversation with a large AI model can use around half a litre of water, just for cooling the computers, for every ten to fifty replies it gives you. That is from a 2025 study by Li and colleagues, and we will unpack what it really means in session three.
