Keep slide 1 up while students join. Ask them to open the Session 2 page in a second tab.
Slide 2What this session gives you
  • They will see what an AI assistant actually does inside when it answers.
  • They will learn why it sometimes invents a study, and why the fake looks convincing.
  • They will see how search assistants find their sources, and what still goes wrong.
  • They will learn which of the four tools fits which job.
  • They will write an investigation prompt in five parts.
  • They will learn where real numbers come from and how to record one.
  • Tell them there are short activities on the Session 2 page, so the page stays open.
Slide 3Why this session comes now
  • The Phase 2 essay asks them to investigate their problem: how big it is, what causes it, what the data say.
  • They will use AI to do it, so they must know how an AI answer is made before they trust it.
  • Use the comparison: you trust a weather forecast because it comes from measurements, not from a neighbour's feeling.
  • Name the four habits: the right tool, a precise prompt, a checked source, a real number.
Slide 4Do this now: your problem in one sentence
  • Ask for one sentence with three things: what the problem is, where it is, and which number shows it.
  • Read the example: fine dust (PM2.5) at three school gates in South Delhi, far above the WHO guideline on winter mornings.
  • Point out the what (fine dust), the where (three school gates) and the number (the level against the guideline).
  • Live: they type it in the Zoom chat. Recording: they write it down.
  • Reassure them: a sentence with no place or no number yet is what today fixes.
⏸ Pause: one minute to write the sentence.
Live: read two or three sentences from the chat aloud. For a vague one, ask "where exactly?" or "which number?".
Slide 5Part 1: what a language model does
  • Define it: Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini are large language models, programs trained on huge amounts of text to continue a text in a likely way.
  • Explain "large": the model has read an enormous amount and has billions of internal settings.
  • Say the key sentence slowly: the assistant writes its answer one small piece at a time, by predicting what comes next.
  • Stress that it does not look the answer up in a book of facts; it predicts. Everything else today follows from this.
Slide 6Tokens
  • The model cuts text into tokens: pieces it treats as one unit.
  • A token can be a whole word ("canals"), part of a word, or a punctuation mark.
  • Show the example: "Bangkok's" may be two tokens, "Bangkok" and "'s"; the cut depends on the model.
  • Each model has a fixed list of tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand tokens; everything it reads and writes is built from that list.
Slide 7Training
  • Describe training as a guessing game played billions of times.
  • The model reads a text up to a point and guesses the next token.
  • It is scored against the real next token, and its parameters are adjusted a little.
  • Define parameters: the billions of numbers inside the model, like tiny dials.
  • Training turns the dials trillions of times; nobody sets them by hand.
Slide 8Fine-tuning
  • After the first training, the model continues text well but is not yet a helpful assistant; it might answer a question with more questions.
  • A second training, fine-tuning, uses examples of good conversations.
  • People, and sometimes other AI models, rate which of two answers is better, and the model learns from the ratings.
  • That is why it now answers the question instead of continuing the sentence.
Slide 9Answering: the probabilities
  • The question is turned into tokens; for the next token, every token in the list gets a probability.
  • Read the example: "In November the air in Delhi is often…"
  • Read the bars: polluted 34 %, hazy 22 %, smoggy 17 %, unhealthy 12 %, cold 8 %, beautiful 4 %, purple 3 %.
  • Say clearly that these numbers are invented for the demonstration; the mechanism is real.
  • One token is picked and added, then the step runs again, hundreds of times for one answer.
Slide 10Temperature
  • Define temperature: the setting that decides how often the model takes a less likely token.
  • At low temperature (0.3), "polluted" rises to about 73 % and "purple" is almost 0 %: answers are predictable.
  • At high temperature (1.8), "polluted" falls to about 25 % and "purple" rises to about 6 %: answers vary and odd words appear.
  • Send them to the Session 2 page, Block 1, to move the slider and pick the next token 20 times.
Slide 11Four facts that change how you use it
  • It writes what is probable, not what is checked; nothing inside checks the facts.
  • It has a training cut-off: without a web search it knows nothing after the date its texts end (example: last month's flood in their city).
  • It has a context window: the most text it can hold at once; in a very long conversation the oldest parts drop out.
  • The same question can get two different answers; two different numbers are a warning sign to check.
Slide 12Part 2: why a model invents a study
  • Define hallucination: the model states something false in the same confident tone as something true.
  • A model can write a reference to a study that does not exist, and it looks exactly like a real one.
  • Recall the Milan story from Session 1: four sources, two real, two invented. Now they will see why.
Slide 13The anatomy of a reference
  • A reference has a regular shape: authors, year, title, journal, volume and pages, DOI.
  • Define DOI: a code that points to one exact study; "https://doi.org/" in front of it opens the study.
  • The model has seen millions of references, so it writes the shape perfectly.
  • Walk the table: common surnames; a title built from the words of their question; a real journal; the publisher's real prefix (10.1016 Elsevier, 10.1038 Nature) and a plausible number.
  • If the model knows the real paper, it may give it; if not, it still writes something reference-shaped, and nothing checks that the paper exists.
Slide 14Two kinds of invented reference
  • The easy kind: doi.org says "DOI not found", and Crossref has no such title.
  • Define Crossref: a free index of published research at search.crossref.org.
  • The dangerous kind: the link opens a real paper, but on another topic by other authors.
  • The rule: compare the authors and the title with what opens, not only whether something opens.
Slide 15Part 3: assistants that search the web
  • Perplexity always searches first; Gemini and ChatGPT do when they use web search.
  • Walk the four stages: rewrite the question into searches; search and take the top results; read and rank passages; write the answer with a citation number on each sentence.
  • Define retrieval-augmented generation: the model first retrieves (finds) real texts, then writes from them.
Slide 16What can still go wrong
  • The citations now point to pages that exist; that is real progress.
  • Search: the top result may be a blog or news summary; follow it to the original.
  • Read: a number from another city or year may be used; find it on the page and check place, year and unit.
  • Write: sources may be blended, or a citation may not support the sentence; read the cited passage.
  • The source: it may be old, wrong or one-sided; ask who published it, when, with what data.
  • The rule: check each citation against what the page really says.
Slide 17Part 4: four tools, four strengths
  • All four are free to start with, and all four can be wrong.
  • Claude: long, careful reasoning; structuring and editing a report.
  • ChatGPT: brainstorming and fast first drafts.
  • Gemini: recent information, images and maps; connected to Google Search.
  • Perplexity: web research with clickable sources; fact-checking.
  • The tools change every few months, so they should test them on their own problem.
  • The firm rule: check anything important with a second AI and a real source.
Slide 18Do this now: match the tools
  • Send them to the Session 2 page, Block 4.
  • For six tasks they pick a tool in the drop-down list; the page says at once if it is the recommended one.

Answers: papers and data, Perplexity; brainstorm, ChatGPT; structure the report, Claude; read a photo or map, Gemini; fact-check another AI, Perplexity + Claude; polish the draft, Claude.
Slide 19Do this now: the verification lab
  • Send them to the Session 2 page, Block 5: four references, as an AI might give them.
  • Tell them two are real and two were invented in the way a model invents them.
  • For each, they open the DOI at doi.org or search the title on Crossref, then compare authors and title.
  • They press Real or Not real; the page explains.
  • They decide all four before looking at the answers.
⏸ Pause: about 10 minutes.
Live: give real quiet time. Do not reveal early. Then ask who found the trap.
Slide 20The answers
  • 1, Nearing et al. (2024), floods, Nature: real. It is Google's flood-forecasting model, back in Session 3.
  • 2, "Sharma & Okafor (2023)", Delhi school gates: invented. The DOI opens a different real paper, Jeon et al. (2023) on dust in Seoul.
  • Stress number 2: anyone who only checked that the link opened was fooled. This is the main lesson of the lab.
  • 3, Li, Yang, Islam & Ren (2025), AI's water use, Communications of the ACM: real. Its numbers come back in Session 3.
  • 4, "Nguyen & Berger (2022)", Bangkok canals: invented. doi.org says "DOI not found"; the journal and the 10.1038 prefix are real, which is why it looks believable.
  • Say all four were checked on Crossref on 13 November 2026. Ask how many they got right.
Slide 21Part 5: an investigation prompt
  • A vague question gets a general answer; a precise one with context and limits gets something they can check.
  • Define prompt: the text you type to the AI.
  • Role: who should answer ("an environmental scientist who works with city air data").
  • Context: their city and problem in one or two sentences.
  • Task: exactly what they want ("list the public datasets that measure…").
  • Constraints: how it must answer ("only sources with a link; say when you are not sure").
  • Format: the shape of the answer ("a table: source, what it measures, years, link").
Slide 22One example
  • Read the full prompt on the slide aloud.
  • Ask them to find the five parts: the scientist (role), the student in Delhi and the school-gate dust (context), the list of datasets (task), working links and "not sure" (constraints), the table (format).
  • Compare with the vague version, "Tell me about pollution in Delhi": a general essay with no checkable sources, from the same tool.
Slide 23Do this now: your own prompt
  • Send them to the Session 2 page, Block 6: five boxes, one per part; the page assembles the prompt.
  • They run it on Perplexity and on one other assistant, and compare the sources.
  • A source found by both is a good start, but they still open and check it.
  • They log both runs in the AI Process Journal (the notebook button): which tool, what they asked, what they got.
⏸ Pause: about 10 minutes.
Live: ask one or two students to read their prompt aloud and name its five parts.
Slide 24Part 6: where real numbers come from
  • The rule: an AI helps find data; the number in the essay comes from the organisation that measured it.
  • Air: OpenAQ (measurements from air monitors worldwide) and WHO air-pollution data.
  • Waste: World Bank "What a Waste" and the city's own environmental report.
  • Water: WRI Aqueduct, with water stress, drought and flood risk on a map.
  • Heat and land: Copernicus Climate Change Service and NASA Earthdata (temperatures, satellite images).
  • Forests: Global Forest Watch. Plants and animals: GBIF and iNaturalist.
  • They pick the one or two sources that fit their problem; all links are in Block 7.
Slide 25A data card
  • Every number needs five things before it goes into the essay; together they form a data card.
  • Value: 178 µg/m³, which means 178 millionths of a gram of dust in each cubic metre of air.
  • Of what: the average PM2.5 in Delhi's air.
  • Year: winter 2024.
  • Source: the Centre for Science and Environment, with a link.
  • Checked: the number was found in the Centre's own report, not only in a news article.
  • If one of the five is missing, the number is not ready. They fill in their first card in Block 7.
Slide 26The Phase 2 essay
  • The essay is 1,500 words, due in the week of 4–8 January 2027, and it investigates the question Phase 1 ended on.
  • About 150 words: the question, made sharper.
  • About 400 words: data and sources, how each was checked, which tool did what.
  • About 450 words: what the data show, compared with a guideline, another city or another year, with a table or chart.
  • About 300 words: the science, why it happens, in their own words.
  • About 200 words: the limits, what the data cannot tell and where sources disagree.
  • The AI Process Journal goes at the end as the appendix.
  • Tip: a strong essay shows one place where an AI was wrong and how they found out.
