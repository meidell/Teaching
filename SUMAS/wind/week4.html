Strategic forecasting and scenario planning

The midpoint of your consulting project. In Weeks 2 and 3 you built a quantitative foundation — units, primary data, a learning curve, three trajectories to 2040. Useful work. But if you stopped there you would be doing what most analysts do: extrapolating the past and calling it strategy. This week you make the leap that defines the profession: from forecasting (one number, with error bars) to scenario planning (multiple coherent futures, each requiring different actions).

Why this matters — a cautionary tale

In late 2023, Ørsted took roughly $4 billion in impairments and cancelled its Ocean Wind 1 and 2 projects in New Jersey. The company had excellent forecasts. What it lacked was a serious scenario in which interest rates rose 400 basis points, steel prices doubled, supply chains seized, and US offshore permitting slipped — all at once. That scenario was visible in 2021. But nobody at Ørsted had written it down, given it a name, and tested the strategy against it.

Your job this week is to build the kind of scenarios that would have caught Ørsted's 2023 collapse — so your Phase 1 analysis is more rigorous than the consulting work that missed it.

By the end of this week you will be able to

  • Distinguish forecasting (point estimates) from scenario planning (multiple plausible futures)
  • Apply Schoemaker's scenario-planning method to wind energy in Europe
  • Identify two critical uncertainties from a longer list using the impact × uncertainty matrix
  • Construct a 2×2 scenario matrix with four distinct, internally consistent futures
  • Stress-test Ørsted's and TotalEnergies' strategies across all four
  • Recognise what makes a strategy robust vs scenario-specific

This week: no required forum posts or hand-ins. The exercises below are self-paced — they produce the raw material you will integrate into your Phase 1 deliverable next week. Treat them as personal consulting prep, not assignments.

Core concepts · 1

Forecasting vs scenario planning — the crucial distinction

ForecastingScenario planning
AsksWhat is the most likely future?What futures must we be ready for?
OutputOne number, with error bars3–4 internally consistent narratives
Best forStable systems, 1–3 year horizonsUnstable systems, 10–30 year horizons
RiskOverconfidence; black swansVague stories that don't drive decisions

Your Week 3 LCOE projections were forecasts. They are inputs to scenarios — not substitutes for them.

A common mistake (and the first pitfall Schoemaker warns about): producing three scenarios labelled best / base / worst along a single axis. That is not scenario planning. That is a sensitivity analysis dressed up. Real scenarios differ on at least two axes — and the four resulting worlds should look qualitatively different, not just numerically different.

2 · Schoemaker's 10-step process, compressed

Define the scope — geography, time horizon, decision being supported
Identify major stakeholders — you did this in Week 1
Identify basic trends — predictable forces: Wright's Law cost decline, EU population ageing, electrification
Identify key uncertainties — unpredictable forces with high impact
Construct initial scenario themes — the 2×2 matrix
Check for consistency and plausibility — kill incoherent scenarios
Develop learning scenarios — refine narratives, add quantitative anchors
Identify research needs → Phase 2
Develop quantitative models → Phase 2
Evolve toward decision scenarios → Phase 3

For your capstone you do steps 1–7 deeply this week. Steps 8–10 connect to Phases 2 and 3.

Core concepts · 3

The impact × uncertainty matrix

You probably already have 15–20 forces shaping EU wind to 2040 (from Weeks 1–3). You cannot build scenarios on all of them. You need the 2 most critical. Plot each force on X: predictability (low → high) and Y: impact on your strategic question (low → high). The top-left quadrant — high impact, low predictability — is where your scenario axes live.

Score it yourself · ten forces, two sliders each

The starting scores are the briefing's own (High/Medium/Low). Move them to where you think they belong — the point of the exercise is the argument you have with the defaults. Your top two become the axes in Exercise 2.

Core concepts · 4

The 2×2 scenario matrix — a worked example

Once you have two critical uncertainties, plot them as orthogonal axes. The four quadrants are your four scenarios. Example axes — X: EU climate-policy ambition (weak ↔ strong); Y: cost-decline trajectory (slow ↔ fast):

▲ fast cost decline
Market-Driven GreeningWeak policy, fast cost decline. Wind wins on price alone; deployment is lumpy and follows corporate PPAs rather than auctions.
Renewable RenaissanceStrong policy, fast cost decline. Both engines fire; the binding constraint becomes execution — vessels, ports, people.
Stalled TransitionWeak policy, slow cost decline. Cancellations, deferrals, gas as the default; the pure-play is in trouble.
Subsidised TransitionStrong policy, slow cost decline. Wind is built because the state wants it; margins depend on CfD design.
◀ weak policy  ·  strong policy ▶     ▼ slow cost decline

Each scenario needs

  • A memorable name — not "Scenario A" or "high / low growth"
  • A one-paragraph narrative describing the world in 2040
  • 3–5 quantitative anchors — LCOE, wind capacity in GW, wind share of the mix, gas price, carbon price
  • 2–3 early signals to watch for that would tell you this scenario is unfolding

A scenario that is just "things go well" or "things go badly" is not a scenario. It is a mood.

Last year's cohort chose different axes — and that is instructiveTheir matrix crossed policy & permitting with supply-chain resilience and gave Green Acceleration (25–30%), Policy-Led Push (30–35%, their base case: reform unlocks pipelines but turbines and vessels are the limit), Supply Chain Advantage (20–25%) and Stalled Transition (10–15%). They rejected cost/LCOE as an axis because it is an output of policy and supply chain, not an independent driver. Read their reasoning on the showcase, then disagree with it in writing. The probabilities, by the way, are a judgment call they chose to make explicit — Schoemaker would say that is optional, Wack would say it is dangerous. Decide where you stand.
Core concepts · 5 and 6

Stress-testing strategies across scenarios

For each of your four scenarios, ask of Ørsted and TotalEnergies: thrive, survive, struggle, or fail?

RenaissanceSubsidisedMarket-DrivenStalled
Ørsted????
TotalEnergies????

The key concept: a strategy is robust if it succeeds (or at least survives) in 3+ of 4 scenarios. A strategy is scenario-specific if it succeeds only in 1–2 — and is therefore a bet, not a strategy.

Ørsted's pure-play offshore strategy is a bet on the "Renewable Renaissance" world. TotalEnergies' hedged strategy is a bet that no single scenario dominates — and therefore optionality has value. Which kind of bet should the client make? That's the consulting question.

Common pitfalls (Schoemaker, p. 39)

  • Too many scenarios — stop at 4. Six are unmanageable.
  • Too few — never just 2 ("optimistic vs pessimistic"). That is binary, not strategic.
  • Internally inconsistent scenarios — "high carbon price + low policy ambition" makes no sense; one drives the other.
  • All scenarios are variations of "things continue" — the most likely scenario is rarely the most useful one for testing strategy.
  • Forgetting to update — scenarios decay. Use them as living tools, revisited annually.
  • Stopping at scenarios — scenarios without strategy testing are just stories.

Self-paced preparation exercises · the spine of Phase 1

Three exercises, written into your file

There are no required posts or hand-ins this week. These exercises produce the raw material you integrate directly into Phase 1, which opens Monday of Week 5. Treat them as building blocks. The fields save as you type and I can read them — which means I can flag a correlated pair of axes before you build a report on it.

Exercise 1 — Identify your critical uncertainties

List 6–8 forces shaping European wind to 2040, drawing on your Week 1 stakeholder map and your Week 3 risk analysis. Score each for impact on your strategic question (1–5) and uncertainty (1–5). Identify your top 2. A useful self-check: are your top 2 truly uncorrelated? If "EU policy ambition" and "carbon price" are both high-impact and high-uncertainty but one drives the other, they do not make good orthogonal axes — pick one and replace the other.

AI prompt that works well: "Help me distinguish between forces that are predictable trends (e.g. continuing offshore wind cost decline due to Wright's Law) and forces that are genuinely uncertain (e.g. EU climate policy ambition post-2030). For each item on my list, classify it as a trend or an uncertainty, and explain why."

Exercise 2 — Build your 2×2 scenario matrix

This is the central piece of work for the week. Using your two critical uncertainties: name your two axes; name your four scenarios (memorable, evocative — "Renewable Renaissance", "Stalled Transition", not "high/low growth"); write a 100-word narrative per scenario describing Europe in 2040; provide 3 quantitative anchors per scenario (wind share of EU electricity in 2040, offshore wind LCOE in 2040, one more of your choice); identify 2 early signals per scenario — events or data points in 2026–2030 that would tell you this scenario is unfolding.

Use AI to stress-test internal consistency: "Here are my four scenarios with their narratives and quantitative anchors. Are they internally consistent? Where do they overlap? Which one is least plausible and why? Are my axes truly orthogonal, or do they secretly correlate?"

Benchmarks to compare against — read these after you have drafted yours, so you don't anchor: IEA WEO 2024 (STEPS, APS, NZE) · Shell Energy Security Scenarios · BloombergNEF NEO 2025 (executive summary public) · DNV ETO 2025 — Europe. How do yours differ? If they don't differ at all, you have copied. If they differ wildly, you may have lost touch with reality. Either is informative.

Exercise 3 — Stress-test Ørsted vs TotalEnergies

For each of your four scenarios: does Ørsted thrive / survive / struggle / fail? Does TotalEnergies? Which scenarios favour which company? Is there any scenario where both fail? Where both thrive? Format: a 2-row × 4-column table plus a short commentary on which company's strategy looks more robust across scenarios. This is a direct preview of Phase 3. Doing it now — even roughly — clarifies what your Phase 1 system map and Phase 2 data analysis need to support.

The forum stays open. If you want to share your 2×2 or your stress-test table for informal feedback from classmates, post it — there is no requirement and no grading. Useful especially if your top-2 uncertainties differ sharply from what others choose: that divergence is exactly the kind of conversation that sharpens consulting judgment. I read it and comment.
