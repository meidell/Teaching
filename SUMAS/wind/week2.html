Core concepts · 1

Understanding energy units

You must master these to analyse the market.

Power (capacity) = how much can be generated at one moment

  • kW (kilowatt) = 1,000 W → powers a household
  • MW (megawatt) = 1,000 kW → one large wind turbine (10–15 MW offshore)
  • GW (gigawatt) = 1,000 MW → a large power plant, or a region's capacity

Energy (generation) = power × time

  • kWh = running 1 kW for 1 hour
  • MWh = 1,000 kWh  ·  GWh = 1,000 MWh
  • TWh = 1,000 GWh → country-level annual consumption

Example from your PDFs: EU industry consumes ~400 TWh/year of final energy as gas-equivalent demand in the briefing's scope; gas provides 40% ≈ 160 TWh. If you want to replace that gas with wind, you need the conversion below.

The one calculation you will do fifty times

Practice question from the week: if 1 GW of offshore wind runs at a 50% capacity factor for a year, how many TWh does it produce? 1 GW × 0.5 × 8,760 h = 4,380 GWh = 4.38 TWh. Now try Europe's 33 GW of offshore at 50%.

Core concepts · 2

Capacity factor — the critical metric

Capacity factor = actual energy produced ÷ maximum possible energy. Why it matters: 1 GW of wind ≠ 1 GW of coal or nuclear.

Typical capacity factors
Offshore wind45–50%
Onshore wind25–35%
Solar PV15–20%
Nuclear85–90%
Coal / gas50–70%

Good offshore sites hit 55–60%. Coal and gas can run higher; they are limited by demand and economics, not by the weather.

What this means for your analysis: to replace 10 GW of nuclear with wind you need roughly 18–20 GW of wind capacity. Offshore beats onshore because its capacity factor is higher — which is exactly why Ørsted's pure-offshore strategy and TotalEnergies' mixed portfolio are different bets, not just different sizes.

Core concepts · 3

LCOE — levelised cost of energy

LCOE = total lifetime cost ÷ total lifetime energy produced. Simplified: LCOE = (CAPEX + OPEX) / total MWh produced over the asset's life, both discounted.

From your PDF (page 7): offshore wind LCOE fell from ~$188/MWh (2010) to ~$86/MWh (2024). Solar fell even more dramatically. Gas sits around $90/MWh but is volatile — fuel-price risk.

Check the arithmetic before you quote itThe briefing calls 188 → 86 "a 70% cost reduction in 14 years". It is a 54% reduction (1 − 86/188). IRENA's own global weighted-average series, $203 → $75/MWh from 2010 to 2023, is a 63% fall. Neither is 70%. This is not a trick: every source rounds, and a consultant who re-does the division catches it. Do it for every number you quote this term.

Why this matters for 2040

  • If LCOE keeps falling, wind becomes cheaper than all alternatives
  • Wright's Law (page 6 of your PDF): every doubling of cumulative production → ~11–15% cost reduction
  • This creates a reinforcing feedback loop: lower LCOE → more competitive → more deployment → more scale → lower LCOE…

Strategic question: will this loop continue to 2040, or will it hit limits? Week 3 fits the curve; Week 7 asks where it breaks.

Wide ranges reflect grid distance, seabed conditions and supply-chain access. Floating wind is still structurally pricier. Note the units: €/MWh here, $/MWh above — one more thing to say out loud every time.

In some countries the share is much higher — Denmark ~60%, Ireland ~35%. The EU target of 500+ GW by 2030 is more than double today's capacity; offshore is expected to grow fastest, to ~100–120 GW by 2030.

Demand by sector · 2024 → 2040 (TWh) · from the previous cohort's briefing, IEA WEO 2024 and European Commission

Europe's electricity demand is set to rise about 66% by 2040 — from roughly 2,730 TWh to 4,530 TWh — not because Europe wastes more energy, but because it deliberately electrifies sectors that used to burn fossil fuels. On the supply side the whole mix is restructured: renewables from ~48% to ~83% of electricity, and offshore wind from ~3% of EU generation to ~18% — roughly a tenfold increase in output. That is why this capstone focuses on offshore.

Your consulting task: is this trajectory realistic? What would it take to reach it? What could stop it?

Using AI for market research

AI as your research assistant

This week you use AI to find and analyse wind energy data. Recommended tools, all free: Claude.ai, ChatGPT, Perplexity.ai (good for finding sources).

For data discovery

  • "What is the current installed offshore wind capacity in Europe? Give me the most recent data with sources."
  • "Where can I find historical data on European wind energy generation by country?"
  • "What are the most authoritative sources for wind energy statistics in Europe?"

For understanding

  • "Explain capacity factor and why it matters for comparing wind to other energy sources."
  • "If Europe has 33 GW of offshore wind at 50% capacity factor, how much electricity does that produce annually in TWh?" — then check it against the calculator above.

For analysis

  • "Based on current growth rates, what would EU offshore wind capacity be in 2040? Show your calculation."
  • "What are the main bottlenecks limiting faster wind energy deployment in Europe?"
Critical: always verify AI outputs. Check that sources are real (click the links). Cross-reference numbers across several sources. Use AI to find data; you verify its accuracy. Week 3 makes this an activity of its own.
Activities · this week's forums

Two required posts, one open debate

Find and share three specific data points about European wind energy. For each one:

  1. Metric & value — e.g. "EU offshore wind capacity = 33 GW (2024)"
  2. Source & URL — e.g. WindEurope Statistics 2024
  3. Why it matters — e.g. "only 15% of total EU wind capacity: the offshore growth potential is huge"
  4. Quality assessment — ✓ authoritative · ⚠ industry estimate · ✗ unclear

Use AI to help find sources: document which tool you used, whether it gave you good sources or bad ones, and what you had to verify yourself. Then reply to two classmates: did you find different numbers for the same metric? Share additional sources; help verify each other's data quality.

Compare one specific metric between Ørsted and TotalEnergies. Choose one: operational renewable capacity (GW) · annual renewable investment (€) · renewable energy as % of total revenue · market capitalisation · offshore wind project pipeline · cost of capital / debt levels.

  1. Find current data for both companies — annual reports, investor presentations, financial databases
  2. Compare — which company is ahead? by how much? is the gap widening or narrowing?
  3. Analyse — what does this metric reveal about their strategies? which company does it favour for 2040? and what does it not tell you?

AI prompt you could try: "Compare Ørsted and TotalEnergies on [your metric]. Help me find their most recent reported numbers and explain what this comparison reveals about their strategies." Document your AI use and sources. Reply to two peers on whether their metric is more or less important than yours for predicting 2040 outcomes.

Based on what you have learned, make a preliminary prediction: "By 2040, wind energy will provide ___% of EU electricity because…" Support it with the 2024 baseline, the growth trajectory needed, one key enabler (what has to go right) and one key risk (what could stop it).

This is speculative — that is fine. You are building intuition; Weeks 7–8 refine it with real analysis. Let's see the range of predictions the class produces.
