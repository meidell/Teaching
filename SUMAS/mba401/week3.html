Section 3.2 · The probability rules you need

Chances from counting

A two-way table

Last month: 400 delivery orders, counted two ways.

Every order is classified by what was ordered and where it went. When every order is equally likely to be picked, a probability is simply a count divided by 400. Choose an event A (a row) and an event B (a column) and read off every rule of chapter 3.

The five rules, in one place

Everything in Part 2's first two exercises is one of these.

P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A)  ·  P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)  ·  P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(B)
  • Complement Aᶜ — "not A". Often the fastest route: P(at least one) = 1 − P(none).
  • Union A ∪ B — "A or B or both". Subtract the overlap, or you count it twice.
  • Intersection A ∩ B — "A and B". A single cell of the table.
  • Conditional P(A | B) — "A, given that B happened": shrink the table to column B and count again.
  • Mutually exclusive: A ∩ B is empty — they cannot both happen. Independent: knowing B does not change the chance of A, P(A | B) = P(A), or equivalently P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B).

Mutually exclusive and independent are not the same thing — they are nearly opposites. If A and B cannot happen together, learning that B happened tells you A did not.

Section 3.3 · Random variables

A number attached to chance

Definition

A random variable assigns a number to each outcome of a chance experiment.

"How many Margheritas will Friday bring?" has an answer only after Friday. Before it, the count is a random variable — written with a capital letter, X, while a particular value it takes is a lower-case x.

  • Discrete — the possible values can be listed: 0, 1, 2, 3… You count them. Number of orders, of no-shows, of late deliveries.
  • Continuous — the possible values fill a whole interval. You measure them. Weight of dough, oven temperature, waiting time.

Count → discrete. Measure → continuous. This week is discrete; Week 4 is continuous.

Two traps. A count can be huge and still be discrete — the number of kernels in a bag of popcorn. And money is treated as continuous when it is an amount (the average spent on electricity), because it can take essentially any value in a range.

Section 3.4 · Probability distributions

The whole table of chances

Two rules, then two numbers

A probability distribution lists every value x and its probability P(x).

For it to be a valid distribution: every P(x) is between 0 and 1, and the P(x) add up to exactly 1. Nothing else is required — the values x need not be whole numbers.

µ = Σ x · P(x)   ·   σ² = Σ (x − µ)² · P(x) = Σ x² · P(x) − µ²   ·   σ = √σ²

The mean µ is a weighted average: each value weighted by how often it happens. The standard deviation σ is the same idea as in Week 2 — the typical distance from the mean — with probabilities in place of 1/n.

Section 3.5 · Expected value

What is a bet worth?

The mean, with money on it

E(X) is what you gain per play, on average, over many plays.

When X is a net gain — winnings minus the price of playing — its mean has a special name, the expected value E(X). It is not a value you expect to see on any single play; it is the long-run average. Insurers, lotteries and casinos set prices by it.

The pizzeria's oven costs CHF 12,000 to replace. An insurer estimates the chance it breaks down within a year at 3%, and sells a one-year policy for a premium C. For the insurer, X takes two values:

x (insurer's net gain)CC − 12,000
P(x)0.970.03
Set the premium

E(X) = C − 360. Below CHF 360 the insurer loses money on average; everything above it pays for staff, risk and profit.

The trap

Build the table from net gains, and check it sums to 1.

A lottery ticket costs $1 and the prize is $1,000: the winner's net gain is $999, not $1,000, and everybody else's is −$1. Forgetting to subtract the stake is the most common mistake in Exercises 5 and 6 of Part 2 — and it moves the answer by exactly the price of the ticket.

Section 3.6 · The binomial distribution

Ten tables, each 30% likely to order dessert

Four conditions

X counts successes in n trials. It is binomial when all four hold:

  1. A fixed number n of trials.
  2. Each trial has two outcomes: success or failure.
  3. The probability of success p is the same on every trial.
  4. The trials are independent.
P(X = k) = C(n, k) · pᵏ · qⁿ⁻ᵏ    with q = 1 − p and C(n, k) = n! ÷ (k! (n − k)!)
µ = np   ·   σ = √(npq)

Read the formula left to right: C(n, k) counts the orders in which k successes can fall among n trials; pᵏ is the chance of k successes; qⁿ⁻ᵏ the chance that the other n − k fail. Ten tables, each ordering dessert with p = 0.3: P(exactly 3) = C(10, 3) · 0.3³ · 0.7⁷ = 120 · 0.027 · 0.0824 = 0.2668.
