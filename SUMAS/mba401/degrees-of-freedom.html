Degrees of freedom: how many of your numbers are still free to vary

Degrees of freedom (written df) is the number of values in a calculation that you can still choose freely once the conditions they must meet are fixed. Almost every formula from Week 1B onwards contains a df: the n − 1 of the sample standard deviation, the df row of the t table, the n − 2 of regression, the (rows − 1)(columns − 1) of chi-square. This page shows where each of those counts comes from. Work through the six sections in order. In each one you change the numbers yourself and the page counts the free values for you.

Where you meet degrees of freedom in this course
  • Week 1B. The sample standard deviation divides by n − 1, not by n. Section 2 below shows why.
  • Weeks 3 and 4. A confidence interval or a test with Student's t reads its critical value from the row df = n − 1 of the t table. With two samples pooled (Week 4B) the row is n₁ + n₂ − 2. Section 5 shows what that row changes.
  • Week 5. Regression measures the scatter around the line with n − 2 degrees of freedom. Section 3 shows why two points tell you nothing about scatter.
  • Week 6. A chi-square test on a table uses (I − 1)(J − 1), where I is the number of rows and J the number of columns, and one-way ANOVA uses K − 1 and n − K, where K is the number of groups. Section 4 lets you count both.

Section 1 · The definition

Once the average is fixed, the last day's sales are no longer yours to choose

Why this comes first. Every df in this course is the same count: how many values were free, minus how many conditions the calculation has already fixed. This section uses nothing but an average, so you can see that count before any formula appears.
Two definitions

A constraint is a condition that the values must satisfy, for example "the average of the five days must be CHF 400".

Degrees of freedom is the number of values you can still choose freely once the constraints are fixed. Each constraint removes one free choice. So the count is: degrees of freedom = number of values − number of constraints.

  2. Move the slider of each day except the last one. You choose those figures freely.
  3. Watch the last day. The page computes it, and it carries a lock, because once the average is fixed the last figure can take only one value.

Section 2 · Week 1B

The sample variance divides by n − 1 because only n − 1 deviations are free

Why you need this. In Week 1B the sample standard deviation is s = √( Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1) ). Students lose points on the exam when they divide by n instead. This section shows the two reasons for n − 1: the deviations are tied together, and dividing by n gives an answer that is too small on average.
Two terms used below

A deviation is the distance of one value from the sample mean x̄: the value minus x̄. A value above the mean has a positive deviation, a value below it a negative one.

The sample variance s² is the sum of the squared deviations divided by n − 1. The sample standard deviation s is its square root.

Part A. The deviations from the sample mean always add up to zero

Below are the prices of a Margherita at five Geneva pizzerias. Move any price. The chart shows each price's deviation from the mean of the five. Look at the sum under the chart: it stays at zero whatever you do.

The sum is zero because x̄ was computed from these same five prices: the mean sits exactly at the balance point of the data. That is a constraint, and it costs one degree of freedom. Four deviations carry information; the fifth is a copy of the other four with the sign reversed. So the sample variance divides the sum of squared deviations by the number of free deviations, n − 1.

Part B. Dividing by n gives a variance that is too small on average, most of all in small samples

The second reason is about accuracy. A population is the whole group you want to know about; here it is every delivery the Pâquis branch made in October. Its true variance σ² is known, because the page holds all 400 delivery times. You then draw samples, small groups taken at random from it, and estimate σ² from each sample in two ways: dividing by n, and dividing by n − 1. Do these three things:

  1. Choose a sample size n.
  2. Press Draw 1 sample once and read the sample and its two estimates in the box below the buttons.
  3. Press Draw 1,000 samples and compare the two averages with the true variance in the bar chart.

Section 3 · Week 5

A regression line uses up two degrees of freedom, so the scatter is measured on n − 2

Why you need this. In Week 5 the regression printout gives the standard error of the line, se = √( SSE ÷ (n − 2) ), and the slope test reads the t table on n − 2 degrees of freedom. This section shows where the 2 comes from: the line has two numbers to fix, the intercept and the slope, and each one uses up one data point's worth of freedom.
Three terms used below

A regression line ŷ = b₀ + b₁x is the straight line that passes as close as possible to the points. b₀ is the intercept (the height of the line at x = 0) and b₁ is the slope (how much ŷ changes when x rises by 1).

A residual is the vertical distance from a point to the line: the actual y minus the y the line predicts. SSE (sum of squared errors) is the sum of the squared residuals.

Click in the chart to add pizzerias, and count the residuals that are free

Each dot is one pizzeria: the price of its Margherita (across) and the number of Margheritas it sells per day (up). The chart starts with two pizzerias. Do these three things:

  1. Look at the two starting points. The line passes through both of them, so both residuals are zero. Two points always fit a line exactly, so they say nothing about how far real pizzerias scatter around a line.
  2. Click anywhere in the chart to add a pizzeria. The page refits the line and draws each residual as a vertical segment.
  3. Read the count under the chart: n points, 2 numbers estimated, n − 2 residuals free.

Section 4 · Week 6

In a table with fixed totals, only (rows − 1) × (columns − 1) cells are free

Why you need this. In Week 6 the chi-square test for independence compares a table of counts with the counts you would expect if the two ways of sorting were unrelated. Its critical value comes from the chi-square table on (I − 1)(J − 1) degrees of freedom. This section shows that the totals of the rows and columns fix every other cell once you have chosen that many.
Two terms used below

A contingency table counts the same orders sorted two ways at once: here by branch (the rows) and by topping (the columns). Each cell holds the number of orders with that branch and that topping.

The row totals and column totals are fixed by the data: the expected counts of the chi-square test are computed from them, so the test treats them as known. Every row total and every column total is a constraint.

Fill the cells one by one, and watch the page fill the cells that the totals force

The totals come from Week 6's 180 orders of last month. Do these three things:

  1. Choose the table size: two branches or three.
  2. Type a count in any empty cell and press Tab or click elsewhere. When a row or a column has only one empty cell left, the page fills it from the total and locks it.
  3. Keep going until the table is full, then compare the number of cells you chose with (rows − 1) × (columns − 1).

Section 5 · Weeks 3 and 4

With fewer degrees of freedom the t curve has fatter tails and a larger critical value

Why you need this. In Weeks 3 and 4 you replace the unknown σ with the sample's s, and the critical value then comes from Student's t table instead of the normal table. You read that table in the row df = n − 1. This section shows what the row changes: the curve, and the number you put into the interval or the test.
Two terms used below

The t distribution is a bell-shaped curve centred on 0, like the standard normal curve, but with more area in its tails. There is one t curve for each number of degrees of freedom.

The 95 % two-tailed critical value t* is the number that leaves 2.5 % of the area in each tail, so 95 % of the area lies between −t* and +t*. A 95 % confidence interval is x̄ ± t* × s ÷ √n.

Move the df slider and read the critical value

The dashed curve is the standard normal curve; it does not move. The solid curve is the t curve for the df you choose. The shaded tails each hold 2.5 % of the area under the t curve. Move the slider, or press one of the buttons, and compare t* with the normal curve's 1.960.

Why fewer free values give a wider curve. With few degrees of freedom, s is computed from few free deviations, so it is a shaky estimate of σ. Sometimes s comes out too small, and then the t statistic comes out too large. The t curve allows for this by putting more area in its tails, which pushes t* outward and widens the interval. As df grows, s becomes a reliable estimate of σ, and the t curve becomes the normal curve.
