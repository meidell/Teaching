Week 7 · Quantitative Methods · SUMAS

Is Rive faster than Pâquis when one delivery took 95 minutes?

Every test of this course so far assumed that the data come from a roughly normal population: a bell-shaped spread of values with no extreme cases. Real business data often break that assumption. A sample is small and one value is extreme; a customer answers on a scale from 1 to 5, which is an order, not a measurement. This week has two parts. First, four non-parametric tests: tests that work on the ranks of the values (1st, 2nd, 3rd …) instead of the values themselves, so that one extreme value cannot dominate the result. Second, principal component analysis (PCA): a method that turns many related survey questions into a few scores, and the place where the statistics of this course meets what companies call machine learning.

The session is three hours. Part 1 is the lesson: each method computed by hand on the pizzeria's data, then run in numiqo, the free online statistics calculator that was called DataTab until it was renamed (datatab.net now opens numiqo.com). Part 2 is ten exercises worked together. The textbook does not cover this week, so every definition here is written for this page.

Section 7.1 · The hook

Sixteen deliveries, two branches, one 95-minute order

Your verdict

Last Friday evening the pizzeria timed eight deliveries from Rive and eight from Pâquis, in minutes from the order to the door.

One Pâquis driver was stuck behind a closed road and took 95 minutes. The manager wants to know whether Rive delivers faster in general, because the delivery promise on the website is the same for both branches. Look at the two rows and decide before any formula.

1 · your verdict: is Rive faster?
2 · what will the two-sample t-test of Week 4B say at the 5% level?

Why this matters for a manager. A t-test compares two means (averages). One extreme value moves a mean a long way: the Pâquis mean is 37.6 minutes, although seven of its eight deliveries took between 24 and 34. The same value also makes the Pâquis standard deviation about six times the Rive one, and a large standard deviation makes any difference look like chance. So the test that you learned answers a question about averages that one closed road has distorted. Section 7.2 shows the problem in numbers, and section 7.3 gives the test that answers the manager's question.

Section 7.2 · Ranks instead of values

When the assumptions of a test fail, rank the data

Learning ObjectivesWeek 7 · section 7.2
  1. To recognise the three situations in which a t-test or an ANOVA should not be trusted.
  2. To rank a sample, giving tied values the average of their positions.
  3. To name the non-parametric test that replaces each test of the semester.
Definitions · parametric and non-parametric testsWeek 7

A parametric test is a test that assumes the population has a known shape, usually the normal distribution, and tests a number that describes it, such as the mean μ. The t-tests of Weeks 4A and 4B and the ANOVA of Week 6 are parametric.

A non-parametric test is a test that makes no assumption about the shape of the population. Most non-parametric tests replace every value by its rank: its position when all the values are sorted from smallest to largest. The smallest value gets rank 1, the next rank 2, and so on.

A tie is two or more equal values. Tied values all get the average of the positions they occupy. If two values share positions 4 and 5, both get rank 4.5.

Three situations call for a non-parametric test: (1) a small sample (under about 30) from a population that is clearly not normal, for example skewed to one side; (2) a small sample with an outlier, a value far from all the others; (3) ordinal data, answers that have an order but no fixed distance between them, such as a rating from 1 to 5. The distance from "poor" to "fair" need not equal the distance from "good" to "excellent", so an average of ratings is hard to interpret, while their order is clear.

Key TakeawaysSection 7.2
  • A rank keeps the order of the values and forgets their distance, so an extreme value can only be ranked last: it cannot pull the result further.
  • Tied values get the average of the positions they share; the ranks of n values always add up to n(n + 1) ⁄ 2.
  • Two independent samples → Mann–Whitney; paired samples → Wilcoxon signed-rank; three or more groups → Kruskal–Wallis; a correlation → Spearman.
Section 7.3 · Two independent samples

The Mann–Whitney U test

Learning ObjectivesWeek 7 · section 7.3
  1. To compute the Mann–Whitney statistic U from the ranks of two independent samples.
  2. To test H0 with the normal approximation, and to read the same test in numiqo.

The question. Two samples are independent when the values in one sample have nothing to do with the values in the other: different deliveries, different customers. The Mann–Whitney test asks whether values from one population tend to be larger than values from the other. If Rive and Pâquis were equally fast, the Rive deliveries would be spread evenly through the sorted list of sixteen, and their ranks would add up to about half of all the ranks. If Rive is faster, its deliveries crowd the top of the list (the small ranks), and its rank sum is small.

By hand · the pizzeriaThe sixteen deliveries

Is Rive faster? The Mann–Whitney test at the 5% level

Rive: 18, 19, 21, 22, 23, 25, 26, 29 minutes. Pâquis: 24, 27, 28, 30, 31, 32, 34, 95 minutes. The ranks are those of section 7.2. Test, at the 5% level, whether delivery times differ between the two branches.

Walked, one step per press
The Mann–Whitney U test · the formulasWeek 7

Hypotheses. H0: the two populations have the same distribution. Ha: values in one population tend to be larger than in the other.

Step 1. Rank all n1 + n2 values together. Step 2. Add the ranks of sample 1; call the sum R1.

U1 = R1 − n1(n1 + 1) ⁄ 2     U2 = n1n2 − U1     U = the smaller of U1 and U2

U1 counts the pairs (one value from each sample) in which the sample-1 value is the larger one. If H0 is true, U is close to half of all n1n2 pairs.

mean of U = n1n2 ⁄ 2     SD of U = √( n1n2(n1 + n2 + 1) ⁄ 12 )     z = (U − mean) ⁄ SD

Decision, two-tailed at level α. Reject H0 if |z| ≥ zα⁄2, which is 1.960 at α = 0.05. The normal approximation is good when both samples have at least 8 values; for smaller samples numiqo also prints an exact p-value, which is the one to report.

Key TakeawaysSection 7.3
  • The Mann–Whitney test compares two independent samples through the sum of the ranks of one of them.
  • U = R1 − n1(n1 + 1) ⁄ 2, take the smaller of U1 and n1n2 − U1; z = (U − n1n2⁄2) ⁄ √(n1n2(n1+n2+1)⁄12).

Section 7.4 · Paired samples

The Wilcoxon signed-rank test

Learning ObjectivesWeek 7 · section 7.4
  1. To recognise paired data and compute the differences.
  2. To compute the Wilcoxon statistic T from the ranks of the differences and test it with the normal approximation.

The question. Two samples are paired when each value in one sample belongs with one value in the other: the same driver before and after a change, the same shop in two months. Week 4B tested paired data with a t-test on the differences. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test ranks the sizes of the differences and then asks whether the large differences point mostly one way.

Walked, one step per press
The Wilcoxon signed-rank test · the formulasWeek 7

Hypotheses. H0: the differences are spread evenly around zero. Ha: the differences tend to be positive, or tend to be negative.

Step 1. Compute each difference d = after − before. Drop any pair with d = 0 and reduce n by one for each. Step 2. Rank the absolute differences |d| (ties get the average rank). Step 3. Add the ranks of the positive differences, T+, and of the negative differences, T−. Check: T+ + T− = n(n + 1) ⁄ 2.

T = the smaller of T+ and T−     mean of T = n(n + 1) ⁄ 4     SD of T = √( n(n + 1)(2n + 1) ⁄ 24 )     z = (T − mean) ⁄ SD

Decision, two-tailed at 5%. Reject H0 if |z| ≥ 1.960. Use the approximation when n is at least 10; numiqo also prints an exact p-value for small samples and applies a small continuity correction to the approximate one, so its p can differ slightly in the third decimal.

Section 7.5 · Three or more groups

The Kruskal–Wallis test

Learning ObjectivesWeek 7 · section 7.5
  1. To compute the Kruskal–Wallis statistic H from the rank sums of k groups.
  2. To test H against the chi-square table of Week 6 with k − 1 degrees of freedom.

The question. Week 6's one-way ANOVA compared three or more means and assumed normal populations with equal variances. The Kruskal–Wallis test asks the same question with ranks: do values in some groups tend to be larger than in others? Rank all the values together; if the groups were alike, every group's mean rank (its rank sum divided by its size) would be close to the overall mean rank, (N + 1) ⁄ 2.

Our text

By hand · the pizzeriaTips at three branches

Are the evening tips the same at Rive, Pâquis and Carouge?

The staff share the evening's tips. Over five evenings the totals, in CHF, were: Rive 42, 38, 51, 47, 45 · Pâquis 31, 36, 29, 40, 33 · Carouge 39, 44, 35, 48, 41. The owner wants to know whether staff at one branch earn more in tips before she changes the rota. Five evenings per branch is too few to check normality, so she uses ranks. Test at the 5% level.

Walked, one step per press
The Kruskal–Wallis test · the formulasWeek 7

Hypotheses. H0: the k populations have the same distribution. Ha: values in at least one population tend to be larger or smaller than in the others.

Rank all N values together. For each group i, add its ranks, Ri; ni is the group's size.

H = 12 ⁄ (N(N + 1)) × Σ Ri² ⁄ ni − 3(N + 1)

If H0 is true and every group has at least 5 values, H approximately follows a chi-square distribution with df = k − 1, the distribution of Week 6. The test is right-tailed: reject H0 if H ≥ χ²α. With 3 groups, χ²0.05 = 5.991; with 4 groups, 7.815.

As with ANOVA, rejecting says that the groups are not all alike; it does not say which group differs. numiqo adds a follow-up comparison of every pair of groups (the Dunn–Bonferroni test) for that.

Section 7.6 · Rank correlation

Spearman's rank correlation

Learning ObjectivesWeek 7 · section 7.6
  1. To compute Spearman's rank correlation rs from the differences of ranks.
  2. To test it with the t statistic on n − 2 degrees of freedom, as Week 5 tested a slope.

The question. Week 5's correlation coefficient r (Pearson's r) measures how close the points lie to a straight line. Spearman's rs measures how close the two variables are to moving in the same order: the branch that spends most on marketing also gets most new customers, the second-most gets the second-most, and so on, whether or not the relation is a straight line. It is Pearson's r computed on the ranks.

Walked, one step per press
Spearman's rank correlation · the formulasWeek 7

Rank the x values among themselves and the y values among themselves. For each case, d = (rank of x) − (rank of y). When there are no ties,

rs = 1 − 6 Σd² ⁄ ( n(n² − 1) )

rs lies between −1 and +1. It is +1 when the two rankings are identical, −1 when one is the reverse of the other, and near 0 when the orders are unrelated. With ties, compute Pearson's r on the ranks instead (numiqo does this).

Test. H0: the two variables are not related (ρs = 0). Compute

t = rs √( (n − 2) ⁄ (1 − rs²) )    on n − 2 degrees of freedom

and reject at level α (two-tailed) if |t| ≥ tα⁄2, read from the t table of Week 3.

Section 7.7 · Principal components · the idea

Two related questions can be summarised by one score

Learning ObjectivesWeek 7 · section 7.7
  1. To explain a principal component as the direction along which the data vary most.
  2. To compute, for two standardised variables, the variance and the share of the variance that the first component explains.

Why. The pizzeria asked 60 customers nine questions, each answered from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent): the taste, the freshness of the ingredients, the crust, how generous the toppings were, the speed of delivery, the friendliness of the staff, whether the order was correct, whether the pizza arrived hot, and value for money. Nine columns are too many to read one at a time, and many of them say almost the same thing: a customer who liked the taste usually liked the crust too. Principal component analysis (PCA) finds a few new scores, each a weighted combination of the questions, that keep most of the information. Section 7.8 runs it on all nine questions; this section shows the idea on two.

Our text

Definitions · principal component analysisWeek 7

To standardise a variable is to subtract its mean and divide by its standard deviation, so that every variable has mean 0 and variance 1. PCA on standardised variables gives each question the same weight, whatever its scale.

A principal component is a new variable made by adding up the standardised questions with weights. The first principal component is the combination that has the largest possible variance: the direction in which the customers differ most. The second has the largest variance among the directions at a right angle to the first (so it carries information the first does not), and so on.

The variance of a component is called its eigenvalue. With p standardised questions the total variance is p (each question contributes 1), so a component's share of the variance explained is its eigenvalue ⁄ p.

For two standardised variables with correlation r, the variance of the data along a direction at angle θ from the first axis is

variance(θ) = 1 + r × sin(2θ)

so with r > 0 the largest variance is at θ = 45°, and equals 1 + r: the first component is the average of the two standardised questions, and it explains (1 + r) ⁄ 2 of the total.

Section 7.8 · Principal components on the whole survey

Nine questions become two scores, and where machine learning starts

Learning ObjectivesWeek 7 · section 7.8
  1. To read a table of eigenvalues and a scree plot and decide how many components to keep.
  2. To read a table of loadings and give each component a name.
  3. To say whether a business task is supervised learning, unsupervised learning, or neither.
Definitions · reading a PCA outputWeek 7

A scree plot is a chart of the eigenvalues in decreasing order. Two rules decide how many components to keep: keep the components whose eigenvalue is greater than 1 (each of them carries more than one question's worth of variance; this is called the Kaiser rule), and stop where the scree plot bends and the remaining eigenvalues are small and similar.

A loading is the correlation between one question and one component. A loading near +1 or −1 means the question moves almost exactly with the component; a loading near 0 means the two are unrelated. To name a component, list the questions with large loadings on it (above about 0.6) and find what they have in common.

A rotation (numiqo shows the result as the rotated component matrix; the usual method is called varimax) turns the kept components so that each question loads strongly on one component and weakly on the others. The components still explain the same total variance together; they become easier to name.

A question's communality is the sum of its squared loadings on the kept components: the share of that question's variance the kept components explain.

Definitions · where this course meets machine learningWeek 7

Machine learning is the name industry gives to methods that learn a rule from data, either to predict a value or to find structure, and that are judged by how well the rule works on new data.

Supervised learning learns to predict a known outcome y from inputs x, using past cases where y was recorded. The regression line of Week 5, price = a + b × distance, is the simplest supervised model. Larger models (decision trees, neural networks) use many more inputs and more flexible shapes, but the task is the same.

Unsupervised learning has no outcome to predict. It finds structure in the inputs alone: PCA finds the directions in which customers differ; clustering sorts customers into groups that answer alike.

Training data are the cases the model learns from; test data (also called held-out data) are cases kept aside to measure how well it predicts. Overfitting is a model that fits its training data very closely but predicts new cases badly, because it has learned the noise. The statistics of this course guard against the same error with degrees of freedom and p-values; machine learning guards against it by testing on data the model has not seen.
