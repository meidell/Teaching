Week 2B · Quantitative Methods · SUMAS

If another thirty pizzerias were sampled, what would their average be?

Part A gave you the normal curve for one measurement. Now the number you actually compute from a sample — the mean x̄, the proportion p̂ — is itself random: draw a different thirty pizzerias and you get a different average. So a statistic has a mean, a standard deviation and a probability distribution of its own, called its sampling distribution. This page follows the textbook's chapter 6 section by section — §6.1 the mean and standard deviation of the sample mean, §6.2 its sampling distribution and the Central Limit Theorem, §6.3 the sample proportion — walking each of the book's own examples step by step, with a your turn after it.

The session is three hours in two parts. Part 1 (~1 h) is the lesson: your instructor presents the deck and you follow the same numbered sections here — every slide names its section. Part 2 (~2 h) is ten exercises taken straight from the textbook, worked together; solutions open as your instructor reveals them.

By the end of Week 2B you will be able to

  • Explain why a statistic such as x̄ is a random variable, and what its sampling distribution is (§6.1).
  • Compute the mean µx̄ = µ and standard deviation σx̄ = σ/√n of the sample mean — and say in words what each formula means (§6.1).

  • Use a sampling distribution to judge a claim: if the claim were true, how surprising would this sample be?

In Week 1B you collected thirty Margherita prices: mean CHF 19.93, standard deviation about CHF 4.20. Tomorrow a friend walks into thirty different Geneva pizzerias and computes the same average. Within how many francs of 19.93 would you bet their mean lands — nineteen times out of twenty?

Your margin, ± CHF
3.0 CHF
The book's opening sentence

A statistic is a random variable.

A statistic, such as the sample mean or the sample standard deviation, is a number computed from a sample. Since a sample is random, every statistic is a random variable: it varies from sample to sample in a way that cannot be predicted with certainty. As a random variable it has a mean, a standard deviation, and a probability distribution. The probability distribution of a statistic is called its sampling distribution.Saylor, chapter 6, introduction

So "what would another thirty average?" is a question about the sampling distribution of the sample mean. The book writes X̄ when the sample mean is thought of as a random variable, and x̄ for a value it takes — your 19.93 is one x̄. Sections 2B.2–2B.5 build the distribution of X̄; section 2B.6 does the same for the sample proportion P̂; and section 2B.4 answers the question above.

One pizzeria can charge anything. The average of thirty cannot — and this week says exactly how far it can go.

The formulas — and what they say

µX̄ = µ  and  σX̄ = σ ÷ √n

Suppose random samples of size n are drawn from a population with mean µ and standard deviation σ. The mean µX̄ and standard deviation σX̄ of the sample mean X̄ satisfy µX̄ = µ and σX̄ = σ/√n.
The first formula says that if we could take every possible sample from the population and compute the corresponding sample mean, then those numbers would center at the number we wish to estimate, the population mean µ.
The second formula says that averages computed from samples vary less than individual measurements on the population do, and quantifies the relationship.Saylor §6.1

The rowing team is the formulas in miniature: the population {152, 156, 160, 164} has µ = 158 and σ = √20 = 4.47, and the 16 sample means have µX̄ = 158 — exactly µ — and σX̄ = √10 = 3.16 — exactly √20 ÷ √2. Not a coincidence. The spread shrinks by √n, not by n: four times as many rowers in the sample only halves the wobble.

Section 2B.3 · §6.2 The Central Limit Theorem

Why the bell shows up

The book's population of zeros and ones

Half the population is 0, half is 1. Take samples of size 1, 5, 10, 20.

Suppose we take samples of size 1, 5, 10, or 20 from a population that consists entirely of the numbers 0 and 1, half the population 0, half 1, so that the population mean is 0.5. […] As n increases the sampling distribution of X̄ evolves in an interesting way: the probabilities on the lower and the upper ends shrink and the probabilities in the middle become larger in relation to them. If we were to continue to increase n then the shape of the sampling distribution would become smoother and more bell-shaped.Saylor §6.2 · Figure 6.2
The distribution of X̄ — the book's four tables, exactly

The population could not be less bell-shaped: two bars, 0 and 1. By n = 20 the sample mean is a bell anyway.

The theorem

For n ≥ 30, X̄ is approximately normal — whatever the population looks like.

The Central Limit Theorem. For samples of size 30 or more, the sample mean is approximately normally distributed, with mean µX̄ = µ and standard deviation σX̄ = σ/√n, where n is the sample size. The larger the sample size, the better the approximation.Saylor §6.2

The book's Figure 6.3 shows it for several population shapes. Here it is for the pizzeria's own: a population of 300 Geneva Margherita prices, skewed to the right like the real market. Each click draws random samples of size n and adds their means to the pile. Watch where the pile is centred, how wide it is against σ/√n, and what shape it takes.

Two random variables, two distributions. The book insists on the distinction before any calculation, and so does every exam question:

  • X, the measurement of a single element selected at random from the population: its distribution is the distribution of the population, with mean µ and standard deviation σ.
  • X̄, the mean of the measurements in a sample of size n: its distribution is its sampling distribution, with mean µX̄ = µ and standard deviation σX̄ = σ/√n.

Section 2B.5 · §6.2 Normally distributed populations

Any n, if the population is normal

The second case

A normal population gives a normal X̄ for every sample size.

The Central Limit Theorem says that no matter what the distribution of the population is, as long as the sample is "large," meaning of size 30 or more, the sample mean is approximately normally distributed. If the population is normal to begin with then the sample mean also has a normal distribution, regardless of the sample size.
For samples of any size drawn from a normally distributed population, the sample mean is normally distributed, with mean µX̄ = µ and standard deviation σX̄ = σ/√n, where n is the sample size.Saylor §6.2

Section 2B.6 · §6.3 The sample proportion

The share above CHF 20, again

p and p̂

A proportion is the mean of zeros and ones — so everything above applies.

The population proportion is denoted p and the sample proportion is denoted p̂. Thus if in reality 43% of people entering a store make a purchase before leaving, p = 0.43; if in a sample of 200 people entering the store, 78 make a purchase, p̂ = 78/200 = 0.39.
Suppose random samples of size n are drawn from a population in which the proportion with a characteristic of interest is p. The mean µP̂ and standard deviation σP̂ of the sample proportion P̂ satisfy µP̂ = p and σP̂ = √(pq/n), where q = 1 − p.Saylor §6.3

Why the Central Limit Theorem reaches p̂: label every pizzeria 1 if it charges over CHF 20 and 0 if not. The population is now numbers, and its mean is (number of 1s) ÷ N — which is p. In the same way the sample proportion p̂ is the sample mean x̄ of the zeros and ones. Click the tiles to see it.

When is the sample large enough?

The book's check: p ± 3σP̂ must lie wholly inside [0, 1].

For large samples, the sample proportion is approximately normally distributed, with mean µP̂ = p and standard deviation σP̂ = √(pq/n). A sample is large if the interval [p − 3σP̂, p + 3σP̂] lies wholly within the interval [0, 1].
In actual practice p is not known, hence neither is σP̂. In that case in order to check that the sample is sufficiently large we substitute the known quantity p̂ for p.Saylor §6.3
The check — for the share above CHF 20 (Week 1B: 11 of 30, p̂ = 0.37)
