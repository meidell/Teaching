Week 2B · Quantitative Methods · SUMAS

How long until the pizza reaches the table?

In Part A you counted: Margheritas, desserts, warranties. This week you measure: delivery time, dough weight, the fill of a can. A measured quantity can take any value in a range, so probability is no longer a bar you add up — it is an area under a curve. The curve that matters most is the normal distribution, and by the end of the session you will read its table forwards and backwards and turn it into a promise the pizzeria can print on its menu. This is the textbook's chapter 5.

The session is three hours in two parts. Part 1 (~1 h) is the lesson: your instructor presents the deck and you follow the same numbered sections here — every slide names its section. Part 2 (~2 h) is ten exercises taken straight from the textbook, worked together; solutions open as your instructor reveals them.

By the end of Week 2B you will be able to

  • Explain why a continuous random variable has P(X = x) = 0 and why probability is area under a density curve.
  • Compute probabilities for a uniform distribution as the area of a rectangle.
  • Read the standard normal table for P(Z < z), P(Z > z) and P(a < Z < b) — using symmetry where the table stops.

  • Read the table backwards: find the value behind a percentile or a tail area, and know z0.05 = 1.645 and z0.025 = 1.96 by heart.
  • Turn "95% of pizzas within __ minutes" into a number you can defend.

Section 2B.1 · The hook

Under twelve minutes?

Your estimate

From order to table: 14 minutes on average, give or take 3.

You timed a month of Friday service. The time from order to plate-on-the-table averages 14 minutes with a standard deviation of 3 minutes, and the times pile up in a bell. A food blogger will write about any place that serves in under 12 minutes. What share of your pizzas make it?

Your estimate of the share under 12 minutes
30%
What changes this week

Time is measured, not counted.

Part A's random variables took values you could list — 0, 1, 2 Margheritas — and a probability was a bar. A time can be 11.7 minutes, or 11.73, or 11.7342…: there are too many values to list, and the chance of any exact one is zero. What has a probability is an interval — "under 12 minutes" — and that probability is an area. Section 2B.4 computes the area you just guessed.

Counting gave you bars. Measuring gives you a curve, and the answer is the area under it.

Section 2B.2 · Continuous random variables

Probability is area

The density curve

A continuous random variable is described by a curve, not a table.

The density function of X is a curve with two properties: it never goes below the axis, and the total area under it is 1. The probability that X falls between a and b is the area under the curve between a and b. A single point has no width, so no area: P(X = x) = 0, and P(X < 12) and P(X ≤ 12) are the same number.

The simplest density is flat: the uniform distribution. The oven takes between 10 and 16 minutes to reach temperature, any time in that range equally likely. The density is a rectangle of width 6, so its height must be 1/6 — the area has to come to 1.

Oven pre-heat time — drag the two bounds
Uniform on [c, d]:  height = 1 ÷ (d − c)  ·  P(a < X < b) = (b − a) ÷ (d − c)

Section 2B.3 · The standard normal

The one curve that fits all

Z ~ N(0, 1)

The standard normal: mean 0, standard deviation 1, and one table.

A normal distribution is the bell you met in Week 1B's Empirical Rule, now with exact areas. Every normal curve is the same shape, stretched and shifted; the one with µ = 0 and σ = 1 is called the standard normal, its variable is written Z, and its areas are printed once, in a table. The table gives the area to the left: P(Z < z).

The standard normal table, live — drag z

P(Z > z) = 1 − P(Z < z)  ·  P(a < Z < b) = P(Z < b) − P(Z < a)  ·  symmetry: P(Z < −z) = P(Z > z)

The table only knows left tails. Every other area is one subtraction, or one use of symmetry, away.

Section 2B.4 · General normal variables

Standardise, then look up

From X to Z

Any normal X becomes Z by one move: z = (x − µ) ÷ σ.

The table is for Z only. To use it for delivery times, turn each time into a z-score — how many standard deviations it sits from the mean, exactly Week 1B's z. Then P(X < x) = P(Z < z). Round z to two decimals, because that is what the table has.

P(X < x) = P(Z < (x − µ) ÷ σ)

Section 2B.5 · Tails and the inverse

The value behind a percentile

Reading the table backwards

You know the area; you want the value.

So far: value in, area out. The other question is just as common: which time do 95% of pizzas beat? Find the area inside the table, read off z, then undo the standardisation: x = µ + z·σ. The z that leaves an area c in the right tail is written zc. Two of them you will use every week from now on: z0.05 = 1.645 and z0.025 = 1.96.

P(Z > zc) = c  ·  x = µ + z·σ
The delivery promise — choose how sure you want to be

A promise is a percentile. "Within 19 minutes, 95% of the time" is z0.05 = 1.645 in the kitchen's own units.

Section 2B.6 · Binomial meets normal

Last Friday, in one line

A promise from Week 2A

140 customers, p = 0.8, 120 dough balls — without adding twenty terms.

In Part A the chance of selling out, P(X ≥ 121) for X binomial with n = 140 and p = 0.8, took the site twenty terms of the formula: 3.2%. Look at those bars again — they are a bell. When n is large, a binomial is close to a normal with the same mean and standard deviation: µ = np = 112 and σ = √(npq) = 4.73.

The binomial bars and the normal curve on top

The half-unit correction uses 120.5 instead of 120, because the bar for 121 starts at 120.5 on a continuous scale. It is a refinement, not a rule you will be examined on — but it shows why the plain line is a little high.

One line, one table lookup, and within a percentage point of twenty terms. That is why the normal curve runs the rest of this course.
