Week 5 · Quantitative Methods · SUMAS

Reading Excel's regression printout, one cell at a time

A regression finds the straight line that best predicts one number (y) from one or more other numbers (x). In practice you never compute it by hand: Excel computes it and prints a table of about thirty numbers. A regression question in an exam usually gives you such a table with some cells left blank and asks you to fill them in and to say what they mean. This week explains every cell of that table: what it measures, how Excel computes it from the cells around it, and what it tells a business.

The printout below is the one we read all week. It comes from a small made-up business: Lina's online tea shop. For eight weeks Lina changed what she spent on Instagram ads and counted the orders each week. y is the number of orders in a week; x is the ad spend that week, in hundreds of CHF (x = 2 means CHF 200).

Section 5.1 · The printout as a whole

Where the printout comes from, and what is in its three blocks

Why this section: before reading single numbers you need to know which block of the printout answers which question. An exam question names cells by their row and column ("the P-value of Parking rates"), so you must find them quickly.

How to get the printout in ExcelData Analysis ToolPak
  1. Put the data in columns: one column for y, and one column for each x next to each other. Put a name in the first row of each column.
  2. Open Data → Data Analysis → Regression. If you do not see "Data Analysis", switch the add-in on once: File → Options → Add-ins → Manage: Excel Add-ins → Go → tick Analysis ToolPak (on a Mac: Tools → Excel Add-ins).
  3. In Input Y Range, select the y column with its name. In Input X Range, select all x columns with their names.
  4. Tick Labels, so that Excel uses your column names in the output. Without it, Excel calls the x columns "X Variable 1", "X Variable 2", and so on.
  5. Leave Confidence Level unticked (section 5.8 explains what it changes), choose an output cell, and press OK.

The output has three blocks. Regression Statistics (top) says how well the line fits. ANOVA (middle) splits the variation of y into the part the line explains and the part it misses, and tests whether the line explains anything at all. The coefficient table (bottom) gives the line itself, one row per coefficient, with a test and an interval for each.

Section 5.2 · The Coefficients column

The intercept and the slopes are the fitted line

Why this section: the Coefficients column is the reason anyone runs a regression. It gives the equation that predicts y, and each slope says how much y changes when one x changes. Every other cell of the printout says how much these coefficients can be trusted.

Theory · the coefficientsSaylor §10.4

The fitted line is the equation ŷ = b0 + b1x. ŷ ("y-hat") is the value of y the line predicts. The intercept b0 is the predicted y when x = 0. The slope b1 is how much the predicted y changes when x rises by one unit.

Excel chooses the line by least squares: of all possible lines, it takes the one for which the sum of the squared vertical distances between the points and the line is the smallest. A vertical distance y − ŷ is called a residual: what the line misses for that observation.

b1 = SSxy ⁄ SSxx and b0 = ȳ − b1·x̄
SSxx = Σ(x − x̄)² SSxy = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ)

Tea shop: x̄ = 5.5, ȳ = 62.875, SSxx = 42, SSxy = 254.5, so b1 = 254.5 ⁄ 42 = 6.0595 and b0 = 62.875 − 6.0595 × 5.5 = 29.5476. These are the two numbers in the Coefficients column: ŷ = 29.55 + 6.06x.

With several x's the equation is ŷ = b0 + b1x1 + b2x2 + …. Each slope is then the change in ŷ when that x rises by one unit and the other x's stay the same. Excel computes them with matrix algebra; you are not asked to compute them by hand.

Section 5.3 · The SS column of ANOVA

SS Total = SS Regression + SS Residual

Why this section: R Square, the Standard Error and F are all computed from the three sums of squares. If you understand the SS column, half of the printout follows from it, and a blank in this column of an exam printout is a subtraction.

Theory · splitting the variation of yANOVA = analysis of variance

Take one week of the tea shop. Its distance from the average orders ȳ can be cut into two pieces at the line:

(y − ȳ) = (ŷ − ȳ) + (y − ŷ)
total distance = the part the line predicts + the part the line misses

Square each piece and add over all observations. The squares add up in the same way (this is a property of the least squares line):

SS Total = Σ(y − ȳ)² — how much y varies around its average
SS Regression = Σ(ŷ − ȳ)² — the part of that variation the line explains
SS Residual = Σ(y − ŷ)² — the part it does not explain (the book calls it SSE)
SS Total = SS Regression + SS Residual

Tea shop: 1592.875 = 1542.149 + 50.726. A large SS Regression and a small SS Residual mean the points lie close to the line.

Section 5.4 · Observations, df, MS and the Standard Error

From sums of squares to the typical miss of the line

Why this section: a sum of squares grows with every observation you add, so 50.7 on its own says little. Dividing it by its degrees of freedom gives an average square (MS), and the square root of the residual MS is the Standard Error: how far, in y's own units, a typical observation is from the line. That one number is what you quote when someone asks "how precise is the prediction?".

Theory · degrees of freedom and mean squaresn observations, k x variables

n is the number of observations: the rows of data Excel used. Excel prints it as Observations. The tea shop has n = 8 weeks.

k is the number of x variables in the regression: the number of columns you selected as Input X Range. Each x variable gets one slope, so k is also the number of slopes, and the coefficient table has k + 1 rows (the k slopes plus the Intercept). The tea shop has one x (ad spend), so k = 1. The flats have two x's (floor area and minutes to the tram), so k = 2.

Degrees of freedom (df) count how many observations are still free to measure the scatter after the line has been placed. Placing the line uses up one observation per coefficient: k for the slopes and 1 for the intercept, k + 1 in all.

df Regression = k
df Residual = n − k − 1
df Total = n − 1 (= the sum of the two above)

A mean square (MS) is a sum of squares divided by its df. Excel prints two:

MS Regression = SS Regression ⁄ k
MS Residual = SS Residual ⁄ (n − k − 1)
Standard Error (in Regression Statistics) = √MS Residual

Tea shop: n = 8, k = 1, so df = 1, 6, 7. MS Residual = 50.726 ⁄ 6 = 8.454 and the Standard Error is √8.454 = 2.91 orders: a typical week is about 3 orders above or below the line.

Two cells are called "Standard Error". The one in Regression Statistics is the typical miss of the line, explained here. The column called Standard Error in the coefficient table is something else: how precise each coefficient is (section 5.7).

Section 5.5 · R Square, Multiple R, Adjusted R Square

How much of the variation in y the line explains

Why this section: R Square is the number managers ask for first ("how good is the model?"). It is also the cell most often left blank in exams, because it follows from the SS column in one division. Adjusted R Square exists because R Square can be made larger by adding x columns that mean nothing.

Theory · the three fit measuresSaylor §10.6 for one x
R Square = SS Regression ⁄ SS Total = 1 − SS Residual ⁄ SS Total
Multiple R = √R Square
Adjusted R Square = 1 − (1 − R Square) × (n − 1) ⁄ (n − k − 1)
n = the number of observations · k = the number of x variables (section 5.4)

R Square is the share of the variation of y that the x's explain, from 0 (nothing) to 1 (every point on the line). Tea shop: 1542.149 ⁄ 1592.875 = 0.968: the ad spend explains 96.8% of the week-to-week differences in orders.

Multiple R is the correlation between the real y and the predicted ŷ. It is a square root, so Excel always prints it positive. With one x it equals the size of the correlation coefficient r, but the sign is lost: for a falling line, r is negative and Multiple R is not. Take the sign from the slope.

Adjusted R Square corrects R Square for the number of x's. Adding any x column, even random numbers, never lowers R Square. Adjusted R Square goes up only when the new x explains more than a random column would. Use it to compare two regressions with different numbers of x's. Tea shop: 1 − (1 − 0.968154) × 7 ⁄ 6 = 0.963.

Section 5.6 · F and Significance F

Does the regression explain anything at all?

Why this section: eight points will always give some slope, even if ads did nothing. The F test asks whether the x's together explain more of y than chance alone would. Significance F is its p-value: the one cell that tells you whether to take the whole regression seriously.

Theory · the F test of the whole regressionthe five steps of Week 4A

Hypotheses. H0: every slope is 0 (the x's explain nothing about y). Ha: at least one slope is not 0.

F = MS Regression ⁄ MS Residual
Significance F = P(an F-distribution with k and n − k − 1 df is larger than the observed F)
n = the number of observations · k = the number of x variables (section 5.4)

F compares the variation the line explains per x with the variation it leaves unexplained per free observation. If the x's explain nothing, F is usually around 1. A large F is evidence that they explain something.

Significance F is the p-value of this test. If it is smaller than the level α (usually 0.05), reject H0: the regression explains a real part of y. Tea shop: F = 1542.149 ⁄ 8.454 = 182.4, Significance F = 1.02E−05 = 0.0000102: far below 0.05.

Excel writes very small numbers in scientific notation: 1.02E−05 means 1.02 × 10−5 = 0.0000102.

Section 5.7 · Standard Error, t Stat and P-value of each coefficient

Is this one coefficient different from zero?

Why this section: the F test says whether the x's matter together. A manager needs to know which ones matter: is it worth paying for the ads, does the walk to the tram really lower the rent? Each row of the coefficient table answers that question for one coefficient, with the test of Week 4A.

Theory · a t test on every rowSaylor §10.5 for one x

Another eight weeks would give a slightly different slope, because orders also move for reasons that have nothing to do with ads. The Standard Error column says how much a coefficient would vary from sample to sample. With one x, Excel computes the slope's standard error as:

SE(b1) = Standard Error of the regression ⁄ √SSxx tea shop: 2.9076 ⁄ √42 = 0.4487

Hypotheses for one row. H0: this coefficient is 0 in the population. Ha: it is not 0 (two-tailed).

t Stat = Coefficient ⁄ Standard Error (how many standard errors the coefficient is from 0)
P-value = P(|T| ≥ |t Stat|) for Student's t with df = n − k − 1 (the df of Residual)
n = the number of observations · k = the number of x variables (section 5.4)

Tea shop, ad spend: t Stat = 6.0595 ⁄ 0.4487 = 13.51, on 6 df. P-value 1.02E−05 < 0.05: the ads' slope is not 0.

The Intercept row tests whether the intercept is 0. That is rarely a business question; usually you skip that row.

With one x, the t test of the slope and the F test are the same test: t Stat² = F (13.506² = 182.4) and the slope's P-value equals Significance F. With several x's they differ.

Section 5.8 · Lower 95% and Upper 95%

The range in which the true coefficient lies, with 95% confidence

Why this section: a P-value says only whether a coefficient is 0 or not. A business needs a size: "each CHF 100 of ads brings between 5 and 7 more orders". The last columns of the printout give that range, and exams often ask for them.

Theory · the confidence interval of a coefficientthe interval of Week 3
Lower 95% = Coefficient − t0.025 × Standard Error
Upper 95% = Coefficient + t0.025 × Standard Error
t0.025 from the t table on the row df = n − k − 1 (n observations, k x variables)

Tea shop, ad spend: df = 6, t0.025 = 2.447. 6.0595 ± 2.447 × 0.4487 = 4.96 to 7.16 orders per CHF 100 of ads.

The link with the P-value: the 95% interval contains 0 exactly when the P-value is above 0.05. Both say the same thing in two ways.

Why are there two pairs of columns? The Regression dialog has a box Confidence Level with 95 in it. Excel always prints the 95% interval first. The second pair (Lower 95.0%, Upper 95.0%) is the level in that box. If you do not tick the box, it is 95 again, and the two pairs are identical. If you tick it and type 99, the second pair becomes Lower 99.0% and Upper 99.0%.

Distance row: Coefficient −2.2865, Standard Error 0.1861, n = 14.

df = 12, so t0.025 = 2.179 (t table).

Minutes to tram row: Coefficient −32.2063, Standard Error 10.9967, n = 12, k = 2.
