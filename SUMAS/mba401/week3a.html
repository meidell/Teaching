Week 3A · Quantitative Methods · SUMAS

If you sampled again tomorrow, how different would the mean be?

Your thirty Geneva prices from Week 1B averaged CHF 19.93. Another thirty would give another number. This week is about the distribution of the sample mean itself: where it is centred, how wide it is — σ/√n — and why it is bell-shaped even when the prices are not. It is the keystone of the course: every interval in Week 3B and every test in Week 4A stands on it. This is the textbook's chapter 6.

The session is three hours in two parts. Part 1 (~1 h) is the lesson: your instructor presents the deck and you follow the same numbered sections here — every slide names its section. Part 2 (~2 h) is ten exercises taken straight from the textbook, worked together; solutions open as your instructor reveals them.

🏫 In class: 3 h · lesson ~1 h, then exercises ~2 h · bring a calculator 📖 Textbook: Saylor, ch. 6 — Sampling Distributions ▶ The Lecture button (top-right) shows the deck we use in class

By the end of Week 3A you will be able to

  • Explain what a sampling distribution is: the distribution of a statistic over all the samples you could have drawn.
  • State the mean and standard deviation of the sample mean — µx̄ = µ and σx̄ = σ/√n — and say why it is √n, not n.
  • State the Central Limit Theorem and say when it lets you treat x̄ as normal.
  • Compute P(x̄ in a range), and tell it apart from P(X in a range) for a single value.
  • Do the same for a sample proportion: µp̂ = p, σp̂ = √(pq/n), and the book's check that p̂ is close enough to normal.
  • Use a sampling distribution to judge a claim: how surprising would our sample be if the claim were true?

Section 3A.1 · The hook

Another thirty tomorrow

Your estimate

Your thirty prices averaged CHF 19.93. What would another thirty average?

In Week 1B you collected thirty Margherita prices: mean CHF 19.93, standard deviation about CHF 4.20. Tomorrow a friend walks into thirty different Geneva pizzerias and computes the same average. Within how many francs of 19.93 would you bet their mean lands — nineteen times out of twenty?

Your margin, ± CHF
3.0 CHF
The question behind the question

A sample mean is itself a random variable.

Every sample gives a different mean. So x̄ has a distribution of its own — over all the samples you could have drawn — and that distribution is called the sampling distribution of x̄. It is narrower than the prices themselves, because averaging cancels extremes; this week says exactly how much narrower, and section 3A.4 answers the question above.

One pizzeria can be anywhere. The average of thirty cannot.

The centre does not move: on average, a sample mean hits the population mean. The spread shrinks by √n, not by n — so four times as many pizzerias only halves the wobble. That √n is the single most expensive fact in statistics: precision costs the square of what you would hope.

Section 3A.3 · The Central Limit Theorem

Why the bell shows up

The theorem

For large n, x̄ is approximately normal — whatever the population looks like.

If the population is normal, x̄ is normal for any n. If it is not, the Central Limit Theorem says the distribution of x̄ still becomes approximately normal as n grows; the textbook's working rule is n ≥ 30. Pick an ugly population and watch.

Population shape and sample size

Averaging is a machine for making bells. That is why the normal table keeps working on data that is not normal.

Section 3A.5 · The sample proportion

The share above CHF 20, again

p̂ is a mean too

A proportion is the mean of zeros and ones — so it has a sampling distribution.

In Week 1B, 11 of your 30 pizzerias charged over CHF 20: p̂ = 0.37. Another thirty would give another p̂. If the true share in Geneva is p:

µp̂ = p   ·   σp̂ = √(pq ÷ n), q = 1 − p

p̂ is approximately normal when n is large enough. The textbook's check: the interval p ± 3σp̂ must lie entirely inside [0, 1]. If it spills below 0 or above 1, a bell centred at p would put probability on impossible shares, so the normal approximation is not safe.

Section 3A.6 · Surprise as evidence

Is the magazine wrong?

Back to Week 1A

"The average Margherita in Geneva costs CHF 21." Your thirty say 19.93.

In Week 1A you met the idea of a test in one breath: assume the claim, ask how surprising your data would be, decide. Now you can do the middle step properly. If the magazine were right (µ = 21, and σ ≈ 4.20), the mean of thirty would be approximately normal with σx̄ = 4.20/√30 = 0.767. How often would it come out as low as 19.93?

The same move works for a proportion: the textbook's Exercise 25 in §6.3 (Part 2, Exercise 10) asks whether a smoking campaign worked, and there the answer is 7 in 10,000 — a very different verdict.

A sampling distribution tells you what "normal wobble" looks like. Evidence is whatever falls outside it — and Week 4A decides where "outside" begins.
