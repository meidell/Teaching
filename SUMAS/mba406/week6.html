Working capital and cash: why is a profitable business running out of cash?

In Week 5 you found that Rochat made 240 of profit in 2025 and produced a free cash flow of −2. Cash fell from 150 to 90 and the bank line grew from 100 to 160. This week explains where the cash went and how to stop it going there. The tools are the cash conversion cycle (the number of days between paying for timber and being paid for the furniture made from it), credit policy (the terms on which Rochat lets hotels pay later), inventory, a cash budget (a month-by-month forecast of the money coming in and going out) and the ways a firm borrows for a few months: the bank line and trade credit. The decision for Léa: what changes so that the Lausanne project can be paid for without the bank calling about the overdraft?

Section 6.1 · The puzzle

A profit of 240, a cash balance that fell by 60, and a bank that is asking questions

The running case

Where did the 240 go?

Rochat's 2025 accounts (CHF 000, Week 1): net income 240; cash 150 at the start of the year, 90 at the end; the bank line (a short-term loan the firm can draw and repay as it needs) rose from 100 to 160. Week 1's cash-flow statement gave the first answer: the firm spent 280 on the router and 130 more was tied up in working capital, which is receivables (invoices the hotels have not paid) plus inventory (timber and half-built furniture) minus payables (invoices Rochat has not paid its suppliers). Working capital went from 380 to 510. Week 5 turned that into a free cash flow of −2.

"We made 240 last year. In February the bank called because the line was at 160 and they want to know how we will pay for Lausanne. Where is the money?" — Léa, Monday

The money is in the hotels' accounts departments and in the workshop. This week measures how long it stays there, what that costs, and what Léa can change.

The one idea

Profit is earned when the furniture is delivered. Cash arrives when the hotel pays. The gap between the two has to be financed.

An income statement records a sale when the goods are delivered (Week 1: accrual accounting). The cash comes later, and the timber was paid for earlier. Between the two payments the firm has money out of the bank and no money in: that gap is financed by the firm's own cash, by its suppliers (who wait to be paid) or by the bank line. A business that grows, or that lets its customers pay later, or that holds more stock, needs more of this financing every year even when it is profitable. That is Léa's puzzle, and it has a number: the cash conversion cycle, in days.

cash conversion cycle = inventory days + receivable days − payable days

Sections 6.2 to 6.4 measure the three terms for Rochat and price each one. Section 6.5 turns the year into a month-by-month cash budget. Section 6.6 chooses how to finance what the budget shows.

Section 6.2 · The cash conversion cycle

Eighty-nine days between paying for the timber and being paid for the table

Three periods, measured in days

Each balance-sheet line, divided by the flow that feeds it, times 365

Each term of the cycle turns a stock on the balance sheet into a number of days by dividing it by the yearly flow that runs through it. Inventory days = inventory ÷ cost of goods sold × 365: how long timber and work in progress sit in the workshop before they are sold; cost of goods sold (COGS) is the cost of what was sold in the year, so the stock is compared with what it costs, not with what it sells for. Receivable days = receivables ÷ revenue × 365: how long a hotel takes to pay after delivery (Week 1 called this days' sales in receivables). Payable days = payables ÷ cost of goods sold × 365: how long Rochat takes to pay its own suppliers. The operating cycle is inventory days plus receivable days: from timber arriving to cash arriving. The cash conversion cycle subtracts the days the suppliers finance: from Rochat paying out to Rochat being paid.

inventory days = inventory ⁄ COGS × 365  ·  receivable days = receivables ⁄ revenue × 365  ·  payable days = payables ⁄ COGS × 365

Year-end balances, as in every week of this course. The textbook sometimes averages the opening and closing balance; say which you used.

A second example, a different trade

A chain of bakeries: a negative cycle

A chain of bakeries around Geneva sells 9,000 a year of bread and pastries that cost 3,600 to make. Customers pay at the till (receivables 0). Flour and butter sit in the stores three days (inventory 30 at the year end). The chain pays its suppliers after 45 days (payables 444). Its cash conversion cycle?

Show the worked answer

Inventory days = 30 ⁄ 3,600 × 365 = 3.0. Receivable days = 0. Payable days = 444 ⁄ 3,600 × 365 = 45.0. Cycle = 3.0 + 0 − 45.0 = −42 days. The chain is paid for its bread six weeks before it pays for the flour. Its suppliers finance it; the more it sells, the more cash it holds. Supermarkets and airlines (tickets paid before the flight) live like this. A furniture maker that delivers to hotels on 60-day terms cannot: its cycle is positive, and growth consumes cash.

Section 6.3 · Receivables and credit policy

Sixty-one days of credit to the hotels: what it costs, and what a discount for paying early would cost

Credit policy

When a firm lets a customer pay later, it lends the customer the money

Rochat's invoices say "payable in 60 days" and the hotels pay in 61 on average. The 400 of receivables on the balance sheet is a loan from Rochat to its customers, at no interest. Rochat finances that loan with its bank line at 6%, so the 400 costs it about 24 a year in interest, one tenth of its profit. A credit policy sets three things: the terms (how many days the customer has, and whether paying early earns a discount), the credit decision (which customers are allowed to pay later at all), and the collection (what happens when a customer is late). Each is a trade-off between selling more and tying up less.

Terms are written as, for example, "2/10, net 60": the customer may deduct 2% if it pays within 10 days; otherwise the full amount is due in 60 days. To a customer that could pay in 10 days, not taking the discount means borrowing 98 for 50 extra days and paying 2 for it. As a yearly interest rate:

yearly rate of a missed discount = (1 + discount ⁄ (100 − discount))365 ⁄ (net days − discount days) − 1

Revenue 2,400 · receivables 400 · bank line 6%
On Rochat → a 2% discount is an expensive way to borrow: it pays the hotels 15.9% a year for money the bank lends at 6%. It makes sense only if the bank will not lend, or if the hotels' finance departments take it anyway (most do, when the rate is this high). The cheaper route is to collect in 45 days instead of 61: a reminder on day 30, a call on day 45, and new contracts written at 45 days. That frees about 104 of cash at no cost but a phone call.

The credit decision

Grant credit when the expected gain is positive: margin if paid, cost if not

A new hotel wants to order 100 of furniture a year on 60-day terms. Making it costs Rochat 60 (the 40% gross margin of Week 1). If the hotel pays, Rochat gains 40. If it does not, Rochat loses the 60 it spent. Call p the probability that the hotel does not pay. The expected gain is 40 × (1 − p) − 60 × p; it is positive as long as p is below 40 ⁄ (40 + 60) = 40%. A business with a 40% margin can afford customers that look risky; a business with a 10% margin cannot (its break-even p is 10%). A repeat customer changes the arithmetic again: the first order is a test, and the gain of the later orders is the reason to run it.

grant credit if margin × (1 − p) > cost × p   ·   break-even p = margin ⁄ (margin + cost)
A second example, a different trade

An IT-services firm in Geneva writes "1/10, net 30" on its invoices

Show the worked answer

Not taking the discount means paying 100 instead of 99 for 20 extra days: 1 ⁄ 99 = 1.01% for 20 days. Compounded over 365 ⁄ 20 = 18.25 periods: 1.010118.25 − 1 = 20.1% a year (18.4% by the simple method). Any client with a bank line below 20% should take the discount; the firm is offering a very expensive loan, which is the point: it wants the cash in ten days.

Section 6.4 · Inventory

Eighty-nine days of timber and half-built furniture, and what holding it costs

The cost of holding stock

Inventory costs interest, space, insurance and waste every day it sits there

Rochat holds 350 of inventory: oak and walnut in the timber store, tables and cabinets in progress, and a few finished pieces waiting for delivery. The carrying cost of inventory is what holding it costs for a year, as a percentage of its value: the interest on the money tied up (the bank line's 6%), plus storage, insurance, and the timber that warps or goes out of fashion (about 4% for a furniture maker: a course assumption). At 10%, 350 of stock costs 35 a year, 15% of the profit. Against that, stock avoids two costs: running out (a hotel's order delayed because the walnut is not in the store) and ordering too often (each delivery of timber costs transport and handling). The textbook's economic order quantity (EOQ) finds the order size that makes the two costs together as small as possible.

carrying cost = inventory × rate   ·   EOQ = √(2 × yearly usage × cost per order ⁄ carrying cost per unit per year)

A second example, a different trade

A bicycle shop ordering tyres

Nyon Cycles sells 2,000 tyres a year. Each order to the importer costs CHF 50 to place and receive, whatever its size. Holding one tyre for a year costs CHF 4 (interest, shelf space, and the rubber ageing). How many tyres should each order contain?

Show the worked answer

EOQ = √(2 × 2,000 × 50 ⁄ 4) = √50,000 = 224 tyres per order, about 9 orders a year, an average stock of 112 tyres. Total cost = 9 orders × 50 + 112 tyres × 4 = CHF 894 a year. Ordering 500 at a time would cost 4 × 50 + 250 × 4 = 1,200; ordering 100 at a time, 20 × 50 + 50 × 4 = 1,200 too. The formula balances the two costs; either side of it, one of them grows faster than the other falls.

Section 6.5 · The cash budget

January to June 2026, month by month: when the money comes in, when it goes out, and how much the bank must lend

A cash budget

A forecast of cash received and cash paid, one month at a time

A cash budget lists, for each month, the cash the firm expects to receive and the cash it will have to pay, and so the balance at the end of the month. It is built from the sales forecast and the firm's own payment habits, not from the income statement: a sale made in January is cash in March if the customer pays in 60 days; timber bought in January is cash out in March if the sawmill is paid in 60 days. Where the balance would fall below the minimum cash the firm wants to keep (here 50), the difference is drawn on the bank line; where it rises above, the line is repaid. The highest draw in the period is the peak borrowing: the size of bank line the firm needs.

Rochat's assumptions for January–June 2026

  • Sales (CHF 000), from Week 5's plan of 2,472 for the year, with the winter peak when hotels renovate: Jan 260 · Feb 250 · Mar 230 · Apr 190 · May 170 · Jun 180. Sales of November and December 2025 were 190 and 210.
  • Collections: the hotels pay two months after delivery (61 days). The 400 owed at 31 December arrives 190 in January and 210 in February.
  • Suppliers: timber is 40% of the following month's sales, bought a month ahead; other supplies 20 a month; both paid two months after the invoice. The 240 owed at 31 December is paid 120 in January and 120 in February.
  • Wages 60 a month (the workshop's 480 and the office's 240 a year), paid in the month. Routine capital expenditure 12.5 a month (150 a year).
  • Interest 15 and tax 15 in March and in June. Dividend 70 in May.
  • Lausanne (Week 3): the fit-out of 400 is paid 200 in February and 200 in March; the 60 of working capital in April.
  • Opening cash 90; minimum cash 50; the bank line's current limit 200 (a course assumption; 160 of it was drawn at the year end and is treated here as part of the long-term financing, so the budget's draws start from zero).

What the table says

The operations pay for themselves. The project does not fit on the line.

Without Lausanne, the six months end with 199 of cash and nothing borrowed: the winter sales, collected in spring, cover the wages, the suppliers, the tax and the dividend. Rochat's operations are not the problem in 2026. With Lausanne paid from the line, the draw reaches 311 in March, 111 above the limit, and is still 311 in June: the 460 is not a seasonal need that the spring collections repay, it is a permanent one. Faster collection (30 days) brings the peak to 141 and would fit; stretching the suppliers to 90 days brings it to 223, and the sawmill would notice. None of these is the right answer. The right answer is section 6.6: a five-year investment is financed with five-year money.

The matching principle: finance an asset with money that lasts as long as the asset does. The 510 of working capital is partly permanent (Rochat always has about 380 of it, whatever the month) and partly seasonal (the winter peak adds 100–150 for a few months). Permanent working capital and the Lausanne fit-out are long-term assets and take long-term money; the seasonal part takes the line. A firm that finances a five-year asset on a line the bank can cancel in a month is the firm that runs out of cash in a good year.

The decision — the answer to Léa's question

The business is not running out of cash. It is financing a factory and a loan to its customers on an overdraft.

Three sentences for Léa, each with its number:

  • Where the 240 went in 2025: 280 into the router and 130 into working capital, because the hotels took 61 days to pay and the cycle lengthened from about 66 to 89 days. The profit was real; it was reinvested before it reached the bank.
  • What to change in the cycle: write new hotel contracts at 45 days and chase at 30 (frees about 104); hold the timber store to 75 days of stock (frees about 54). Together about 158 of cash, with no discount given away. Do not offer "2/10, net 60": it borrows from the hotels at 15.9% when the bank lends at 6%.
  • How to pay for Lausanne: not from the 200 line, which the budget shows reaching 311 in March and staying there. The 460 is a five-year asset; it takes a five-year loan or new equity (Week 7), and the committed line of 200 stays for the season.

A profitable business runs out of cash when it finances long-lived assets, including the loan it gives its customers, with money that must be repaid in weeks. Measure the cycle, budget the months, match the money.
