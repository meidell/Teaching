Crash course in accounting

This course reads financial statements from the first hour, and not everyone has met one before. This page is for you if you have never studied accounting. It is a learning path through one business and one year: you start from the balance sheet Atelier Rochat SA — a furniture workshop in Nyon — had on 31 December 2024, you book the twelve things that happened in 2025 one at a time, and you watch the three statements of Week 1 appear with exactly their numbers: total assets 2,000, net income 240, cash 150 → 90. Along the way each stop teaches the piece of accounting that moment needs. Nothing here is graded. It is the floor Week 1 stands on.

Seven stops, in order, each with something to do. Walk the path once from top to bottom; come back to any stop whenever a statement in class stops making sense. If you have done accounting, skim stops 1 and 7 and move on.

Stop 1 · Léa's three questions

Three questions every owner asks — and the three statements that answer them

The idea

Accounting is a way of keeping score when the game is complicated.

Léa Rochat makes furniture. Every week she buys timber, pays twelve people, delivers a table to a hotel, sends an invoice, waits to be paid, pays the bank. Money flows in and out, promises are made in both directions, and machines slowly wear out. At the end of the year she wants to know three things, and no single number can answer all three:

"What do we own, and who has a claim on it?"

2
Stop 2 · 2025 · the year, transaction by transaction

Assets = Liabilities + Equity — and how Week 1's statements got their numbers

The one rule

Everything the firm owns was paid for by somebody. The two sides are always equal.

An asset is something the firm owns that is worth money: cash, timber in the store, a machine, an invoice a customer still has to pay. The right-hand side says who provided the money for those assets. There are only two kinds of people who can: lenders (and suppliers who have not been paid yet) — their claims are called liabilities — and the owners, whose claim is called equity. Equity is simply what would be left for the owners if every asset were sold and every debt repaid.

Assets  =  Liabilities  +  Equity   ·   what we own  =  what we owe others  +  what belongs to the owners

This is not a theory that is sometimes true. It is true by construction: every transaction changes at least two lines, and it changes them so that the sides stay equal. That is what "double entry" means. Watch it happen.

Two words you will hear

Debit and credit

Accountants record every transaction as a debit to one account and a credit to another; the names come from Latin bookkeeping and mean nothing more than "left column" and "right column". A debit increases an asset or an expense; a credit increases a liability, equity or revenue. You will not need to post a single debit in this course — the statements arrive already drawn up, and your job is to read them. But when a colleague says "we credited the account", you now know they mean the right-hand side went up.

3
Stop 3 · the year's results

From sales to profit, one subtraction at a time

The staircase

Revenue at the top, net income at the bottom, and every step is a kind of cost.

The income statement covers a period — a month, a quarter, a year. It starts with revenue (also called sales or turnover: the value of what the firm delivered to customers) and subtracts costs in a fixed order, so that each intermediate line answers a different question:

  • Cost of goods sold (COGS) — what the delivered goods themselves cost: timber, varnish, the hours of the people who made them. Revenue minus COGS is gross profit: how much a franc of sales leaves after the product is paid for.
  • Selling, general & administrative (SG&A) — rent, the office, marketing, the accountant's fee: the cost of running the business whether or not a table is sold this week.
  • Depreciation — this year's share of the machines' cost (Stop 5 explains it). Gross profit minus SG&A minus depreciation is EBIT, earnings before interest and tax: what the business itself earned, before anyone asks how it was financed.
  • Interest — the cost of borrowed money. Then tax, as a percentage of what is left. The bottom line is net income — profit — which belongs to the owners.
Revenue − COGS = Gross profit  ·  − SG&A − Depreciation = EBIT  ·  − Interest = Profit before tax  ·  − Tax = Net income

4
Stop 4 · the hotel that pays in February

When does something count? Not when the money moves.

The big idea

Revenue is recorded when it is earned; a cost when it is incurred. Cash can come earlier or later.

This is the single idea that separates accounting from looking at your bank balance, and it is the reason the income statement and the cash-flow statement are two different documents. Two rules:

  • Revenue counts when the work is done and the customer owes you — when the table is delivered, not when the hotel's payment arrives sixty days later. In the meantime the firm holds an account receivable: an asset, because the hotel owes it money.
  • A cost counts in the period it helps earn revenue. Timber bought in November and still in the store at year-end is not yet a cost — it is inventory, an asset, until it is used. A supplier's invoice you have not paid is already a cost — and an account payable, a liability. A machine that will serve for ten years is not a cost this year: it is an asset, and a tenth of it becomes a cost each year as depreciation.

So four balance-sheet words are really timing differences between cash and the income statement: receivables (sold, not yet paid), payables (bought, not yet paid), inventory (bought, not yet used) and fixed assets (bought, used over many years). Every one of them will come back in Rochat's cash-flow statement at Stop 6.

Léa: "The hotel ordered twelve tables in December. I've paid for all the oak. We delivered them on the 20th. They'll pay me in February." — Revenue: December, when the tables were delivered. Cost of the oak: December too, matched to the tables it went into. Cash: oak out in November, hotel's money in February. Three different months; the income statement cares about one of them.

5
Stop 5 · the router that lasts five years

The machine that cost nothing this year — and something every year

What it is

Spreading the cost of a long-lived asset over the years it is used.

In 2025 Rochat bought a CNC router and tooling for 280 — transaction 7 at Stop 2. The cash left on day one. But the router will cut furniture for seven years, so charging all 280 against 2025's sales would make 2025 look terrible and 2026–2031 look free. Accounting instead records the router as an asset worth 280, and each year moves a slice of that value to the income statement as depreciation. The simplest method — straight-line — takes equal slices:

Depreciation per year = Cost ÷ Useful life = 280 ÷ 7 = 40  ·  Book value after t years = 280 − 40 t

Rochat's older machines are being depreciated too; the 120 on the 2025 income statement is the charge on all the equipment together. Three things follow, and all three matter later in the course. Depreciation is a real cost (the machine really is wearing out) but not a cash payment — the cash went out when the machine was bought. The book value on the balance sheet is cost minus depreciation so far, and says nothing about what the machine could be sold for. And a firm with heavy equipment can be very profitable on paper in a year in which it spent far more cash than it earned — or the reverse.

Stop 6 · 2025 · profit 240, cash down 60

Profit 240, cash down 60 — where did it go?

Three buckets

Every franc that moved is sorted into one of three activities.

  • Operating — cash from running the business: what customers actually paid, minus what was actually paid to suppliers, staff, the tax office. It starts from net income and undoes the accrual timing differences of Stop 4.
  • Investing — cash spent on long-lived assets (machines, buildings, a second site) or received from selling them. Usually negative for a growing firm.
  • Financing — cash from or to the people who fund the firm: new loans and repayments, new shares, dividends paid to the owners.

The three add up to the change in cash for the year, which must match the two balance sheets. If it does not, something is wrong — which is exactly why the statement exists.

Operating cash flow = Net income + Depreciation − Δ Receivables − Δ Inventory + Δ Payables

Read the formula in words: start from profit; add back depreciation (a cost that took no cash); subtract the increase in receivables (sales you have not been paid for yet); subtract the increase in inventory (cash sitting in timber); add the increase in payables (bills you have not paid yet are cash you still hold).

Stop 7 · reading the whole set of books

How the three statements hold together — and the words to know

One system

Three documents, one set of books. Each one feeds the next.

Income statement → Balance sheet

  • Net income that is not paid out as a dividend stays in the firm and increases equity. Rochat: equity 830 + 240 − 70 dividend = 1,000.
  • Depreciation reduces the book value of equipment.

Balance sheet → Cash-flow statement

  • The changes in receivables, inventory and payables between two balance sheets are the operating adjustments.
  • The change in equipment plus depreciation is capital expenditure: 160 + 120 = 280.

Cash-flow statement → Balance sheet

  • The three buckets sum to the change in cash: 230 − 280 − 10 = −60.
  • Opening cash + change = closing cash on the new balance sheet: 150 − 60 = 90. The loop closes.

When you read a set of accounts in this course, this is the test: do the three documents agree with each other? If equity moved by something other than profit minus dividends, or cash moved by something other than the three buckets, you have found either a transaction you do not understand or a mistake. Both are worth knowing about.

Vocabulary

Twenty words, in the course's own meaning.

Asset — something the firm owns that is worth money: cash, receivables, inventory, equipment.
Liability — a debt: money owed to a supplier, a bank or the tax office.
Equity — the owners' claim: assets minus liabilities. Grows with profit, shrinks with dividends and losses.
Current — within twelve months. Current assets turn into cash within a year; current liabilities fall due within a year.
Net working capital — current assets minus current liabilities. Rochat 2025: 840 − 400 = 440.
Revenue (sales, turnover) — the value of what was delivered to customers in the period, paid or not.
COGS — cost of goods sold: what the delivered goods themselves cost to make.
Gross profit — revenue minus COGS. As a share of revenue: the gross margin.
SG&A — selling, general and administrative costs; the overhead of running the business.
EBIT — earnings before interest and tax; what the business earned regardless of how it is financed.
Net income (profit, earnings) — the bottom line, after interest and tax; belongs to the owners.
Accrual — recording revenue when earned and costs when incurred, not when cash moves.
Accounts receivable — invoices customers have not paid yet. An asset.
Accounts payable — supplier invoices the firm has not paid yet. A liability.
Inventory — raw materials, work in progress and finished goods not yet sold. An asset.
Depreciation — this year's slice of a long-lived asset's cost. A cost with no cash payment.
Book value — an asset's cost minus accumulated depreciation; not its market value.
Capital expenditure (capex) — cash spent on long-lived assets. Investing cash flow.
Dividend — profit paid out to the owners. Financing cash flow; reduces equity.
Free cash flow — operating cash flow minus capex: what is left for lenders and owners. Rochat 2025: 230 − 280 = −50. Week 3 lives on this number.
