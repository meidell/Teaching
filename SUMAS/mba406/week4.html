Risk and the cost of capital

Last week Lausanne was worth +118 at a hurdle rate of 10% that nobody explained. Léa's answer: "My father always used ten per cent." This week you build the number yourself: what risk means for an investor, why diversification removes some of it and not the rest, how beta and the CAPM turn the rest into a required return, what Rochat's debt costs after tax, and how the two combine into a weighted-average cost of capital. Then the decision: what rate should the Lausanne site clear, and does it?

The session is three hours in two parts. Part 1 (~1 h) is the lesson: your instructor presents the deck and you follow the same numbered sections here, and every slide names its section. Part 2 (~2 h) is nine exercises worked together, given on the slides and as forms below; solutions open as your instructor reveals them.

Section 4.1 · The hook

"My father always used ten per cent"

The running case

Where does the 10% come from?

Week 3 ended with Lausanne worth +117.6 at 10%, +87.0 at 12%, +58.8 at 14%, and nothing at its IRR of 18.8%. The rate is not a detail. You asked Léa where her 10% comes from.

"My father ran the workshop for thirty years and he used ten per cent for everything: a new saw, a van, a second showroom. The bank charges us six. Ten felt safe. Why would it be any different for Lausanne?" — Léa, Tuesday

She is asking three questions without knowing it: what return do Rochat's owners require, what does its debt cost, and should one rate fit every project? First, your instinct.

Your instinct

What would you need to earn to put your own savings into Rochat?

Imagine you have CHF 100,000 and two choices for the next five years: a Swiss government bond paying about 1% a year, or a minority stake in Atelier Rochat, whose profits swing with the hotel trade. Set the yearly return the stake would have to promise before you would choose it, and lock it.

The yearly return I would require from a stake in Rochat
10.0% a year
The one idea

The cost of capital is an opportunity cost: what investors could earn elsewhere at the same risk.

Nobody sends Rochat a bill for its equity. The owners' money costs what they give up by leaving it in the workshop instead of an investment of the same risk. So the rate has two parts: the return on a safe investment, which compensates for waiting, plus a risk premium, which compensates for the risk. This week is about measuring the second part.

required return = risk-free rate + risk premium

Everything else this week — standard deviation, diversification, beta, the CAPM, the WACC — is a way of putting a number on the risk premium that the market would actually pay.

The company paid 10 points a year more on average. It also lost money in two years out of five. To compare the extra return with the extra risk, the risk needs a number.

By hand

The standard deviation of the company's returns

One press per step.

Furniture company · five yearly returns
variance = Σ (r − r̄)² ⁄ (n − 1)   ·   standard deviation σ = √variance

Dividing by n − 1 is the usual choice for a sample of past years, and what Excel's STDEV.S does. For scenarios with probabilities (Exercise 1), each squared deviation is weighted by its probability instead.

On Rochat → the company earned a risk premium of 10 points for a standard deviation of 17.8 points. That ratio, return per unit of risk, is what investors trade. The question for the next section is which risk they are paid for.

Section 4.3 · Diversification

Some risk disappears in a portfolio. The rest does not.

Two kinds of risk

Specific risk diversifies away; market risk does not.

Specific (unique) risk belongs to one company: a hotel client goes bankrupt, a joiner leaves, a router breaks. In a portfolio of many shares these surprises are unrelated and largely cancel. Market (systematic) risk hits every company at once: a recession, a rate rise, a strong franc. It does not cancel, however many shares you hold.

An investor can remove specific risk for free by diversifying. The market therefore does not pay a premium for bearing it: only market risk earns a risk premium.

Léa is not diversified

The market pays for market risk. Léa carries both.

Almost all of Léa's wealth is in Rochat. For her, a lost hotel client is not a small surprise that cancels against others; it is the whole year. That is a real cost to her, but the CAPM will not price it, because a diversified buyer of Rochat would not pay for it. Owners of small firms often add a premium on top of the CAPM rate for this reason. Treat that premium as a judgement you state openly, not as part of the cost of capital.

How much market risk — and what it is worth

Rochat has no share price

So we borrow a beta from a listed company in the same trade.

Rochat is not listed, so its own beta cannot be measured. The usual route is a listed company in the same business. Five years of returns, a comparable furniture maker against the market index (illustrative numbers, in per cent):

By hand

Beta: how far the share moves when the market moves one point

Beta from five years of returns
β = covariance(share, market) ⁄ variance(market)   ·   β = 1 moves with the market; above 1 amplifies it; below 1 dampens it
The CAPM

Required return = risk-free rate + beta × market risk premium

rE = rf + β × (rm − rf)   ·   Rochat: 1% + 1.5 × 6% = 10.0%

rf is the return on a safe government bond (we use 1%, a rounded course assumption for Swiss francs). rm − rf is the extra return investors have historically demanded for holding the whole market (we use 6%, an assumption within the range the textbook discusses). β scales that premium to the share's market risk. Rochat's equity beta is taken as 1.5: the comparable's.

A second example, a different trade

A Swiss electricity distributor and a software company

A regulated distributor's profits barely move with the economy: β = 0.6. A software company's swing with corporate budgets: β = 1.4. Same rf and premium. Their costs of equity?

Show the worked answer

Distributor: 1% + 0.6 × 6% = 4.6%. Software: 1% + 1.4 × 6% = 9.4%. A franc of their profit is worth more for the distributor, because investors accept less to hold it. The same project — say a data centre — would be judged at a lower rate inside the distributor, and that would be a mistake: the rate belongs to the project's risk, not the firm's (section 4.6).

Section 4.5 · The cost of debt and the WACC

Two kinds of money, one rate

The cost of debt

The rate on new borrowing, after tax — not last year's interest bill

Rochat's 2025 accounts show 60 of interest on 760 of debt (600 long-term, 160 on the bank line): 60 ⁄ 760 = 7.9%. That is history, partly an old loan and an expensive overdraft. The cost of debt for a new project is what Rochat would pay to borrow now: the bank quoted 6% for the router in Week 2.

Interest is deductible, so every franc of interest saves 20 centimes of tax. The cost that matters is after tax:

rD after tax = rD × (1 − T) = 6% × 0.8 = 4.8%
The weighted-average cost of capital

Weight each cost by its share of the money

WACC = E ⁄ V × rE + D ⁄ V × rD × (1 − T)   ·   Rochat: 0.70 × 10% + 0.30 × 4.8% = 8.44%

The weights should be market values or the firm's target mix, not book values: the owners' stake is worth what a buyer would pay, not the 1,000 of book equity. Rochat has no share price, so we use the mix Léa and the bank have agreed to aim for: 30% debt, 70% equity. (With book weights, 760 debt and 1,000 equity, the WACC would be 7.75%, and it would change every time the accountant revalued a machine.)

Section 4.6 · Company or project cost of capital — and the decision

The rate belongs to the project, not to the firm

Company cost of capital

8.44% is right for a project as risky as Rochat's average business.

The WACC is the return Rochat's investors require on the firm as it is: a mix of hotel contracts, private clients and repair work. A project with the same risk should clear 8.44%. A riskier one should clear more; a safer one less. Using one rate for everything makes the firm accept too many risky projects and turn down safe ones.

Lausanne is riskier than the average Rochat business

New market, new clients, fixed rent and joiners

Lausanne's sales depend on hotels Rochat has not yet signed, and most of its costs — rent, two joiners — do not fall when sales do. That is more market risk, not less. A beta of 1.8 for the new site, against 1.5 for Rochat as a whole, is a reasonable judgement. Same financing mix.

The decision — and what the number leaves out

Lausanne should clear about 9.7%. It does, by a wide margin.

At its own rate, 9.70%, Lausanne is worth +122; its IRR of 18.8% is twice the hurdle. Léa's father's 10% was close, for a reason he never stated: Lausanne is somewhat riskier than Rochat's average business, and the market inputs happen to land there. Week 3's recommendation stands, and now it has a rate behind it. Still outside the number:

  • The inputs are judgements. rf 1%, a 6% premium and a borrowed beta are assumptions; a range (9–11%) is more honest than two decimals.
  • Léa is not diversified. If she adds a premium for having everything in Rochat, say so, and show the NPV with and without it.
  • Rates move. A 2022-style rise lifts the rate to about 10.3%; Lausanne still clears it. The sales line from Week 3 is still the bigger risk.
  • The mix moves. More debt changes the cost of equity too. Week 7.

The rate is not a family tradition. It is what an investor would give up to fund this project instead of another of the same risk.
