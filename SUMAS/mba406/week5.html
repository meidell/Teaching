Valuation: what is the business worth?

Week 4 gave Rochat a rate: 8.44% for the business as it is, 9.70% for Lausanne. This week the rate puts a price on things: a loan or a bond, a share, and then the whole company, from the cash it will generate. The reason is a letter on Léa's desk: a hotel-furnishing group from Lausanne offers CHF 2.5 million for all of Rochat's shares, two and a half times the book equity of 1,000. The decision: what is the business worth to a buyer, and is 2.5 million enough?

The one idea

Anything is worth the present value of the cash it will pay, at a rate that matches its risk.

A loan, a bond, a share and a whole company are valued the same way. What changes from one to the next is which cash flows you count and which rate you discount them at. Book equity is the money the owners put in and left in the business over the years; it says nothing about the cash still to come.

value = CF1 ⁄ (1 + r) + CF2 ⁄ (1 + r)² + … + CFn ⁄ (1 + r)n

This week applies the formula four times, from the simplest claim to the whole business: a bond, a loan, a share, and Rochat.

Section 5.2 · Pricing a loan or a bond

Fixed payments, a price that moves

A bond

A promise of fixed payments: coupons every year, the face value at the end

A bond with a face value of 100 and a coupon of 3% pays 3 a year and 100 at maturity. Those payments never change. What changes is the return investors require today on a claim of that risk and length, the yield to maturity. The price is the present value of the payments at that yield.

price = coupon × [1 − 1 ⁄ (1 + y)n] ⁄ y + face ⁄ (1 + y)n   ·   an annuity (Week 2) plus one single payment

A second example: a loan is a bond too

Week 2's router loan, the day after rates rise

In Week 2 the bank lent Rochat 120,000 at 6%, repaid in 60 monthly payments of 2,319.94. At 6% those payments are worth exactly 120,000. Suppose that the day after signing, banks start charging 8% for the same loan. What is Rochat's loan now worth to the bank?

Show the worked answer

The payments do not change; the rate used to value them does. At 8% a year, 0.6667% a month, the 60-payment annuity factor is 49.318433. Value = 2,319.94 × 49.318433 = 114,415.62. The bank has lost 5,584 on paper: it could now lend the same money at 8%. Rochat has gained the same amount, because it locked in 6%. A fixed-rate loan is a bond the bank holds.

On Rochat → in the valuation in section 5.5 Rochat's debt of 760 is taken at book value. Its rates are close to what new money costs (Week 4), so its market value is close to 760. If rates had moved a lot, you would value the debt the way you just valued the router loan.

Section 5.3 · Valuing a share

The present value of the dividends

A share

No promise, no maturity: dividends that may grow, and a price you can sell at

An investor who buys a share today and sells it in a year receives the dividend and the price at that time. Discounted at the return shareholders require, rE from the CAPM in Week 4, that gives today's price. Next year's price depends in the same way on the year after, and so on: the price is the present value of all future dividends. If the dividend is expected to grow at a constant rate g, smaller than rE, the sum has a short form.

P0 = (DIV1 + P1) ⁄ (1 + rE)   →   P0 = DIV1 ⁄ (rE − g)   (constant growth, g < rE)

Rochat through its dividends

The dividend model says 1,030. It is the wrong model for Rochat.

Rochat paid 70 in dividends in 2025. Growing at 3%, at Rochat's cost of equity of 10%: 70 × 1.03 ⁄ (0.10 − 0.03) = 1,030, almost exactly the book equity. But Rochat paid out only 70 of its 240 of profit and reinvested the other 170, at a return on equity of 24%. A firm that reinvests 71% of its profit at 24% would grow its dividends by about 0.24 × 0.71 = 17% a year, faster than the 10% required return, and the formula stops working (g must be below rE).

The constant-growth dividend model suits a mature firm that pays out most of what it earns. For a firm that keeps most of its profit to grow, value the cash the whole business generates, before anything is paid out: its free cash flow.

Section 5.4 · Free cash flow

The cash the business generates for all its investors

Free cash flow

Operating profit after tax, plus depreciation, minus the investment the business needs

Free cash flow is the cash the business produces after paying its costs and taxes and after the investment it needs, in machines and in working capital. It is what is left for all the investors, lenders and owners together. Interest is left out, as it was in Week 3: the cost of the money is in the discount rate, the WACC. So tax is computed on EBIT, as if the firm had no debt.

FCF = EBIT × (1 − T) + depreciation − capital expenditure − increase in working capital

Working capital here is the operating part: receivables plus inventory minus payables. Cash and the bank line are financing, not operations.

Section 5.5 · DCF valuation of the company

Five forecast years, a terminal value, one rate

The plan for the Nyon business

Léa's forecast, without Lausanne

The assumptions come from Léa and her accountant. Each is a judgement, and the widget below lets you change it:

  • Sales grow 3% a year from 2,400, for five years (2026–2030).
  • The EBIT margin stays at 2025's 15% (360 ⁄ 2,400). Tax 20%.
  • Depreciation 120 a year; capital expenditure 150 a year, to replace machines and add a little.
  • Working capital stays at 2025's share of sales, 510 ⁄ 2,400 = 21.25%, so it grows with sales.
  • After 2030, free cash flow grows 2% a year forever, about the rate of inflation plus a little.
  • The rate is the company WACC, 8.44% (Week 4): the Nyon business is Rochat's average risk.
The method

Enterprise value = present value of the forecast FCF + present value of the terminal value

EV = Σ FCFt ⁄ (1 + WACC)t + TV5 ⁄ (1 + WACC)5   ·   TV5 = FCF6 ⁄ (WACC − g)   ·   equity = EV − debt + cash

The terminal value is the constant-growth formula of section 5.3, applied to free cash flow at the end of the forecast. Discounting free cash flow at the WACC values the whole business, the enterprise value, which belongs to lenders and owners together. The owners get what is left after the debt is repaid, plus the cash.

Section 5.6 · A multiples cross-check — and the decision

What similar businesses sell for

Valuation multiples

Price a business by what investors pay for similar ones

A multiple divides a value by one line of the accounts. EV/EBITDA compares the enterprise value with operating profit before depreciation: it ignores how the firm is financed, so it is compared with the enterprise value. P/E compares the equity value with net income. The multiples of listed firms in the same trade give a range. For this course, take listed furniture and fit-out companies at 7× to 9× EBITDA and 12× to 16× earnings: illustrative assumptions, not market data.

EV = EBITDA × (EV ⁄ EBITDA of peers)   ·   equity = net income × (P ⁄ E of peers)

A chain of bakeries around Geneva

EBITDA 1,200, net income 450, net debt 2,000. Listed food retailers trade around 7× EBITDA and 14× earnings. Its equity by each multiple?

Show the worked answer

EV = 1,200 × 7 = 8,400; equity = 8,400 − 2,000 = 6,400. By earnings: 450 × 14 = 6,300. The two agree within 2%. When they do not, the usual reason is debt: P/E moves with leverage and EV/EBITDA does not, so compare like with like, and look at what is different about the firm.

The decision — and what the number leaves out

2.5 million is below every estimate. Rochat is worth about 3.4 million to Léa.

The DCF gives 3,413 for the Nyon business alone and 3,535 with Lausanne; half a point either way on the WACC gives 3,117 to 3,759. The multiples give 2,690 to 3,840. The offer of 2,500 is below all of them. Book equity, 1,000, is not a value at all. A reasoned answer is a counter-offer around 3.4 to 3.5 million, with the DCF behind it. Still outside the number:

  • Three quarters of the value is the terminal value. The forecast for 2031 onwards matters more than the next five years. Show the range, not one figure.
  • Rochat may be worth more to this buyer. It already buys from Rochat and could add its hotel network: those gains (synergies) are the buyer's reason to pay above the stand-alone value, and the room Léa can negotiate in.
  • Léa is not diversified (Week 4). Selling would spread her wealth. That is a real reason for her to accept somewhat less than the DCF value; it should be stated as such, not hidden in the valuation.
  • The buyer will argue for a discount because Rochat is small and unlisted. That is a negotiating position about the price, not a correction of the value.

Book value is what was put in. Value is what will come out, discounted at the rate its risk deserves.
