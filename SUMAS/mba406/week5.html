Valuation: what is the business worth?

In Week 4 you computed the return Rochat's money must earn: 8.44% a year for the business as it is (its WACC), and 9.70% for the riskier Lausanne project. This week that rate is used to value things. To value something is to compute how much its future cash payments are worth today. You will value a bond, a loan, a share, and then the whole company. The reason is a letter: a hotel-furnishing group from Lausanne offers CHF 2.5 million in cash for all of Rochat's shares. Rochat's balance sheet shows equity of CHF 1 million, so the offer is two and a half times that figure. The decision for Léa: is CHF 2.5 million more or less than the business is worth?

Section 5.1 · The offer

An offer of CHF 2.5 million for a company whose balance sheet shows CHF 1 million

The running case

An offer for the whole company

A hotel-furnishing group from Lausanne, which already buys from Rochat for two of its hotels, has written to Léa. It offers CHF 2.5 million in cash for 100% of the shares. Rochat's balance sheet shows shareholders' equity of 1,000 (all figures in this course are in CHF 000, so 1,000 means CHF 1 million). Shareholders' equity, also called book equity, is an accounting figure: the share capital the owners paid in, plus the profits the company kept since it started, minus the dividends it paid out. It records what happened in the past. It is not a measure of what the company will earn.

"Our books say the company is worth one million. They offer two and a half. My accountant says take it before they change their mind. My heart says no. Which one is right?" — Léa, Monday

The one idea

The value of anything is the present value of the cash it will pay, at a rate that matches its risk

The present value of a future payment is the amount you would need today, invested at a given rate, to have that payment on the day it arrives (Week 2). Every valuation this week is the same computation: list the cash payments the asset will make, choose the rate that fits their risk, compute the present value of each payment, and add them up. A loan, a bond, a share and a whole company are all valued this way. What changes from one to the next is which cash flows you count and which rate you discount them at. Book equity is not part of this computation: it records the money the owners paid in and the profits kept in the past, and says nothing about the cash the company will produce in the future.

value = CF1 ⁄ (1 + r) + CF2 ⁄ (1 + r)² + … + CFn ⁄ (1 + r)n

This week applies the formula four times, from the simplest case to the hardest: a bond, a loan, a share, and the whole of Rochat.

Section 5.2 · Pricing a loan or a bond

A bond pays fixed amounts, and its price changes when interest rates change

A bond

A bond is a loan from many investors at once, with a fixed payment every year and the amount repaid at the end

A bond is a loan that a company or a government takes from many investors at the same time. The borrower promises two things: a fixed payment every year, the coupon, and the repayment of the amount borrowed, the face value, on a fixed date, the maturity. A bond with a face value of 100 and a coupon of 3% pays 3 every year and 100 at maturity. Those payments never change. What changes is the return investors require today for lending money of that risk for that length of time: this required return is the yield to maturity, written y. The price of the bond is the present value of its payments at that yield.

price = coupon × [1 − 1 ⁄ (1 + y)n] ⁄ y + face ⁄ (1 + y)n

The first part values the coupons: they are an annuity, the same payment every year for n years (Week 2). The second part values the face value: one single payment in year n.

A second example: a loan is a bond too

Week 2's router loan, the day after rates rise

In Week 2 the bank lent Rochat 120,000 at 6%, repaid in 60 monthly payments of 2,319.94. At 6% those payments are worth exactly 120,000. Suppose that the day after signing, banks start charging 8% for the same loan. What is Rochat's loan now worth to the bank?

Show the worked answer

The payments do not change; the rate used to value them does. At 8% a year, 0.6667% a month, the 60-payment annuity factor is 49.318433. Value = 2,319.94 × 49.318433 = 114,415.62. The bank has lost 5,584 on paper: it could now lend the same money at 8%. Rochat has gained the same amount, because it locked in 6%. A fixed-rate loan is a bond the bank holds.

On Rochat → in the valuation in section 5.5, Rochat's debt of 760 is taken at the amount shown on the balance sheet. Its interest rates are close to what a new loan would cost today (Week 4), so what the debt is worth to the bank is close to 760. If rates had moved a lot, you would revalue the debt the way you just revalued the router loan.

Section 5.3 · Valuing a share

The present value of the dividends

A share

A share has no fixed payments and no end date: its value is the dividends it will pay

A share is a part of the ownership of a company. Its owner receives a dividend, the part of the year's profit the company pays out to its owners, when the company decides to pay one; nothing is promised and there is no end date. An investor who buys a share today and sells it in a year receives one dividend and the sale price. Discount both at the return shareholders require, rE (from the CAPM in Week 4; 10% for the listed furniture maker below), and you have today's price. Next year's price depends in the same way on the year after, and so on, so the price today is the present value of all the dividends the share will ever pay. If the dividend is expected to grow at the same rate g every year, and g is smaller than rE, the sum of all those present values has a short form: next year's dividend divided by the gap between the required return and the growth rate.

P0 = (DIV1 + P1) ⁄ (1 + rE)   →   P0 = DIV1 ⁄ (rE − g)   (constant growth, g < rE)

The dividend model says 1,030. It is the wrong model for Rochat.

Rochat paid 70 in dividends in 2025. If that dividend grew 3% a year, at Rochat's cost of equity of 10% (Week 4): 70 × 1.03 ⁄ (0.10 − 0.03) = 1,030, almost exactly the book equity. But Rochat paid out only 70 of its 240 of profit and kept the other 170 in the business, where it earns a return on equity of 24% (profit 240 ÷ equity 1,000). A firm that keeps 71% of its profit and earns 24% on it would grow its profit, and so its dividends, by about 0.24 × 0.71 = 17% a year. That is faster than the 10% required return, and the formula has no answer (g must be below rE).

The constant-growth dividend model suits a mature firm that pays out most of what it earns. For a firm that keeps most of its profit to grow, value the cash the whole business generates, before anything is paid out: its free cash flow.

Section 5.4 · Free cash flow

The cash the business generates for all its investors

Free cash flow

Operating profit after tax, plus depreciation, minus the investment the business needs

Free cash flow is the cash the business produces in a year after paying its operating costs and its taxes, and after the investment it needs to keep running and growing. It is what is left for all the investors together, lenders and owners. Four terms in the formula:

  • EBIT is operating profit: the profit before interest and tax, from the income statement.
  • Depreciation is the part of the cost of machines and buildings charged against profit each year. It was subtracted to compute EBIT, but no cash leaves the firm for it, so it is added back.
  • Capital expenditure (capex) is the cash spent on machines and buildings in the year.
  • Working capital is receivables (what customers owe the firm) plus inventory, minus payables (what the firm owes its suppliers). When it grows, cash is tied up in unpaid invoices and stock. Cash in the bank and the bank line are financing, not operations, so they are not part of it.
FCF = EBIT × (1 − T) + depreciation − capital expenditure − increase in working capital

Interest is left out, as it was in Week 3: the cost of borrowed money is already in the discount rate, the WACC. So tax is computed on EBIT, as if the firm had no debt.

Section 5.5 · DCF valuation of the company

Five forecast years, a terminal value, one rate

The plan for the Nyon business

Léa's forecast, without Lausanne

The assumptions come from Léa and her accountant. Each one is an estimate, not a fact; the table further down lets you change any of them and watch the value move.

  • Sales grow 3% a year from 2,400, for five years (2026–2030).
  • The EBIT margin stays at 2025's 15% (360 ⁄ 2,400). Tax 20%.
  • Depreciation 120 a year; capital expenditure 150 a year, to replace machines and add a little.
  • Working capital stays at 2025's share of sales, 510 ⁄ 2,400 = 21.25%, so it grows with sales.
  • After 2030, free cash flow grows 2% a year forever, about the rate of inflation plus a little.
  • The rate is the company WACC, 8.44% (Week 4): the Nyon business is Rochat's average risk.
The method

Enterprise value = present value of the forecast FCF + present value of the terminal value

EV = Σ FCFt ⁄ (1 + WACC)t + TV5 ⁄ (1 + WACC)5   ·   TV5 = FCF6 ⁄ (WACC − g)   ·   equity = EV − debt + cash

The forecast stops after five years, but the business goes on. The terminal value is one number that stands for all the free cash flows after 2030: the constant-growth formula of section 5.3 applied to free cash flow, computed as of the end of 2030, then discounted back five years like any other payment made in 2030. Free cash flow belongs to lenders and owners together, so discounting it at the WACC gives the value of the whole business, the enterprise value (EV). The owners get what is left after the debt is repaid, plus the cash in the bank: that is the equity value, the figure to compare with the offer for the shares.

Section 5.6 · A multiples cross-check — and the decision

What similar businesses sell for

Valuation multiples

Price a business from what investors pay for similar listed businesses

A valuation multiple is a value divided by one line of the accounts. The idea: if similar listed companies sell for 8 times their EBITDA, a buyer will pay about 8 times Rochat's EBITDA too. EBITDA is EBIT plus depreciation, the operating profit before the yearly charge for machines (Rochat 2025: 360 + 120 = 480). EV/EBITDA divides the enterprise value by EBITDA; both are measured before interest, so the ratio does not depend on how the firm is financed. P/E, the price–earnings ratio, divides the equity value by net income (Rochat 2025: 240); it gives the equity directly. Listed firms in the same trade give a range of multiples. For this course, listed furniture and fit-out companies are taken at 7× to 9× EBITDA and 12× to 16× earnings. These two ranges are course assumptions, not market data.

Where the two ranges come from. A multiple is a shortcut for a DCF. For a business growing at a steady rate g, EV = FCF ⁄ (WACC − g); divide both sides by EBITDA and the multiple is (FCF ⁄ EBITDA) ⁄ (WACC − g). For Rochat, free cash flow in a normal year is about 250 of 480 of EBITDA, a share of 0.52; at a WACC of 8.44% and growth of 2%: 0.52 ⁄ 0.0644 = 8.1×. A buyer with a slightly higher rate or lower growth lands near 7×, a more optimistic one near 9×: that is the range. For the owners, P ⁄ E = 1 ⁄ (rE − g) when a franc of profit is worth a franc to them: 1 ⁄ (0.10 − 0.03) = 14.3×, so 12× to 16×. The two ranges agree with each other: 8 × 480 − 670 = 3,170, divided by 240, is 13.2× earnings. In practice an analyst reads the multiples of five to ten listed companies in the same trade from today's share prices and takes the middle; listed companies are larger and easier to sell than Rochat, so a buyer argues for the low end.

EV = EBITDA × (EV ⁄ EBITDA of peers)   ·   equity = net income × (P ⁄ E of peers)

The decision — and what the number leaves out

2.5 million is below every estimate. Rochat is worth about 3.4 million to Léa.

The discounted cash flow (DCF) gives 3,413 for the Nyon business alone and 3,535 with Lausanne; a WACC half a point lower or higher (7.94% or 8.94%) gives 3,759 or 3,117. The multiples give 2,690 to 3,840. The offer of 2,500 is below all of them. Book equity, 1,000, is an accounting record of the past, not a value. A reasoned answer is a counter-offer around 3.4 to 3.5 million, with the DCF as the justification. Four things the number does not contain:

  • Three quarters of the value is the terminal value. The assumptions about 2031 onwards (2% growth, the WACC) matter more than the five forecast years. Give the buyer a range, 3,117 to 3,759, not one figure.
  • Rochat may be worth more to this buyer than to Léa. The buyer already purchases from Rochat and could sell Rochat's furniture to its whole hotel network. Extra profit that exists only if the two companies are combined is called a synergy. It is the buyer's reason to pay more than the stand-alone value of 3,413, and the room Léa has to negotiate.
  • Léa is not diversified (Week 4): almost all her wealth is in this one company. Selling would let her spread it. That is a real reason for her to accept somewhat less than the DCF value; it should be said openly, not hidden inside the valuation.
  • The buyer will argue for a lower price because Rochat is small and its shares are not listed on a stock exchange, so they are hard to resell. That is a negotiating argument about the price, not a correction of the value.

The book equity of 1,000 records what the owners paid in and kept in the company in the past. The value of the company is the present value of the cash it will produce in the future, at the rate its risk requires: about 3,400.
