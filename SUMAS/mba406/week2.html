Week 2 · Managerial Finance · SUMAS

The time value of money

Rochat's CNC router is at capacity. The supplier offers a new one on a lease; the bank offers a loan to buy it. The two quotes are a few francs apart per month, and Léa has already decided. This week you learn the one idea the rest of finance is built on — a franc today is not a franc next year — and the four tools that follow from it: future value, present value, annuities and effective rates. By the end you can price any stream of payments in today's francs, and tell Léa which quote is actually cheaper.

The session is three hours in two parts. Part 1 (~1 h) is the lesson: your instructor presents the deck and you follow the same numbered sections here — every slide names its section. Part 2 (~2 h) is nine exercises worked together, given on the slides and as forms below; solutions open as your instructor reveals them.

🏫 In class: 3 h · lesson ~1 h, then exercises ~2 h · bring a calculator 📖 Textbook: Brealey, Myers & Marcus, ch. 5 — The Time Value of Money ▶ The Lecture button (top-right) shows the deck we use in class

By the end of Week 2 you will be able to

  • Say why a franc today is worth more than a franc tomorrow, and what that does to any comparison of cash flows at different dates.
  • Compute a future value with compound interest and a present value with a discount factor — for a single sum and for a stream.
  • Value an annuity and a perpetuity, including a growing one — and turn a loan into its monthly payment.
  • Convert a quoted rate (APR) into the effective annual rate, and know which one to compare.
  • Build a loan amortisation schedule and read interest and principal off it.
  • Compare a lease and a loan in present-value terms, and say what the comparison leaves out.

Section 2.1 · The hook

"They're the same price — and the lease keeps debt off the books"

The running case

The router is full. Léa has two quotes.

The workshop's CNC router runs two shifts and the hotel orders keep coming. A second, five-axis machine costs CHF 120,000. Two offers are on the bench:

  • The supplier's lease: CHF 2,400 a month for 60 months, then buy the machine for CHF 12,000 — or hand it back.
  • The bank's loan: CHF 120,000 at 6% a year, repaid in 60 equal monthly instalments. The bank's letter says the instalment is CHF 2,319.94.
"They're the same — 2,400 or 2,320, eighty francs a month, who cares. And the lease isn't a loan, so it keeps debt off the balance sheet, which after last week I gather we want. Let's lease." — Léa, Thursday

Two streams of monthly payments, one of them with a lump sum five years out. Léa is comparing them by adding up the francs — and francs paid in 2031 are not the same thing as francs paid next month. The week starts with why.

Your instinct

CHF 100,000 today, or CHF 110,000 in two years?

A client offers to pay for a fitted library either now or, with a bonus, in two years. Which would you take? It depends on one number: what you could earn on the money meanwhile. Set the yearly return you think Rochat could get, and see which offer wins.

Return Rochat could earn on 100,000 for two years
3.0% a year
The one idea

A franc today is worth more than a franc tomorrow — because it can be put to work.

Not because of inflation (that is a separate, smaller effect) and not because tomorrow is uncertain (also separate). Simply: CHF 100 today can be invested and become more than CHF 100 next year. So cash flows that fall at different dates are different goods, and you cannot add them up until you have moved them to the same date. Moving money forward in time is compounding; moving it back is discounting. The rate you move it at is the return the money could earn elsewhere — the opportunity cost of capital.

Never add francs from different dates. Move them to one date first — usually today.

Every valuation in this course — a loan, a bond, a share, a second workshop — is this sentence applied with care.

Section 2.2 · Future value

Compounding: interest on the interest

The formula

FV = PV × (1 + r)t

Put CHF 50,000 in at 4%. After one year it is 50,000 × 1.04 = 52,000. After two, it is 52,000 × 1.04 — the second year's interest is earned on the first year's interest too. That is compound interest, and after t years the multiplier is (1 + r)t. Simple interest, by contrast, pays 4% of the original 50,000 every year and never on the interest — the straight line below.

FV = PV × (1 + r)t   ·   50,000 × 1.043 = 50,000 × 1.124864 = 56,243.20

Section 2.3 · Present value

Discounting: what is a future franc worth today?

The formula, run backwards

PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)t — the discount factor is 1 ÷ (1 + r)t

If 50,000 grows to 56,243.20 in three years at 4%, then 56,243.20 in three years is worth exactly 50,000 today — that is the only amount you would need to set aside to have it. Dividing by (1 + r)t is discounting; the number you divide by, written as a multiplier, is the discount factor. At 6% over five years it is 1 ÷ 1.06⁵ = 0.7473: a franc in five years is worth 74.7 centimes today.

PV = FV × DF   ·   DFr,t = 1 ÷ (1 + r)t   ·   200,000 × 0.7473 = 149,451.63
On Rochat → a hotel group offers CHF 200,000 for a fitted library, payable on delivery in five years. If Rochat can earn 6% on its money, that promise is worth CHF 149,451.63 today — and that is the price to compare with a client who would pay 155,000 now.
A stream

Several cash flows: discount each one, then add.

Three instalments — 40,000 next year, 50,000 the year after, 60,000 the year after that. Each one gets its own discount factor; only then may they be added. Drag the rate and watch the far instalments shrink faster than the near ones.

Section 2.4 · Annuities and perpetuities

Level payments, and the shortcut for valuing them

The annuity

The same amount every period, for a fixed number of periods.

A loan instalment, a lease, a pension, rent: finance is full of level streams. You could discount sixty monthly payments one by one. The shortcut is the annuity factor — the present value of CHF 1 per period for n periods at rate r:

PV of an annuity = C × 1 − (1 + r)−n⁄r   ·   the fraction is the annuity factor

Turn it round and it prices a loan: a payment C that repays principal P over n periods is C = P ÷ annuity factor. Rochat's loan: 6% a year is 0.5% a month; over 60 months the factor is 51.7256; 120,000 ÷ 51.7256 = 2,319.94. That is where the bank's letter got its number.

The perpetuity

A level payment forever — and the simplest formula in finance.

PV of a perpetuity = C ⁄ r   ·   growing at g: C₁ ⁄ (r − g)   ·   annuity due (paid at the start): ordinary PV × (1 + r)

A maintenance contract that costs CHF 3,000 a year forever, at 5%: 3,000 ÷ 0.05 = 60,000. If the fee rises 2% a year: 3,000 ÷ (0.05 − 0.02) = 100,000. Infinite payments, finite value — because the distant ones are discounted to almost nothing. And note the annuity factor is just a perpetuity with its tail cut off: 1⁄r minus the present value of a perpetuity starting at year n.

On Rochat → the lease is paid at the end of each month in the supplier's quote. Had it been at the start — an annuity due — every payment would arrive one month earlier and the whole stream would be worth 0.5% more. Read the contract before you reach for the formula.

Section 2.5 · Nominal versus effective rates

"6% a year" — but compounded how often?

APR and EAR

The quoted rate is a convention. The effective rate is what you pay.

Banks quote an annual percentage rate (APR) and then compound it monthly: 6% APR means 0.5% a month, and twelve months of 0.5% compound to 1.005¹² − 1 = 6.17%. That 6.17% is the effective annual rate (EAR) — the rate you would need with annual compounding to end up in the same place. Two loans can carry the same APR and different EARs; only the EAR is comparable.

EAR = (1 + APR ⁄ m)m − 1   ·   6% monthly: 1.00512 − 1 = 6.168%  ·  18% monthly: 1.01512 − 1 = 19.56%

More frequent compounding always raises the effective rate, but with diminishing returns: monthly to daily is worth less than a basis point and a half on 6%. The trap is not the size of the gap; it is comparing an APR with an EAR.

Real versus nominal — the other adjustment

Inflation is a separate correction, not the reason for discounting.

If Rochat earns 6% and prices rise 2%, its money buys 1.06 ÷ 1.02 − 1 = 3.92% more — roughly 6 − 2 = 4%, and exactly that only when both rates are small. Keep the rule simple: discount nominal cash flows at nominal rates, real cash flows at real rates, and never mix. Every number this week is nominal.

Section 2.6 · Loan amortisation — and the decision

Inside a level payment, and lease versus borrow

The schedule

Each instalment is part interest, part principal — and the mix shifts every month.

What the comparison leaves out

The present-value answer is right. It is not yet complete.

  • Who owns the machine at the end. With the loan Rochat owns it outright after five years; with the lease it pays 12,000 more or hands it back. Both were in the numbers — but the value of a five-year-old router was not. Week 3's cash-flow thinking covers residual values.
  • What the lease includes. Supplier leases often bundle maintenance and insurance. If so, the 2,400 is buying more than the machine, and the comparison needs the loan side to add those costs.
  • Tax. Lease payments and loan interest plus depreciation are deductible in different patterns. Week 3 and Week 7.
  • "Off the balance sheet". Léa's instinct is twenty years out of date: a five-year lease with a purchase option is a liability under current accounting, and a bank reads it as one anyway. There is no free debt.

The present value says borrow, by about CHF 13,000. The caveats say: check what the lease bundles, then borrow. Either way, the decision is now made with the right ruler.
