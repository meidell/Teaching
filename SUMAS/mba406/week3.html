Week 3 · Managerial Finance · SUMAS

Capital budgeting

Léa wants to open a second workshop in Lausanne: CHF 400,000 of fit-out and machines, and she says it "pays for itself in under three years". Week 2 gave you the ruler — a franc today is not a franc next year. This week you use it on a whole project: turn a plan into incremental, after-tax cash flows, discount them to a net present value, read the internal rate of return off the same numbers and know when it lies, see what payback misses, and then shake the plan — sensitivity, scenarios, break-even — to find out which assumption the decision actually rests on.

The session is three hours in two parts. Part 1 (~1 h) is the lesson: your instructor presents the deck and you follow the same numbered sections here — every slide names its section. Part 2 (~2 h) is nine exercises worked together, given on the slides and as forms below; solutions open as your instructor reveals them.

By the end of Week 3 you will be able to

  • Turn a project plan into a stream of incremental, after-tax cash flows — and say what stays out (sunk costs, allocated overhead, financing) and what comes in (working capital, opportunity costs, lost sales elsewhere).
  • Compute operating cash flow three ways and get the same number each time — and explain why depreciation is not a cash flow but still matters.
  • Compute a project's net present value and state the decision rule.
  • Find the internal rate of return by interpolation, read it off an NPV profile, and name the three cases where it misleads.
  • Compute payback, discounted payback and the profitability index, and say what each one is for.
  • Run a sensitivity and a scenario analysis, find the break-even of the variable that matters, and write a recommendation that names it.

Four things are wrong with that paragraph and one thing is right. By the end of the hour you will be able to list them. First, your instinct.

Your instinct

How fast must a project pay back before you say yes?

Most managers carry a number in their head — "if it isn't back in three years, no". Set yours. Then see what that number ignores.

The longest payback you would accept on a 400 investment
3.0years
The one idea

A project is worth what its cash flows are worth today, minus what it costs. Accept if that is positive.

Week 2's sentence — never add francs from different dates — applied to a whole plan. List every franc the project will make Rochat pay or receive, and only those; move each one to today at the opportunity cost of capital; add them up. The result is the net present value: the amount by which the owners are richer the day the project is accepted. Positive, do it; negative, don't; and between two projects, take the larger NPV.

NPV = C0 + C1 ⁄ (1 + r) + C2 ⁄ (1 + r)² + … + Ct ⁄ (1 + r)t   ·   C0 is negative

Everything else this week — IRR, payback, the index, sensitivity — is either a shortcut to this number or a way of asking how sure of it you are.

Section 3.2 · Cash flows, not profits

Incremental, after tax, when the cash moves

Three rules

Count cash, count only what changes, count it after tax.

  • Cash, not accounting profit. Depreciation is not a payment to anyone — the 400 left the bank on day one. But the tax office accepts depreciation as a cost, so it lowers the tax bill: 80 × 20% = 16 a year of tax saved. Depreciation enters the cash flows only through that shield.
  • Incremental. Only francs that exist because of the project. The market study is spent whether or not Lausanne opens — sunk, out. The overhead allocation moves no cash — out. The 20 of contribution Nyon loses to Lausanne is real — in. A van Rochat already owned and could have sold would be an opportunity cost — in.
  • Financing stays out. No loan, no interest, no dividend in the cash flows. The cost of the money is the discount rate; putting interest in the flows as well would charge for it twice.
OCF = net income + depreciation  =  (sales − cash costs) − tax  =  (sales − cash costs) × (1 − T) + T × depreciation

Section 3.4 · The internal rate of return

The rate at which the project breaks even — and when it lies

The definition

IRR is the discount rate that makes NPV exactly zero.

It is the project's own return, read off the profile where the curve crosses the axis. The rule: accept when the IRR is above the opportunity cost of capital — Lausanne's 18.8% against 10% says yes, and for a normal project (money out first, money in later) IRR and NPV always agree. There is no formula for it; you find it by trial and interpolation: pick a rate that gives a positive NPV and one that gives a negative, and draw a straight line between them.

IRR ≈ rlow + (rhigh − rlow) × NPVlow ⁄ (NPVlow − NPVhigh)

Three traps

When the IRR rule gives the wrong answer

  • Borrowing, not lending. A "project" that brings cash in first and pays it out later — a customer's deposit, a supplier's financing — has an IRR too, but now you want it low. +100 today, −115 in a year: IRR 15%, and at a 10% cost of capital the NPV is −4.5. Reject.
  • Two IRRs. When the cash flows change sign more than once — a kiln rented for a year then dismantled: −100, +230, −132 — the profile crosses zero twice, at 10% and 20%. Neither "is" the return. Use NPV.
  • Scale and timing. IRR is a percentage; it cannot see size. A showroom-only Lausanne site — 150 in, 55 a year for five years — has an IRR of 24.3% against the workshop's 18.8%, and an NPV of 58 against 118. Between mutually exclusive projects, take the higher NPV, or check the IRR of the difference (310 more in, 81 more a year: 16.3%, above 10% — the extra money is well spent).

IRR is a useful way to talk about a project. NPV is the way to decide.

Section 3.5 · Payback, the index and book returns

The other rules — what each one is for

Payback

How long until the cash is back — and what that ignores

Add the cash flows until the outlay is recovered. Lausanne: −460, then 136 a year — after three years 52 is still missing, and year 4 brings 136, so payback is 3 + 52 ⁄ 136 = 3.38 years. Léa's "under three" counted the 150 before tax and forgot the 60 of working capital. The discounted version adds the present values instead: 4 + 28.9 ⁄ 146.5 = 4.20 years.

Payback ignores the rate, ignores everything after the cut-off, and treats a franc in year 3 like a franc in year 1. It is a quick screen for small decisions and a useful measure of how long the money is at risk — not a decision rule.

A payback cut-off against three projects
The profitability index — and the book rate of return

NPV per franc invested; profit per franc of book value

Profitability index = NPV ⁄ investment   ·   Lausanne 117.6 ⁄ 460 = 0.26  ·  showroom 58.5 ⁄ 150 = 0.39

When money is rationed — Rochat can raise 460 this year and no more — the index ranks projects by what each franc buys, and a bundle of small high-index projects can beat one large one. Without rationing, NPV decides: Lausanne adds 118, the showroom 58, and the owners want francs, not ratios.

The book (accounting) rate of return — average net income over average book value, 56 ⁄ 260 = 21.5% for Lausanne — is what the annual report will show. It depends on depreciation conventions, ignores timing and says nothing about the rate the money could earn elsewhere. Know it because the board will quote it; never decide on it.

Section 3.6 · Sensitivity, scenarios — and the decision

Which assumption is the decision standing on?

Sensitivity

Move one thing at a time and watch the NPV.

An NPV of 118 is a forecast's NPV. The question a bank asks is: how wrong can the forecast be before the answer changes? Drag each input on its own and read the NPV. Sales will turn out to be the one that matters — not because it is the biggest number, but because every franc of it is 80 centimes of cash flow five times over.

Scenarios and break-even

Move several things together — then find the edge.

Sensitivity moves one input; a scenario moves a consistent set. The pessimistic case — two hotel groups stay in Nyon, so sales 600, and a joiner costs more, so costs 490 — gives NPV −64. The optimistic — sales 720, costs 485 — gives +315. The plan sits between, and the thing to know is where it tips: the break-even sales at which NPV is exactly zero. One press per step.

Break-even sales — the NPV = 0 point
On Rochat → sales can fall to 611 — six per cent below the plan — before Lausanne stops being worth doing. The accounting break-even, where the site merely shows no loss, is 580: a site that "breaks even" in the accounts is losing the owners 10% a year on 460.

The decision — and what the analysis leaves out

Open Lausanne, on one condition.

At 10%, the plan is worth +118 and returns 18.8%; payback is 3.4 years, not "under three"; the study is sunk and the overhead is not a cost. Léa's instinct was right and three of her reasons were wrong. The condition is the sales line: a 6% shortfall erases the value, so the recommendation is to sign the two hotel contracts before the lease, not after. Still outside the numbers:

  • The rate. 10% was given. If Rochat's true cost of capital is 14%, the margin halves. Week 4.
  • The option to wait. A second site next year, with the hotel contracts signed, may be worth more than this one now. NPV compares now or never; real decisions have a third choice.
  • Financing. Where the 460 comes from — the bank, Léa, a partner — is a separate decision, and must not leak into these cash flows. Week 7.
  • Cash. A positive NPV does not mean Rochat can pay for it in month three. Week 6.

The analysis does not make the decision. It tells Léa which of her beliefs the decision depends on — and that is what she pays a finance manager for.
