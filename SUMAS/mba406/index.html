About this course

What it's about

The two decisions every manager faces — what to invest in and how to pay for it — and the one idea underneath both: the value of a cash flow depends on when it arrives and how risky it is. Reading the statements, the time value of money, capital budgeting, risk and the cost of capital, valuation, working capital, financing (including green bonds and sustainability-linked loans), and a pro-forma model in a spreadsheet. Each method is learned by applying it to a real decision the workshop has to take, so that the finance of a professional role is practised, not just heard.

How it is taught

A cooperative, student-centred approach: a high level of discussion, the lecture element consolidated by exercises worked together, and case studies. A homework set with hints and worked solutions follows each session, and doing it is part of your participation mark.

Prerequisites & tools

None. Bring a calculator with a yx key to every session. From Week 8, a spreadsheet (Excel or Google Sheets); Google Colab with Python is offered as an alternative — browser-based, nothing to install.

What you'll be able to do

Each one is a thing you will be able to do by Week 10 — with the week that teaches it, so you always know where you are on the map.

  • Read the three statements as a manager — position, performance, cash — and turn them into the ratios a bank asks for. Week 1
  • Move money through time. Price any stream of payments in today's francs: present and future value, annuities, perpetuities, effective rates, a loan schedule. Week 2
  • Decide whether a project is worth doing. Incremental cash flows, NPV, IRR and its traps, payback, sensitivity and scenarios. Week 3
  • Set the hurdle rate. Risk and return, diversification, beta and the CAPM as a recipe, the cost of debt and equity, the WACC. Week 4
  • Value a loan, a bond, a share and a company — by discounted cash flow, with a multiples cross-check. Week 5
  • Explain why a profitable business runs out of cash, and fix it: the cash conversion cycle, credit policy, inventory, the cash budget. Week 6
  • Choose how to finance a project — debt or equity, leverage in practice, and the sustainable instruments: green bonds, sustainability-linked loans, a carbon price inside the NPV. Week 7
  • Build a pro-forma model in a spreadsheet and read the sustainable growth rate and external financing needed off it. Week 8
How each one is practisedEvery objective is met three times: the lesson in Part 1 of the session, the exercises in Part 2 worked with the room, and the homework in your own time. If you can do the exercises without the hint, you have the objective.

30% · Case study — individual

You are assigned a financial case study to work on individually: analyse the situation of a company, solve the financial problem it faces and recommend a decision, using the tools and methodologies of this course and your own research with secondary data. The case, the questions and the marks per question are on Moodle.

Format: 2,000 words ± 10%, submitted through Moodle. Deadline: 23 January 2027.

Marked on four criteria of 25 points each: work structure & writing · analysis & understanding · critical thinking ("thinking out of the box") · presentation.

The weekly workshop is your rehearsalEvery week ends with a workshop that makes you apply that week's method to Atelier Rochat, in your own words — a verdict, a recommendation, a number you can defend. It is not graded; it is exactly the move the case study asks of you on a company you have never seen.

50% · Final written exam — Week 10

Closed book, on the methods of Weeks 1–9. Week 9 is a case-based review across every topic in preparation for it. The date is announced on Moodle.

Practise in the exam's styleEach week's page ends with a self-check of ten questions, every answer explained — it does not count, but it is the style the exam is written in, and the in-class exercises are the methods it tests.
The running case

Across all nine weeks we run one business: Atelier Rochat SA, a solid-wood furniture workshop in Nyon — twelve people, CHF 2.4 million of sales, three hotel groups among the clients, and a founder, Léa Rochat, who wants to open a second site in Lausanne. You are its first finance manager. Week 1 asks whether the business is healthy enough to expand; Week 2 whether to lease or borrow for a new router; Week 3 whether the second site is worth opening at all; Week 4 what return it must clear; Week 5 what the business is worth to a buyer; Week 6 why a profitable workshop is running out of cash; Week 7 how to finance its low-carbon project; Week 8 whether it can grow 30% without new equity. By the end, every decision Léa took on instinct has a number behind it that you could defend to her bank.

The numbers are small and hand-computable on purpose: the exam is by hand. From Week 8 the same case moves into a spreadsheet.
