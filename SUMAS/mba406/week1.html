Week 1 · Managerial Finance · SUMAS

Reading the numbers as a manager

You have just been hired as the first finance manager of Atelier Rochat SA, a furniture workshop in Nyon. The founder wants to open a second site and believes the books say yes. This week you learn to read the three statements the way a manager has to — what the firm owns and owes, what it earned, where the cash went — and to turn them into the ratios that answer one question: is this business healthy enough to expand?

The session is three hours in two parts. Part 1 (~1 h) is the lesson: your instructor presents the deck and you follow the same numbered sections here — every slide names its section. Part 2 (~2 h) is eight exercises worked together, given on the slides and as forms below; solutions open as your instructor reveals them.

By the end of Week 1 you will be able to

  • Say what finance is for — the investment decision and the financing decision — and what "value" means to the owner of a small firm.
  • Read a balance sheet, an income statement and a cash-flow statement, and say what each one can and cannot tell you.
  • Explain why a profitable year can end with less cash — accrual accounting versus cash.
  • Compute and interpret the ratios that matter: liquidity, leverage, efficiency and profitability.
  • Decompose return on equity the DuPont way and say which lever is doing the work.
  • Give the founder a verdict on "can we expand?" that a bank would accept.

Section 1.1 · The hook

"We made 240 — so we can afford it, right?"

The running case

Atelier Rochat SA, Nyon.

Léa Rochat started making solid-wood furniture in a rented barn fourteen years ago. Today the workshop employs twelve people, sells to private clients, architects and three hotel groups, and turned over CHF 2.4 million last year. Léa knows timber, joints and clients. She does not read a balance sheet — until now she had an accountant for that, and the accountant has just retired. You are the new finance manager.

"Here is the thing. We made CHF 240,000 profit last year. A second workshop in Lausanne — the lease, the fit-out, two more benches — costs about 400. We have 240 in profit and the bank likes us. So we can afford it, right? I'd like to sign the lease next month." — Léa, Monday morning

Every week of this course is one decision Léa has to take, and the finance that decision needs. This week's decision is the first and the biggest: is the business healthy enough to expand? Before you open the books, an instinct check.

Your estimate

Of the CHF 240,000 profit, how much is sitting in the bank at year end?

Profit was 240. The year started with CHF 150,000 in the bank. Léa assumes the profit is "in the account". What's your guess for how much of the 240 is actually there — cash the business could spend tomorrow?

Three statements, three questions

Each statement answers one question — and only one.

  • Balance sheet — what does the firm own and owe, at this moment? A photograph, taken on 31 December. Section 1.2.
  • Income statement — what did it earn over the year? A film of twelve months, in the accountant's language: revenue when earned, cost when incurred. Section 1.3.
  • Cash-flow statement — where did the cash actually come from and go to? The bank account's own story, in three chapters. Section 1.4.

Léa's mistake is to read the second as if it were the third. The profit figure is true. The cash figure is also true. They are answers to different questions, and the whole of this week is learning to ask each statement the question it can answer.

Finance has two jobs: deciding what to invest in and deciding how to pay for it. Both start by reading the numbers properly.

Section 1.2 · The balance sheet

What the firm owns, and who paid for it

The identity

Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Always.

The left side lists everything the firm owns — the assets, from the most liquid (cash) to the least (the building). The right side says who has a claim on it: the people the firm owes (liabilities) and, as the remainder, the owners (equity). The two sides are equal by construction — equity is defined as assets minus liabilities. It is not a coincidence that has to be checked; it is a definition.

Assets  =  Liabilities  +  Shareholders' equity   ·   Net working capital = Current assets − Current liabilities

Read it top to bottom once. Current means "within a year": current assets will turn into cash within twelve months, current liabilities must be paid within twelve. The gap between them — net working capital, CHF 440k at the end of 2025 — is the cushion the firm runs its daily operations on.

On Rochat → cash fell from 150 to 90, but receivables rose by 100 and inventory by 70. The money did not vanish: it is sitting in hotels that have not paid yet and in timber that has not been sold yet. That is the first clue to Léa's puzzle.
Make it balance

Every transaction touches at least two lines — and the sides stay equal.

Start from the 2025 balance sheet and apply a transaction. Watch which lines move, and check the totals.

The balance sheet after…
Pick a transaction.

Buying a machine with a loan changes nothing about profit and nothing about equity — the firm has more assets and more debt. Collecting a receivable changes nothing about total assets either: one asset became another. The balance sheet records positions, not performance.

Book value is not market value

The balance sheet is history, at cost.

Rochat's building appears at what it cost minus depreciation — not what it would sell for. Léa's reputation, her client list and her twelve craftsmen appear nowhere, because accounting only records what was bought. The equity figure of CHF 1,000k is the book value of the owners' claim; what a buyer would pay for the firm is its market value, and the two can differ by a factor of two in either direction. Week 5 is about the second number. This week is about reading the first honestly.

Each subtotal answers a different reader. Gross profit says what the product itself earns after the direct cost of making it. EBIT — earnings before interest and tax — says what the business earns before anyone asks how it is financed; it is the number a buyer looks at. Net income is what is left for the owners after the bank and the tax office. Of those 240, Léa took 70 as a dividend and 170 stayed in the firm — which is exactly why equity rose from 830 to 1,000.

Accrual accounting

Revenue when earned, cost when incurred — not when cash moves.

This is the rule that separates profit from cash, and it is a good rule: it matches each sale to the costs of making it, in the period the work was done. Two consequences you must carry in your head:

  • A dining set delivered in December to a hotel on 60-day terms is December revenue, even though the cash arrives in February. Until then it sits in accounts receivable.
  • Depreciation is not a cash payment. The CNC router was paid for three years ago; the income statement spreads that cost over its useful life, CHF 120k a year, so that each year carries its share. Cash left once, when it was bought; the expense comes back every year as an entry, not as money.
The December order — profit view vs cash view

Same three months, same business, two honest answers. The accrual view tells you whether the work was worth doing. The cash view tells you whether you can pay the wages on Friday. A manager needs both.

Section 1.4 · The cash-flow statement

Where the cash actually went

Three chapters

Operating, investing, financing — and they must add up to the change in cash.

  • Operating — cash generated by running the business. Start from net income, add back depreciation (it was never cash), then adjust for working capital: a rise in receivables or inventory absorbs cash, a rise in payables releases it.
  • Investing — cash spent on long-lived assets (capital expenditure, "capex") or received from selling them.
  • Financing — cash from or to the people who fund the firm: new borrowing, repayments, dividends, new shares.
Operating CF = Net income + Depreciation − ΔReceivables − ΔInventory + ΔPayables   ·   ΔCash = Operating + Investing + Financing

Two of the inputs are not printed anywhere — you derive them from the two balance sheets. Capital expenditure is whatever made net fixed assets go up after depreciation took its bite; dividends are whatever stopped equity rising by the full net income. Section 1.6 · The DuPont decomposition

Where does a 24% return on equity come from?

Three levers

ROE = margin × turnover × leverage.

ROE = Net income⁄Revenue × Revenue⁄Assets × Assets⁄Equity  =  10% × 1.2 × 2.0  =  24%

Multiply the three fractions and revenue cancels, assets cancel, and you are left with net income over equity. So the identity is trivially true — and enormously useful, because the three factors are different businesses:

  • Net profit margin — how much of each franc of sales is kept. Pricing, cost control, product.
  • Asset turnover — how many francs of sales each franc of assets generates. Utilisation, working capital, the efficiency ratios of 1.5.
  • Equity multiplier (assets ÷ equity) — how much of the asset base the owners financed with other people's money. This one is leverage, and it raises ROE in good years and sinks it in bad ones.

The decision

So — healthy enough to expand?

Put the week together. Liquidity: fine. Leverage: moderate, with room for a second loan but not an unlimited one. Profitability: good, and genuinely earned. Cash: falling, because receivables and inventory are swallowing the profit faster than the business generates it. A bank will lend against this balance sheet; a careful finance manager will first ask why the hotels take 61 days to pay, and what a second site does to working capital. The verdict is "yes, but not yet, and not like that" — and in section 1.9 you will write it in your own words.

Léa's 240 is real. It is already spent — on a router, on timber, on hotels that have not paid. The question is not whether the firm is profitable. It is whether a second site is the best use of the next 400, and how to pay for it. That is Weeks 2 and 3.
