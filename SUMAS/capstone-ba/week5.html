Learning focus · required materials

Two halves of one week

First, finalising the framing — locking in your two scenario axes, naming your four worlds, and using them to discipline what your system map includes and excludes. Second, executing the map itself: actors, flows, feedback loops, indicators. By the end of the week you will be able to:

  • Translate a 2×2 scenario matrix into a focused system boundary
  • Construct a feedback-loop diagram that shows why your sustainability challenge is hard
  • Identify the indicators that distinguish your four scenarios from each other

Suggested structure (you may adapt)

Executive summary (~150 words). The strategic question, your 2×2 axes, your headline finding so far.
System scope and boundary (~200). What you are analysing — geography, time horizon, decision being supported. What you are explicitly leaving out.
Stakeholders and drivers (~400). The actors that matter and why; the forces shaping their behaviour; one or two feedback loops.
Sustainability challenge framed through your scenario matrix (~400). Your two critical uncertainties, the four worlds they generate, what each implies for the strategic question.
Indicators and datasets (~300). The metrics that would tell you which scenario is unfolding; the sources you will use in Phase 2.
Limitations and next steps (~150). Honest acknowledgment of what your framing leaves underspecified and how Phase 2 closes the gaps.

The 2×2 matrix itself is not the Phase 1 deliverable. It is the framing through which you select stakeholders, draw boundaries and choose indicators. Students who treat the matrix as an appendix produce weaker reports than students who let it discipline the whole document.

Closing the syllabus / platform loop

A question that comes up every year. The programme syllabus lists illustrative sustainability challenges — on one side infrastructure, supply chains, mobility, water and climate risk; on the other ESG risk, climate stress testing, green bonds, AI-driven ESG scoring and sustainable supply-chain finance. This platform builds out a default challenge: EU wind to 2040, Ørsted vs TotalEnergies. These are complementary, not conflicting. Pick the framing closest to where you want to work after the Bachelor:

  • If operations and infrastructure interest you: the natural framing is sustainability transitions in critical infrastructure — wind capacity, grid build-out, supply-chain decarbonisation, a just transition for fossil-fuel workers.
  • If finance interests you: sustainable finance applied to the same case — climate transition risk in lender portfolios, ESG scoring of a pure-play vs an integrated firm, green bonds and sustainability-linked debt as financing instruments, capital allocation between concentrated and hedged bets on the transition.

If you would prefer a different topic entirely you may propose one — but it requires my formal approval before you start, and with Phase 1 due Sunday, switching now is high-risk. I would not recommend it.

The orthogonality test · a cohort-wide pitfall

Make the second axis a driver, not an outcome

A matrix a student drafted last year illustrates the most common failure mode in scenario design. The two axes were: Axis 1 — European coordination and investability (integrated, rules-based, financeable ↔ fragmented, protectionist, insecure); Axis 2 — delivery capacity and system resilience (high delivery / resilient ↔ low delivery / fragile).

The thinking behind both is sound. Each is a real critical uncertainty. But put together they fail the independence test. A fragmented, protectionist Europe will, in the real world, tend to produce lower delivery and a more fragile system; an integrated one, higher delivery. If the axes correlate, two of your four cells collapse — the off-diagonal scenarios become hard to construct without an explicit causal story for why they should exist. This pattern — a "policy" axis paired with what is effectively a "policy outcome" axis — also fails Schoemaker's plausibility test (Pitfall 3, p. 39).

The general fix: make sure your second axis is a driver, not an outcome. Drivers that pair well with a policy or coordination axis:

  • Pace of cost decline — Wright's Law continues vs stalls
  • Supply-chain geopolitics — open vs weaponised, especially China
  • Capital-cost environment — rates stay elevated vs fall back
  • Demand-side electrification speed — mass vs partial
  • Speed of grid build-out — held up vs unblocked

The worked example — climate-policy strength × renewable cost-decline speed — is chosen precisely because the two are reasonably independent. A strong-policy world might still see slow cost decline if supply chains seize; a weak-policy world could still see fast decline if Chinese manufacturers keep flooding the global market.

Self-check before you commit your axes

Write a one-sentence narrative for each of the four cells. If the off-diagonal cells need lengthy contortions to make plausible, your axes are correlated and you should fix one before you commit.

If cells 2 and 3 took you three times as long as 1 and 4, that is the test failing. Post the final axes in Monday's forum and I will tell you by Tuesday.

From scenario matrix to system map

Three ways the 2×2 disciplines the map

1 · Boundary. The two uncertainties you commit to determine which actors and flows belong inside your system boundary. If you have chosen "EU coordination" as an axis, EU institutions, member-state regulators and capital markets are inside; the consumer-side electrification of European households may be outside — a different system, even if it touches yours.

2 · Indicators. Each of your four scenarios should have at least three quantitative anchors that distinguish it from the others. Those anchors are your indicators. They give you a list — not a comprehensive list, a discriminating one — of what you will need data on for Phase 2.

3 · Feedback loops. The reason scenarios diverge is that systems contain reinforcing and balancing loops, and small differences in starting conditions amplify. Your map should show at least two loops — typically a reinforcing loop driving deployment (cost decline → adoption → scale → cost decline) and a balancing loop constraining it (deployment → grid congestion → curtailment → revenue compression → slower investment). Sterman Chapter 1 is the source for how to draw them.

Build a loop · mark each link + (same direction) or − (opposite), and read off R or B

The rule from Sterman: count the minus signs around the loop. Even (including zero) → reinforcing. Odd → balancing. Two loops from the wind system, with the signs left for you.

Self-paced workshop activities

Monday to Sunday

Activity 1 — Lock in your axes. Apply the orthogonality test to your draft 2×2. If you fail it, replace one axis. Post your final two axes and a one-sentence narrative for each of the four cells. Short — this is a checkpoint, not an essay. It gives me a chance to flag any issues before you commit the matrix to your Phase 1 draft, and I will answer every post in the thread by Tuesday evening.

Activity 2 — Draw your causal-loop diagram (Tuesday–Wednesday)

Using Sterman Chapter 1 as your guide, draw a feedback-loop diagram of the system you are analysing. Two loops minimum — one reinforcing, one balancing. Hand-drawn is fine; Loopy ↗, Kumu ↗ or a whiteboard photo are also fine. The diagram goes into your Phase 1 report; the work of drawing it is what teaches you which actors and flows actually matter.

Activity 3 — Indicator shortlist (Wednesday)

For each of your four scenarios, list the three quantitative anchors that distinguish it. For each anchor, name the dataset where you will get the data in Phase 2. This is the bridge between Phase 1 (qualitative framing) and Phase 2 (quantitative analysis). A useful pattern:
