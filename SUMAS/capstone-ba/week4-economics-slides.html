A guess in the chat · A, B, C or D

How much of the cost of each MWh is the cost of the money?

  1. A · about 10%
  2. B · about 30%
  3. C · about 50%
  4. D · about two thirds

D. For a UK offshore farm at a 6.2% cost of capital, paying back the money spent upfront is about two thirds of the cost of every MWh. The rest is running the farm. We will check it by hand in five minutes.

The shape of the business

A wind farm is a loan with blades

Year 0

Almost all the money is spent before the first MWh is sold: turbines, foundations, cables, a substation, installation ships.

Years 1–35

The fuel is free and running costs are modest. The farm earns its money back slowly, MWh by MWh, over decades.

So the price of a wind MWh is mostly the price of money borrowed or invested years earlier. A gas plant is the opposite: cheap to build, and the fuel is paid as you go.

By hand · from costs to a price per MWh

What one MWh must earn

StepCalculationPer MW
Spread £3,976k over 35 years at 6.2% (like a mortgage)£3,976k × 0.0706£281k a year
Add the running cost£281k + £138.5k£419k a year
Divide by what it produces£419k ÷ 4,424 MWh≈ £95 per MWh
The capital part of it£281k ÷ 4,424 MWh£63.5 = 67%

0.0706 is the annuity factor: 0.062 ÷ (1 − 1.062−35). Arup's full model, with construction time and decommissioning, gives £88.5/MWh. The simple version is close enough to reason with.

Name it

The levelised cost of electricity (LCOE)

LCOE = (capex spread over the life at the cost of capital + yearly running cost) ÷ MWh produced per year

  • Capex: what it costs to build, per MW.
  • Cost of capital: the return lenders and shareholders demand, often written WACC (weighted average cost of capital).
  • MWh per year: capacity × 8,760 hours × load factor.

You met LCOE in Week 2 and fitted its fall in Week 3. Tonight is what is inside it.

Two percentage points on the cost of capital move the cost of every MWh by about 16%, and the turbines have not changed at all.

Simple model from slide 7. A peer-reviewed study finds the same order of magnitude: a 3.3-point higher cost of capital in Germany means 26% higher LCOE (iScience, 2023).

What actually happened

2022–2023: the price of money jumped

The central banks

ECB deposit rate: −0.50% until July 2022 → 4.00% from September 2023.
US Federal Reserve: near zero in early 2022 → 5.25–5.50% in July 2023, 525 basis points in total.

One company's arithmetic

Ørsted, fourth quarter of 2024: a 0.75-point rise in the cost of capital it applied to its US portfolio led to a DKK 4.3 bn impairment. Three quarters of a percentage point, four billion crowns.

Projects priced in 2019–21 with cheap money were built with expensive money.

Read-out

Rates, capex and wind, compared

Alone: rates +16%, capex +10%, load factor +11%. Together: about +43%, from £95 to £135 per MWh. 2022–23 delivered the first two at once, which is why projects with a fixed price agreed before 2022 stopped making sense.

The point for your scenarios: interest rates are a driver of wind's cost, independent of EU climate policy. Week 4's orthogonality test likes that.

How the farm gets paid

Four ways to sell the power

Contract for difference (CfD)The state guarantees a fixed price (the strike price) for 15–20 years. The most common model for new European offshore wind.
MerchantSell at whatever the market pays each hour. All the upside, all the risk, including the falling capture price (Week 5).
Corporate PPAA long contract with a big buyer: Ørsted–Covestro, 10 years, 100 MW from Borkum Riffgrund 3; EnBW–Google, 15 years from He Dreiht.
Pay for the seabedThe developer pays the state for the right to build: Germany's June 2023 auction raised about €12.6 bn, with oil majors among the winners, paying €1.56–2.07 m per MW.

Elsewhere in Europe

Auctions with no bidders

Denmark

December 2024: zero bids for 3 GW in the North Sea. No subsidy, the developer paid the state, and the state took 20%. May 2025: relaunched with 20-year CfDs. August 2026: Vattenfall won two sites at DKK 504 and 542 per MWh.

Germany

June 2023: developers paid €12.6 bn for 7 GW of seabed. August 2025: zero bids for 2.5 GW, a first. January 2026: the 2026 tenders were pushed to 2027.

The same lesson in three countries: when the state shifts all the risk to the developer, the developer stays at home.

Build, sell half, operate

Ørsted's way to recycle capital

The model

Develop and build a farm, then sell a large stake to an investor who wants long, steady returns (a pension fund, an infrastructure fund). Keep building and operating it for a fee. Use the cash for the next farm.

An example

December 2025: 50% of Hornsea 3 sold to Apollo for about DKK 39 bn. Ørsted remains the builder and the operator. In 2024 it planned about DKK 115 bn of such sales to 2030.

The model works when buyers pay well for steady returns, which means when interest rates are low.

The same wind, two different answers

A company that pays more for its money needs a higher price for the same wind. Cheaper capital is a competitive advantage in this industry, sometimes a larger one than better turbines.
