Core concepts · 1

Time series vs learning curves — two lenses on the same future

Wright's Law projects cost as a function of cumulative capacity — a structural relationship grounded in the economics of learning. Time-series forecasting projects an indicator as a function of time — a statistical relationship: whatever made the past behave as it did, assume it continues. The two lenses can converge or diverge. When they converge you have more confidence. When they diverge you have a question worth answering.

LensWhat it capturesWhere it fails
Learning curveStructural cost reduction from scaleWhen learning slows — supply chain, materials, ecological limits
Time seriesAggregate momentum of the indicatorWhen the underlying regime changes — policy shift, technology breakthrough, geopolitical shock

For Phase 2, apply both. Compare the answers. Where they agree, that is your central case. Where they disagree, that is a finding worth interpreting.

The cheapest time-series model that is still honestBefore ARIMA: fit a straight line to the logarithm of your series against the year. The slope is the average annual growth rate; the residuals tell you whether growth is speeding up or slowing. For capacity it is often enough. For cost, compare its 2040 value with the Wright's Law value — if the time-series line says $40 and the learning curve says $55, the gap is the deployment pace you are implicitly assuming. Name that pace. Hyndman's Forecasting: Principles and Practice ↗ is the free reference if you want to go further.
Core concepts · 2

Uncertainty bands — why a point estimate is dishonest

A forecast without an uncertainty band is a guess presented as a fact.

Three types of uncertainty you should report:

  • Parameter uncertainty — you don't know the right learning rate. Your conservative / central / aggressive projections from Week 7 capture this.
  • Model uncertainty — you don't know if Wright's Law (or your time-series model) is even the right shape. Comparing two methods that disagree captures this.
  • Scenario uncertainty — the world you projected from might not be the world that arrives. Your Phase 1 four-scenario matrix captures this.
Draw it · the chart your Phase 2 needs, with your own numbers

A credible Phase 2 chart shows at minimum: your central projection as a solid line; conservative and aggressive bounds as a shaded band or dashed lines; historical data clearly labelled as observed, not modelled; and a note on which type of uncertainty the band represents — these are not 95% confidence intervals unless you actually calculated them. Be precise.

Compare with how IEA, BloombergNEF and DNV present their forecasts. They almost never put hard error bars on a single projection — they use scenarios, which is itself a way of communicating uncertainty without false precision. You can do the same.

Core concepts · 3

Cross-checking — your forecast vs the industry

Three forecasts to find and compare your central case against: IEA World Energy Outlook (Net Zero Emissions, Announced Pledges, Stated Policies); BloombergNEF New Energy Outlook (Net Zero and Economic Transition scenarios); DNV Energy Transition Outlook (a single most-likely projection rather than scenarios). For your headline indicator, find the three corresponding 2040 numbers and tabulate them next to yours.

The cross-check table · enter what you found
Enter the three industry numbers to see where you sit.

If yours is significantly more aggressive or more conservative than all three, you need a reason. Two acceptable ones:

  • Different boundary conditions — "IEA's Net Zero scenario assumes coordinated global action that my Fragmented Europe scenario explicitly rejects."
  • A known methodological blind spot — "IEA has systematically underestimated solar PV growth for 15 years; my projection corrects for that learning-rate bias."

The IEA bias on renewables is real and well documented — Carbon Brief and others have tracked it. Don't be afraid to disagree with the IEA if you can justify it. That kind of defensible disagreement is what consulting work actually is.

Writing the Phase 2 report

2,000 words — a suggested allocation, and three rules for charts

SectionWordsWhat goes here
Question and approach~250Restate your Phase 1 scenarios; state your quantitative question; preview your answer
Data and method~400Datasets used, why; analytical methods, why; what you ruled out
Results~700The actual analysis with charts: learning rate, three projections, time-series fit, cross-check table
Interpretation~450What does this mean for which scenario is unfolding? What does it mean for Ørsted vs TotalEnergies?
Limitations~200What could invalidate your work; what your method misses
  • Readable in black and white. Assume someone prints it.
  • Sources cited under each chart. Even if you made the chart, the underlying data has a source.
  • No chart without a takeaway. If you can't write a one-sentence caption stating what the chart shows, the chart doesn't belong in the report.

What to include

  • The five sections above
  • At least three charts — your fitted curve, your three projections, your industry cross-check; more if the analysis warrants
  • A complete reference list, including the datasets you used with URLs and access dates
  • A short note at the end declaring AI use — which assistant, for what purposes, same standard as Phase 1

Method (30%) and interpretation (25%) together are 55% of the grade. Charts are 10%. Spend your time on the analysis and the meaning, not on making things pretty.
