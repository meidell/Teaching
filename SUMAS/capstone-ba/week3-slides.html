Part 1 · Wright's Law

1936: the cost of airplanes

Theodore Wright, an aeronautical engineer, noticed that each time the number of planes built doubled, the labour needed per plane fell by about the same share: roughly 20%.

Not each year. Each doubling of everything built so far.

Wright, T. P. (1936). Factors affecting the cost of airplanes. Journal of the Aeronautical Sciences, 3(4), 122–128.

Then the letters

The same ladder, for any capacity

Cost(Q) = Cost(Q₀) × (Q / Q₀)−b

  • Q · cumulative installed capacity, in GW
  • b · how steep the curve is
  • learning rate = 1 − 2−b · b = 0.234 gives 15%; b = 0.319 gives 19.8%

600 GW is 2.85 doublings beyond 83 GW, not three. That is why 2040 at 15% lands at about $49.7, not $48.5.

Computed: 600 / 83 = 7.23 = 22.85.

Why the fitter uses logarithms

On log scales, the curve becomes a straight line

Points: the fitter's default rows on the Week 3 page, rounded from IRENA (cost) and GWEC (capacity) — the data lead verifies them. Line: ordinary least squares of ln(cost) on ln(capacity); slope −0.319, learning rate 19.8%, R² 0.94. Computed.

What R² tells you · and what it does not

0.94 says the line fits these eight points

It does say

94% of the variation in log-cost across these eight years lines up with log-capacity.

It does not say

That capacity caused the fall. That the line continues. That the rows are right. A wrong row that sits on the line raises R².

A high R² on eight points is a description, not a forecast.

Whose learning?

The curve counts the world's gigawatts

Turbine makers, cable layers and installation vessels learn on every project, wherever it is built. A farm in China or Taiwan lowers the cost of one in Denmark.

The client is European. The learning is not. Week 2's tiles counted European gigawatts; the learning curve needs the global series.

Global cumulative offshore capacity, about 83 GW at the end of 2024: GWEC, Global Offshore Wind Report 2025.

Is it learning, or just time?

Two laws that fit the past equally well

Wright

Cost falls with every doubling of cumulative production. More building, faster fall.

Moore

Cost falls by a fixed share every year, whatever happens.

Nagy and colleagues tested both on 62 technologies: both predicted about equally well, because production itself grew exponentially over time.

The difference matters for policy: under Wright, building more is what makes it cheaper.

Nagy, B., Farmer, J. D., Bui, Q. M., Trancik, J. E. (2013). Statistical basis for predicting technological progress. PLoS ONE 8(2): e52669.

Why this matters to the client

Trusting the curve has a price tag

Way and colleagues used Wright's Law to forecast energy costs. A fast transition to clean energy came out cheaper than no transition, by about $12 trillion in net present value.

Every serious outlook rests on a learning rate somebody chose. Yours is one of them.

Way, R., Ives, M. C., Mealy, P., Farmer, J. D. (2022). Empirically grounded technology forecasts and the energy transition. Joule 6(9), 2057–2082.

The curve can bend

2021–2024: offshore costs went up

  • Steel, vessels and turbines became more expensive after 2021.
  • Interest rates rose, and an offshore farm is mostly paid for before it produces anything.
  • In the UK's 2023 auction (AR5), no offshore wind project bid at all: the price cap was too low.

Wright's Law counts gigawatts. It does not see interest rates. Week 4 does.

UK Department for Energy Security and Net Zero, Allocation Round 5 results, September 2023.

Part 2 · AI as an analyst

"Use AI" means four different things

MethodWhat it doesIn this capstone
Large language modelWrites text that is likely to follow your promptDrafting, summarising, structuring an argument
RegressionFinds the best line through dataWright's Law, tonight
Time-series forecastExtends past values forwardCapacity to 2040
ClusteringGroups similar casesMature and emerging wind markets

Asking a chat assistant for a learning rate is asking the first tool to do the second tool's job.

What an assistant does

It predicts the next word

A language model writes, one piece at a time, the text most likely to follow what came before. It was trained on text, not on a database of facts.

  • A sentence that sounds like a sourced number is easy for it to produce.
  • Whether the number is true depends on whether it appeared, often enough, in what it was trained on.
  • Assistants that search the web cite pages; they still summarise them, and can misread them.

A real case

Six court cases that did not exist

In 2023 two New York lawyers filed a brief citing six earlier court decisions. ChatGPT had produced them; none existed. The court fined the lawyers and their firm $5,000.

When asked, the assistant had confirmed that the cases were real.

Mata v. Avianca, Inc., US District Court, Southern District of New York, opinion and sanctions order of 22 June 2023.

The audit question

What did Ørsted announce in 2023?

What the company reported

Impairments of DKK 28.4 bn in the first nine months of 2023, DKK 19.9 bn of it on Ocean Wind 1. For the full year: DKK 26.8 bn of impairments plus a DKK 9.6 bn provision for cancelling Ocean Wind 1 and 2.

Typical assistant errors

A rounded "$4 bn" with no period · nine-month and full-year figures mixed · Ocean Wind 1 and 2 merged · Hornsea or Revolution Wind named as cancelled · links to pages that do not exist.

Ørsted, Interim report Q3 2023 and Annual report 2023. Check the full-year figures in the annual report itself before you quote them.

Sorting · type the number

Which failure mode is it?

  1. "In 2023 Ørsted cancelled Hornsea 3", with a link that does not open. 1 · hallucinated: Hornsea 3 went ahead; Ocean Wind 1 and 2 were cancelled
  2. Ørsted's 2021 strategy targets presented as current. 2 · outdated knowledge
  3. "A 1 GW farm produces about 1 TWh a year." 3 · maths: 1 GW × 50% × 8,760 h ≈ 4.4 TWh
  4. "Yes, offshore wind is clearly the better bet", after you asked "why is offshore wind the better bet?" 5 · confirmation bias
