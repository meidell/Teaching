Reference · grows every week

The vocabulary, in the course's own words

A consultant who cannot say what a capacity factor is loses the room in the first minute. Every term the ten weeks use is here, under the week it first appears, with where it comes back. Definitions are written the way the week pages use them — if a reading defines a term differently, the reading wins and the forum is the place to say so.

Week 1 · The brief and the toolkit

Consulting, forecasting, systems

Team report
The one document each team of four writes over nine weeks, on this site (report.html): eighteen sections in three phases, handed in phase by phase from the page itself. The forums stay individual.W1
Charter
Four short answers a team writes in Week 1: how it meets, how it decides, what it lets an AI do and how it declares it, and who writes what by Week 5. Not graded; read by the instructor before Week 2.W1
Hand-in
A phase of the team report frozen at a moment in time, from the report page, by any one member. A later hand-in replaces it; the most recent is marked, and if it is late the penalty is 5% per 24 hours. A PDF can be attached on the hand-in page when a figure will not paste.W1
Plenitude
Eni's renewables, retail and e-mobility subsidiary — the part of the Italian major that carries its hedged bet, about 4 GW of installed renewables in 2024, mostly solar and onshore wind. When you compare Eni with Ørsted, compare Plenitude's capacity, not the group's oil and gas figures.W2
Client question
The one sentence everything is evidence for or against: is wind a sound long-term investment for Europe's net-zero transition, and is Ørsted or Eni better positioned? Every post, report and exam answer serves it.W1 · W9
Pure-play
A company that does one thing. Ørsted sold its oil and gas and bet everything on offshore wind: high upside if the bet lands, no cushion if it does not.W1 · W4 · W9
Hedged (integrated) strategy
Keeping several bets at once — Eni's fossil portfolio plus growing renewables. Less upside in any single future, more survival across many. The capstone's central contrast.W1 · W4 · W9
Net zero · climate neutrality
Emissions reduced as far as possible and the remainder removed, so the net is zero. The EU's legal target for 2050; the capstone asks about the path to 2040.W1
European Green Deal
The EU's 2019 roadmap to climate neutrality by 2050 — the policy frame that makes "strength of climate policy" a scenario axis rather than a given.W1 · W4
Cone of uncertainty
The further out you look, the wider the range of plausible futures. Short term (1–3 years) is mostly decided; medium term (5–10) opens the key uncertainties; long term (15–30) holds several different worlds. Your 2040 question sits in the cone's wide end.W1 · W4
Trend
A development whose direction is predictable even if its pace is not — population ageing, electrification, cost decline under Wright's Law. Plan around it; do not build a scenario axis on it.W1 · W4
Uncertainty
A high-impact factor that could genuinely go either way — post-2030 policy ambition, interest rates, Chinese access to EU auctions. The two most critical become your scenario axes.W1 · W4 · W5
Scenario planning
Shell's 1970s method: not predicting one future but preparing for several plausible ones, then testing a strategy against each. The spine of the course.W1 · W4 · W9
Stakeholder map
An inventory of the actors in a system — governments, companies, investors, grid operators, NGOs, communities — and who influences whom. The first forum asks for eight actors and one surprising connection.W1 · W5
Systems thinking
Going past "who are the actors" to "how do they influence each other" and "what loops does that create". Stakeholders are the inventory; the loops are the dynamics.W1 · W5
Reinforcing loop
A feedback loop where each turn strengthens the next: falling LCOE → more investment → more deployment → more scale → lower LCOE. Growth engines are reinforcing loops; so are collapses.W1 · W5
Balancing loop
A loop that pushes back: faster deployment → grid congestion → curtailment → lower revenues → slower investment. Every reinforcing loop eventually meets one.W1 · W5 · W7
Doughnut economics
Kate Raworth's goal for an economy: everyone above a social foundation (12 essentials) and the whole thing below an ecological ceiling (9 planetary boundaries). Wind serves the first and presses on the second.W1 · W7
Week 2 · The market

Units, costs and the size of the thing

Power (capacity)kW · MW · GW
How much can be generated at one moment. One large offshore turbine is 14–15 MW; a big plant or a region is measured in GW. It says nothing about how often it runs.W2
Energy (generation)kWh · MWh · GWh · TWh
Power multiplied by time. Running 1 kW for one hour is 1 kWh; a country's year is counted in TWh. GW is the engine, TWh is the distance driven — never swap them.W2 · W6
Capacity factorCF
Energy actually produced ÷ the maximum possible if the plant ran flat out all year. Offshore wind 45–50% (good sites higher), onshore 25–35%, solar 15–20%, nuclear 85–90%. The reason 1 GW of wind is not 1 GW of nuclear.W2 · W3
8,760
Hours in a year. Capacity (GW) × capacity factor × 8,760 = energy (GWh). 1 GW at 50% gives 4.38 TWh. Learn it cold.W2
LCOElevelised cost of energy, $/MWh
Total lifetime cost (build it, run it, finance it) ÷ total lifetime energy produced. The one number that lets you compare wind with gas or nuclear. Offshore wind fell from ~$188/MWh in 2010 to ~$86 in 2024.W2 · W3 · W7
CAPEX · OPEX
Capital expenditure — what it costs to build — and operating expenditure — what it costs to run each year. Both sit in the numerator of LCOE; interest rates change CAPEX's share dramatically.W2 · W4
Offshore · onshore
Turbines at sea versus on land. Offshore wins on wind strength, steadiness and turbine size, and loses on installation, maintenance and grid-connection cost. Ørsted is offshore; Eni's renewables are mostly solar and onshore.W2
Grid congestion · curtailment
When the wires cannot carry what the turbines produce, output is deliberately reduced (curtailed) and the operator earns nothing for it. The balancing loop in numbers.W2 · W5
Electricity mix vs total energy mix
Two numbers that get confused in every first draft. Renewables are about a fifth of the EU's total energy (heat, transport, industry included) and about half of its electricity. Say which one you mean, every time.W1 · W2
Market capitalisation
Share price × number of shares: what the market thinks the whole company is worth today. One of the metrics you can compare the two companies on — and one that moves with sentiment, not just fundamentals.W2
Project pipeline
Capacity a developer has won, is permitting or is building but has not yet switched on. Ørsted's pipeline is the bet; its 2023 write-downs were pipeline that stopped making sense.W2 · W4
Primary · authoritative source
The organisation that produced the number (IRENA, WindEurope, Eurostat, a company's annual report) rather than someone quoting it. An AI summary is never a source; the link it points to might be.W2 · W3 · W5
Week 3 · AI tools

What the assistant does, and how it gets it wrong

LLMlarge language model
Claude, ChatGPT, Perplexity: a model that synthesises, summarises and reasons in text. Good at setting up an analysis and explaining it; incapable of knowing which scenario is right. That judgment is yours.W3 · W6
Regression · curve fitting
Finding the line (or curve) that best runs through a set of data points. Wright's Law is fitted this way, on logarithms.W3 · W7
Time-series forecasting
Projecting an indicator forward as a function of time from its own past. A statistical lens, to be laid beside the learning curve's structural one.W3 · W8
Clustering
Grouping similar things — e.g. mature versus emerging offshore markets — without being told the groups in advance.W3 · W6
Wright's Law
Every doubling of cumulative production cuts unit cost by a fixed share. Cost(Q) = Cost(Q₀) × (Q/Q₀)−b. The single most important pattern in the renewable transition — and the assumption every optimistic 2040 forecast rests on.W2 · W3 · W7 · W10
Learning rateLR = 1 − 2−b
The share of cost lost per doubling. Published offshore-wind rates sit around 9–18%; onshore 8–12%; solar famously 20%+. You fit your own and compare.W3 · W7
Cumulative installed capacityQ, in GW
Everything ever built, added up — the x-axis of a learning curve. Not annual additions, and not what is currently running.W3 · W7
Log-log plot
Both axes on logarithmic scales. A Wright's Law relationship appears as a straight line; if the dots curve away, the law is the wrong shape for your series.W3 · W7
Hallucination
A confident, specific number or source that does not exist. Caught by checking the primary source — never by asking the model again.W3
Outdated knowledge
A 2022 figure presented as current. Always ask the model for the year of its data, then check it has not moved.W3
Out-of-distribution
A question outside what the model has seen much of — a niche wind farm, a 2025 auction. The answer arrives just as confident and is far more likely wrong.W3
Confirmation bias
You frame the question, the model agrees, you feel validated. Antidote: ask it to argue the opposite side, seriously.W3 · W9
Enabler · inhibitorVinuesa et al. 2020
AI can help reach ~79% of the SDG targets and hinder ~35%. For wind it enables forecasting, siting and maintenance; it inhibits through the electricity, carbon and water its own data centres consume.W3 · W6 · W7 · W10
SDG 7 · SDG 13
The UN Sustainable Development Goals for affordable, clean energy and for climate action — the two the capstone's question lives under.W3
Week 4 · Scenarios

From one number to four worlds

Forecast
One number with error bars: "the most likely future". Honest for stable systems over 1–3 years; overconfident beyond that. Your Week 3 projections are forecasts — inputs to scenarios, not substitutes.W4 · W8
Scenario
One internally consistent story of a 2040 world, with a name, a paragraph, three to five quantitative anchors and two or three early signals. "Things go well" is not a scenario; it is a mood.W4 · W5 · W9
Impact × uncertainty matrix
Score each force shaping the future on how much it matters and how unpredictable it is. The high-impact, low-predictability corner is where scenario axes come from.W4 · W5
Critical uncertainty
One of the two forces you choose to build the matrix on. They must be high impact, genuinely open — and independent of each other.W4 · W5
2×2 scenario matrix
Two critical uncertainties as crossed axes, four quadrants, four worlds. Not three cases on one axis (that is a sensitivity analysis dressed up) and not six (unmanageable).W4 · W5 · W9 · W10
Orthogonal axes
Two uncertainties that do not drive each other. "Policy ambition" × "carbon price" fails — one causes the other and two cells collapse. "Policy" × "cost decline" passes. The self-check: write one sentence per cell; if the off-diagonal ones need contortions, fix an axis.W4 · W5
Driver vs outcome
The most common axis mistake: pairing a cause (fragmented policy) with its effect (low delivery). A second axis must be a driver — cost decline, supply-chain geopolitics, capital cost, electrification speed, grid build-out — not a result.W5
Quantitative anchor
A number that pins a scenario to the ground: wind share of EU electricity in 2040, offshore LCOE, capacity in GW, a carbon price. Three per scenario; they later become your indicators.W4 · W5 · W6
Early-warning signal
Something observable in 2026–2030 that would tell you a scenario is unfolding — an auction result, a rate decision, a share of Chinese turbines. Operational enough to build a quarterly review on.W4 · W9 · W10
Robust strategy
One that thrives or at least survives in three or more of the four worlds. A strategy that wins in one and fails in three is a bet, not a strategy.W4 · W9
Thrive · survive · struggle · fail
The four labels you give each company in each scenario. The pattern across the row is the finding.W4 · W9 · W10
STEPS · APS · NZE
The IEA's three World Energy Outlook scenarios: stated policies, announced pledges, net zero by 2050. Benchmarks to compare your worlds with — after you have drafted your own, so you do not anchor on them.W4 · W8
NGFS scenarios
Climate scenarios built for central banks and used in bank stress tests. Worth knowing if you are heading for finance.W4
BloombergNEF · DNV
The two private outlooks (New Energy Outlook; Energy Transition Outlook) your Week 8 cross-check sets your projection against, alongside the IEA.W4 · W8
Week 5 · System mapping · Phase 1

Boundaries, loops and indicators

System boundary
What your analysis includes and what it explicitly leaves out. Your two axes decide it: choose "EU coordination" and the Commission, regulators and capital markets are inside; household electrification may be outside.W5
Causal-loop diagram
Variables joined by arrows, each marked + or −, closing into reinforcing and balancing loops. Sterman's chapter 1 is the reference; two loops minimum in Phase 1.W5
Stock and flow
A stock is an amount that accumulates (installed GW, debt); a flow is a rate that changes it (GW added per year). Confusing the two is how a 2040 capacity number goes wrong.W5
Indicator
A measurable quantity that would tell you which scenario is unfolding. It must be trackable (published regularly), discriminating (moves differently across your worlds) and forecastable.W5 · W6 · W8
Dataset
Where the indicator's numbers actually come from — IRENA, WindEurope, Ember, Eurostat — named with a URL and an access date. Phase 2 cannot start without one per indicator.W5 · W6
Executive summary
The one page a busy reader reads: the question, the answer, the conditions, the signals. Written last, placed first.W5 · W9
Week 6 · The quantitative pivot

Measuring the futures — and the tool

Monte Carlo simulation
Vary the inputs many times at random within plausible ranges and look at the spread of outcomes — a way to put probability bands on an LCOE or a scenario.W6 · W8
Training vs inference
Training is building the model once (GPT-3: enormous); inference is every answer it gives afterwards. Most AI use is inference, and that is where the energy load now grows.W6 · W7 · W10
Wh · kWh per query
A single text query through a large model costs roughly 0.3–10 Wh; a long working session 100–500 Wh. Small per person, a grid load at scale. Your Week 6 audit counts your own.W6
Grid carbon intensitygCO₂/kWh
How much CO₂ a kilowatt-hour from a given grid carries. European data centres: roughly 150–300 as a mid-point; Electricity Maps gives it live. Energy × intensity = your carbon footprint.W6
CO₂-equivalentCO₂e
All greenhouse gases expressed as the amount of CO₂ that would warm the same. The unit a footprint is reported in.W6
Week 7 · Learning curves and their limits

Where the curve breaks

Residuals
The gaps between your data points and the fitted line. Random residuals are fine; a systematic pattern means a variable is missing from the model.W7
Sanity check
Does the projected 2040 number make physical sense? Offshore LCOE below $30/MWh or above $120 should make you nervous. Done by hand, not by the assistant.W7 · W8
Planetary boundariesRockström et al. 2009
Nine biophysical limits — climate, biosphere integrity, biogeochemical flows, freshwater, land, novel entities and more — within which humanity can operate safely. Six are now transgressed (Richardson et al. 2023).W7 · W10
Novel entities
Substances the Earth system has not met before — here, the radioactive and acid wastes of rare-earth processing. The boundary wind's own supply chain presses on.W7
Biosphere integrity
The boundary for living systems: offshore wind's underwater noise, cable fields and fishing displacement; onshore wind's birds and bats.W7
Rare earths · neodymium
The metals in permanent-magnet turbines, mined and processed in a handful of places (Bayan Obo, Mount Weld). The material constraint most likely to bend the learning curve.W7
On-site vs off-site water
Water a data centre evaporates to cool itself versus water the power plant feeding it uses. Off-site often exceeds on-site. Both land somewhere — possibly a water-stressed region.W7
Water stressWRI Aqueduct
Demand for water relative to what a region has. The index that turns "litres" into "litres where it hurts".W7
Week 8 · Forecasting under uncertainty · Phase 2

Honest numbers

Uncertainty band
The range around a central projection — a shaded band or dashed bounds. A forecast without one is a guess presented as a fact. Say which kind of uncertainty the band represents; it is not a 95% confidence interval unless you computed one.W8 · W10
Parameter uncertainty
You do not know the right learning rate. Your conservative / central / aggressive projections capture it.W8
Model uncertainty
You do not know whether Wright's Law is even the right shape. Two methods that disagree (learning curve vs time series) capture it.W8
Scenario uncertainty
The world you projected from may not be the one that arrives. Your four-scenario matrix captures it.W8
S-curve
Slow start, rapid growth, saturation — the shape most technologies follow. Linear extrapolation misses the flattening; an S-curve fit is better for a maturing market.W3 · W8
Cross-check
Your 2040 number tabulated beside the IEA's, BloombergNEF's and DNV's. If yours is well outside all three you need a reason — different boundary conditions, or a known blind spot like the IEA's long under-estimation of solar.W8 · W9
Week 9 · The recommendation · Phase 3

From analyst to advisor

Analyst vs advisor
An analyst describes the world and can hedge across four scenarios. An advisor commits to a position the client should take, names the most likely world and the most dangerous one to ignore. Phase 3 is the switch.W9
First-generation scenariosWack 1985
Plausible, interesting descriptions of possible futures that the executive reads, nods at, and changes nothing. They confirm the priors.W9 · W10
Second-generation scenarios
Scenarios that "rattle the manager's microcosm": vivid enough to force a re-perception of the present and change the decision. The test for your Phase 3: would the CEO change anything?W9 · W10
Predetermined elements
What is already locked in by 2040 — turbines ordered, auctions awarded, demographics — as opposed to the genuine uncertainties. Wack's first sorting move.W9
Perspective
Whose advice this is. A pension fund wants robustness; a growth investor accepts variance; a government weighs other things. "From the perspective of a long-horizon institutional investor…" is a recommendation a client can use.W9 · W10
Peer challenge
Two classmates attack your draft — weakest link, counter-evidence, the question a hostile client would ask — before you submit. Challenge the argument, never the person; the best challenges are the hardest on the work and the kindest to the author.W9
AI declaration
The short note at the end of every hand-in: which assistants, for what, what you verified yourself, and (from Phase 3) the energy and water you estimated in Weeks 6–7.W5 · W8 · W9
Week 10 · The exam

The words on the exam page

Open book
Your notes, the readings, this site and your team's report are allowed; AI assistants, translation tools and classmates are not. Two hours, sixty questions, one attempt, in order — the notes can check a definition, they cannot make the move for you.W10
Sequential questions
Sixty multiple-choice questions answered in order; no skipping, no going back. Two hours from the moment you start; one attempt; late is zero.W10
Transfer
What the exam tests: the same toolkit — systems, scenarios, indicators, learning curves, uncertainty, boundaries — applied to a case you have not seen. Not the Ørsted numbers.W10
