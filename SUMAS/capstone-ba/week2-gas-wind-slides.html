Gas, wind and the industrial energy fight

Wind is winning power generation. The harder contest, and the bigger prize, is the energy European industry uses: volumes, costs, the 2022 price shock, and why industry is harder to decarbonise than power.

The crisis lesson

At €340, energy became an existential cost: German chemical plants cut output, fertiliser producers shut down.

Why wind looked cheap

Fixed-price wind contracts at €55–75 drove a wave of industrial PPAs in 2022–23.

The normalisation problem

With gas back near €35, industrial electricity at about €85 is again dearer for direct heat. The crisis made the case; normalisation softened it.

The hard-to-abate problem

Where gas cannot simply be replaced

The difficulty is thermodynamic and economic: high-temperature heat and chemical feedstocks cannot be replaced by electrons alone.

How hard, by sector

Hard (feedstock or heat above 400 °C): chemicals, iron and steel, cement, glass.
Moderate: paper and pulp, food and beverage.
Electrifiable now: aluminium, machinery.

The green premium · € per tonne of CO₂ avoided

Green ammonia (electrolysis) 220 · green steel (hydrogen) 180 · industrial heat above 400 °C (hydrogen boiler) 140 · cement with carbon capture 95 · food and beverage steam 25 · heat below 200 °C (heat pump) 15 · aluminium 5

The EU carbon price averaged about €65 a tonne in 2024: enough for low-temperature heat, far from enough for steel or ammonia.

Strategic implications

What this means for Ørsted and Eni

Ørsted · the industrial opportunity

Industrial PPAs with energy-intensive manufacturers: a revenue pillar and a hedge against market prices.
Green hydrogen (offshore wind + electrolysis) is the long game, if its cost falls on a learning curve.
The timing risk: industry needs supply certainty before it retrofits plants; project delays cost credibility.
The scale: replacing EU industrial gas with green hydrogen would need ~800–1,000 GW of offshore wind, 24–30 times EU offshore capacity today (~33 GW).

Eni · the last stronghold and its limits

Industrial gas is gas's best remaining argument: feedstock and high-temperature heat need it longest, and Eni's gas and LNG business serves it.
Carbon pricing closes the window as the ETS rises toward €100–130/t.
Carbon capture is Eni's bridge: decarbonise industry without abandoning gas.
The capital test: every euro in new gas infrastructure is a euro not in Plenitude or hydrogen.

Consulting hypothesis: the industrial transition will be decided by green hydrogen costs and the carbon price, not by the offshore wind learning curve alone.

Wright's Law

Why the cost keeps falling

The rule

Every time cumulative production doubles, cost falls by a fixed percentage, the learning rate. It does not matter whether the doubling takes two years or ten.

Why it compounds

Cheaper technology → more deployment → another doubling → another cost cut. A reinforcing loop.

Deployment is the R&D

Technologies deployed at scale get much cheaper; those that are not stagnate. Policy that drives deployment drives cost down.

Offshore wind: about 11–15% per doubling. Solar PV: about 23%. New nuclear: about zero, or negative.

The long run

Larger turbines, a learning supply chain and experienced developers: a fall of 54–60%, not the "~70%" the original deck claimed.

The shock

Inflation, interest rates and supply chains. Ørsted booked DKK 28.4 bn of impairments in the first nine months of 2023 on US projects priced before inflation.

The recovery

The curve resumed in 2024: the interruption looks cyclical. Week 4 shows why: most of the cost of offshore wind is the cost of money.

The strategic tension

What falling LCOE means for Ørsted and Eni

Ørsted · riding the curve

Wright's Law is the business model: every cost fall validates the pivot from fossil fuels.
A reinforcing loop: lower LCOE → more viable projects → more scale → lower LCOE.
2022–23 exposed the execution risk: projects at fixed pre-inflation prices became loss-making; the cost curve is not project profit.
The open question: can it keep going in deeper water and harder markets?

Eni · straddling the divide

Falling LCOE erodes the fossil case: industrial buyers prefer fixed-price renewable contracts to volatile gas.
Stranded-asset risk: gas infrastructure is a 20–30-year investment and may be uncompetitive before it is paid off.
The hedge: gas as a "transition fuel" holds today; to 2035 depends on the curve.
The core question: can Plenitude grow as fast as costs fall, or do fossil returns crowd it out?

Consulting hypothesis: Ørsted's risk is execution at scale in adverse markets; Eni's is a strategic identity that is hard to sustain if the cost curve continues for another decade.
