Core concepts · 1

From Phase 1 narrative to Phase 2 numbers

Phase 1 was a system map. Phase 2 is a stress test.

Your Phase 1 said something like: "In a Climate-First + High-China-Integration scenario, costs fall fastest and deployment accelerates." Phase 2 has to answer the harder question: how would I know if that is the scenario actually unfolding right now? What number is moving, in which direction, by how much?

That is why the indicators you proposed in Phase 1 matter so much. They are now your measurement instruments. The next three weeks depend on having two or three that are:

  • Trackable — published regularly by credible sources (IRENA, BloombergNEF, WindEurope, IEA, Ember)
  • Discriminating — they move differently across your scenarios; if all four worlds produce the same number, the indicator is useless
  • Forecastable — you can project them to 2040 with some method, however imperfect

Most Phase 1 submissions name 3–5 indicators. For Phase 2, narrow to 2–3 you will actually analyse quantitatively. The rest stay in your bibliography.

Core concepts · 2

Methods you might use

You met these in Week 3. Now you commit to one or two:

MethodWhat it doesWhen to use it
Regression / curve fittingFit a function through historical dataWright's Law on LCOE, learning-rate estimation
Time-series forecastingProject future values from past valuesEU wind capacity to 2040, generation growth
Monte Carlo simulationVary input drivers, see the distribution of outcomesCapital-cost sensitivity on LCOE, scenario probability bands
ClusteringGroup similar things togetherSegment EU offshore markets by maturity, cost, policy support

You don't need to build any of these from scratch. You need to know which one your indicators call for, and how to use Claude / ChatGPT / Perplexity to apply it.

A Monte Carlo in one paragraph, since the table mentions itTake your LCOE formula. Instead of one value for the cost of capital, give it a range — say 5% to 9% — and let a spreadsheet draw a random value 1,000 times, recomputing LCOE each time. The 1,000 results are a distribution: "LCOE is $54/MWh" becomes "LCOE is between $48 and $66 in 90% of draws, and the cost of capital explains most of that". It is the honest way to put a band around a number when the uncertainty is in an input, not in the data. Ask your assistant for the spreadsheet formula; it is =NORM.INV(RAND(), mean, sd) and a copy-down.
Core concepts · 3

The AI you are using has an energy cost

By the end of Phase 2 you will have spent significant compute on this project. Luccioni and colleagues quantify what that costs. Some numbers to hold in your head while reading:

  • Generating one image with a large model — roughly the energy of charging your phone halfway
  • A single text query through a GPT-4-class model — 0.3 to 10 Wh, depending on length and model
  • A long working session with extensive back-and-forth, document uploads and image generation — easily 100–500 Wh

That is small per person. At scale — millions of users, several sessions a day — it becomes a measurable load on the grid. Some of that grid is wind. Some of it isn't.

Your AI footprint · the calculation Activity 2 asks for

Defaults: 3 Wh per turn is mid-range for a GPT-4-class model on a longish prompt (Luccioni et al. give 0.3–10); ~3 Wh per image is their Table 3 figure for the largest image model (2.9 kWh per 1,000); 220 gCO₂/kWh is a European mid-point (150–300). Use Electricity Maps ↗ for the data-centre region you think your provider routes through. The point is not precision; the point is that the number exists and you can estimate it within an order of magnitude.

Why this matters for your consulting recommendation: if you are going to claim that wind helps decarbonise European electricity, you should also be able to estimate whether AI tools — growing fast, run in data centres, increasingly sited near cheap renewable supply — are net helpful or net harmful to that decarbonisation story. Week 7 returns to this with the water footprint paper.

Activities · this week

The proposal, the audit, and an optional timeline

Activity 1 — Phase 2 proposal · on the report page by

Submit a ~600-word Phase 2 proposal that I can approve before you begin the data work. It must include:

The central quantitative question your Phase 2 will answer. One sentence, derived directly from your Phase 1 scenarios. Example: "How fast must offshore wind LCOE fall for the Hyper-Scale Transition scenario to remain plausible by 2040?"
The 2–3 indicators you will track. For each: what it measures, why it discriminates between your scenarios, the source (with URL), and how often it is updated.
The analytical method(s) you will apply, and why. Be specific. "I will fit a Wright's Law curve to historical offshore wind LCOE 2010–2024 using log-log regression, then project to 2040 under three learning-rate assumptions (10%, 15%, 20%)." Not "I will analyse the data."
One paragraph on what could go wrong. Data gaps, definitional inconsistencies (Europe vs EU-27), method failure modes, anything that might invalidate your conclusions. Acknowledging this in advance is what separates analysis from advocacy.

Activity 2 — Your AI audit. Estimate the energy and carbon footprint of producing your Phase 1 report.

  1. Review your Phase 1 chat history with Claude, ChatGPT or whichever assistant you used. Count the substantial conversational turns (a turn = a question + answer).

  3. Convert energy to CO₂-equivalent with a reasonable grid factor — for European data centres serving European users, ~150–300 gCO₂/kWh is a rough mid-point.

Wednesday's topic · report §4 and §6

The supply chain and China

Europe still builds most of its own offshore turbines. Its dependence on China sits one or two levels below them: in magnets, castings, steel, and in the ships and cables needed to install and connect them. Cheap wind and secure wind pull in opposite directions.

Italy is in the middle of it. Beleolico off Taranto (2022, ten Mingyang turbines) was the first offshore farm in the Mediterranean and the first in Europe with Chinese turbines. Germany (Waterkant) and the UK (the Ardersier factory) have both turned Mingyang away on security grounds.

Week 7 preview · Learning curves and their limits

Next week you take the Wright's Law work from Week 3 and apply it to your own indicators. But you also confront the limit: Rockström and colleagues defined the planetary boundaries the global economy is pressing against — rare earths, steel, biodiversity, freshwater. Learning curves quietly assume scaling is unbounded. The planet says otherwise. And Li et al. (2025) extend the thinking to AI itself: every query also has a water footprint. Bring: an approved Phase 2 proposal, the historical series for at least one indicator, and a working hypothesis about where the cost curve might bend or break.
