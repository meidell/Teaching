A cautionary tale

Ørsted, 2023: excellent forecasts, no scenario

What happened

In autumn 2023 Ørsted cancelled Ocean Wind 1 and 2 off New Jersey and booked DKK 28.4 billion of impairments (about €3.8 billion) for the year, most of it on Ocean Wind 1. Its share price had fallen by more than half within a year.

Why

Four things moved at once: interest rates (up more than four percentage points in 2022–23), turbine and steel costs, the supply chain, and US permitting and tax-credit timing. Each was visible in 2021. Nobody had put them together in one written, named future and tested the strategy against it.

A forecast asks what is most likely. Ørsted needed to ask what it must be ready for.

The distinction

Forecast vs scenario

ForecastScenario planning
AsksWhat is the most likely future?What futures must we be ready for?
OutputOne number, with error barsThree or four internally consistent stories, each with numbers
Works forStable systems, 1–3 yearsSystems in transition, 10–30 years
Fails byOverconfidence: blind to a change of regimeVague stories that do not change any decision

Your Week 3 projections are forecasts. They are inputs to scenarios, not substitutes for them.

Four things get called "scenarios"

Only one of them is

ForecastOne best estimate of one future. One line, one number.
SensitivityOne input varied at a time: "what if gas is +20%?" Tests a model; it is not a scenario.
ContingencyA plan for one bad event, the "downside case". Still assumes one future.
ScenarioSeveral coherent futures built on different axes, used to stress-test a strategy.

Schoemaker (1995) draws this line. Best / base / worst on one axis is a sensitivity analysis, not scenario planning.

Where the method comes from

Shell, 1973, and a distinction from 1921

Pierre Wack at Shell

From the late 1960s Wack's team at Royal Dutch Shell wrote scenarios instead of single forecasts, including one in which oil producers unite and prices jump. In October 1973 the oil embargo came and the crude price roughly quadrupled. Shell had already thought it through. Wack described the method in the Harvard Business Review in 1985.

Frank Knight: risk vs uncertainty

Risk can be given a probability, because history is a fair guide: the chance a wind farm produces 5% less than expected in a given year. Uncertainty cannot: EU climate policy in 2040. Scenarios are the tool for uncertainty.

Knight, Risk, Uncertainty and Profit (1921) · Wack, "Scenarios: Uncharted Waters Ahead", HBR (1985)

Step 3 and step 4

Plan around trends, build scenarios around uncertainties

Trends · fairly predictable

Electrification of transport and heat · turbines keep getting larger · old fossil plants retire on known dates · climate targets already in law · data-centre demand rising

They go into every scenario.

Uncertainties · could go either way

Does the EU hold its climate line after 2030? Does the learning rate continue or stall? Do grid and permitting bottlenecks ease? Is the supply chain open or used as a weapon? Do interest rates stay high?

Your two axes come from here.

The test: if you are fairly sure which way it goes, it is a trend, not an axis.

Step 5 · the hardest judgement call

Picking your two axes

  1. Truly uncertain. Both ends of the axis must feel plausible in 2040.
  2. High impact on the question. The answer to "is wind a sound investment?" must differ at each end. Otherwise the axis is decoration.
  3. Independent of each other. If axis B is partly caused by axis A, you get three worlds, not four.

The orthogonality test: "If axis A lands at one end, does that tell me anything about axis B?" If yes, change one of them.

Before you commit

Some forces travel together

EU policy—weakSTRONGmoderate
Interest ratesweak—weakSTRONG
Carbon priceSTRONGweak—moderate

EU policy and the carbon price cannot be two axes: the carbon price is a policy instrument. High rates raise the cost of capital and slow cost decline, so those two are linked as well.

Make the second axis a driver, not an outcome of the first.

Verdicts

What a pass and a fail look like

Fail · the axes move together

"EU coordination" × "delivery capacity". A fragmented Europe also delivers less, so the off-diagonal worlds need a long story to exist. The second axis is an outcome of the first.

Pass · two drivers

"EU policy ambition" × "pace of cost decline". A strong-policy world can still see slow cost decline if supply chains seize; a weak-policy world can still get cheap turbines from global manufacturing.

Drivers that pair well with policy: cost decline · supply-chain geopolitics · interest rates · speed of electrification · grid build-out.

Step 6 · three checks

Is each world a scenario, or a mood?

Consistent

Every element fits the axis it sits on. "High carbon price + weak policy" fails: one drives the other.

Distinct

Four different worlds, not one story with four numbers. If all four are "growth", you have missed a break.

Plausible

Each world can unfold from today without a miracle. Start from where Europe is in 2026.

The newspaper test: write the front-page headline of 14 March 2040 in that world. If you cannot, the world is still too vague.

Early signals

How would you know which world is coming?

For each world, two or three signals you could see between 2026 and 2030. Examples for Renaissance:

  • Offshore auctions clear below €60/MWh in three rounds in a row
  • The EU's 2040 climate target is written into law without being weakened
  • A large offshore project is commissioned on time and on budget

Igor Ansoff (1975) called these weak signals. In Phase 3 they become the early-warning dashboard for your client (report §17).

From scenarios to strategy

Wind tunnelling

Take a strategy and put it in each world, like a model plane in a wind tunnel. Does it thrive, survive, struggle or fail? What breaks first?

Robust

Works in all four worlds. Lower upside, lower downside.

Flexible

Keeps options open: pay a little now for the right to commit later, when the signals are clear.

Bet

Wins big in one world, fails in others. Only with real conviction about that axis.

Your two companies

Ørsted and Eni through this lens

Ørsted · pure-play

Sold its oil and gas; everything on offshore wind. A bet: wins in Renaissance, exposed in Stalled and when capital is expensive. Little optionality: committed capacity. 2023 is what failing in one world looks like.

Eni · hedged

Keeps oil and gas; grows renewables inside Plenitude, with minority offshore stakes. Robust and flexible: rarely first, rarely last. Exposed in Renaissance, where pure-plays take the market.

Which is right depends on which worlds you take seriously. That is the question your report answers.

Five pitfalls

What to avoid in Phase 1

  1. Best / base / worst. That is sensitivity analysis on one axis.
  2. Axes that move together. Policy × carbon price gives two worlds, not four.
  3. The official future in disguise. One world equals the EU target; the other three exist to flatter it.
  4. No numbers. Without anchors a scenario is a mood.
  5. No strategy test. Scenarios that never meet Ørsted and Eni are just stories.
