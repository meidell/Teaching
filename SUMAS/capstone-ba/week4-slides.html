Why a company builds scenarios

A plan built on one forecast fails when the forecast does

Scenario analysis is a method in which a company writes several different but believable descriptions of the future (usually four), and then tests its strategy in each one: "would our plan still work if this happened?"

Nobody can predict Europe's electricity system in 2040: interest rates, climate policy, China, the grid are all open. A forecast gives one number; if the number is wrong, every decision built on it is wrong too.

A strategy that works in all four scenarios is robust. A strategy that works in only one is a bet. That is what the client pays you for in Phase 1: not a prediction of 2040, but a test of two companies' plans against futures they cannot control.

An example · Ørsted, 2023

Good forecasts, no scenarios: what it cost Ørsted

What happened

In autumn 2023 Ørsted cancelled two offshore projects off New Jersey, Ocean Wind 1 and 2, and wrote down DKK 28.4 billion (about €3.8 billion) in the first nine months of 2023, DKK 19.9 billion of it on Ocean Wind 1. A write-down (impairment) means the company declares that an asset is worth less than it paid. The dividend was suspended for 2023–2025.

Why

Four things moved at once: interest rates (up more than four percentage points in 2022–23), turbine and steel prices, the supply chain, and the timing of US permits and tax credits. Each was visible in 2021. Nobody had written them down together as one possible future and asked "what do we do if this is the world?"

Ørsted interim report, Q3 2023. A forecast asks what is most likely. Ørsted needed to ask what it must be ready for.

The cone of uncertainty

The further ahead you look, the wider the range of possible futures

The cone of uncertainty: draw the possible values of a number, say Europe's wind capacity, year by year from today. For next year they sit close together; for 2040 they spread far apart. The shape is a cone opening from today. It shows where a single forecast still works and where it stops working.

2026–2028

1–3 years. The projects are already financed. A forecast works here.

2030

5 years. Auctions, interest rates and supply chains can still move the number a lot.

2040

15 years. Policy, technology and geopolitics can all change direction. One number here is a guess presented as an answer.

Your Week 3 learning curve is one line through the middle of this cone: the central estimate, not a certainty.

Two different questions

A forecast asks what is most likely; a scenario asks what we must be ready for

ForecastScenario analysis
The questionWhat is the most likely future?Which futures must we be ready for?
The outputOne number, with a margin of errorThree or four different, internally consistent descriptions of the future, each with numbers
Works forStable systems, 1–3 years aheadSystems in transition, 10–30 years ahead
Fails whenThe world changes regime and the forecast cannot see itThe stories are vague and change no decision

Your Week 3 projections are forecasts. They are inputs to the scenarios, not substitutes for them.

Four things people call "scenarios"

Only the fourth one is a scenario

ForecastOne best estimate of one future. One line, one number: "wind supplies 38% of EU electricity in 2040".
Sensitivity analysisOne input changed at a time, everything else fixed: "what if the gas price is 20% higher?". It tests a model. It is not a scenario.
Contingency planA plan for one bad event, the "downside case". It still assumes one future.
ScenarioSeveral coherent futures, built on two independent uncertainties, each with a story and numbers, used to test a strategy.

Schoemaker (1995) draws this line. "Best case, base case, worst case" on one axis is a sensitivity analysis, not scenario analysis.

Where the method comes from

Shell in 1973, and a distinction from 1921

Pierre Wack at Shell

From the late 1960s Wack's team at Royal Dutch Shell wrote scenarios instead of single forecasts. One of them described oil producers uniting and prices jumping. In October 1973 the oil embargo came and the crude price roughly quadrupled. Shell had already thought it through. Wack described the method in the Harvard Business Review in 1985.

Frank Knight: risk and uncertainty are different

Risk can be given a probability, because the past is a fair guide: the chance that a wind farm produces 5% less than expected in a given year. Uncertainty cannot: EU climate policy in 2040 has no track record to count. Scenarios are the tool for uncertainty.

Knight, Risk, Uncertainty and Profit (1921) · Wack, "Scenarios: Uncharted Waters Ahead", HBR (1985)

Steps 3 and 4

Trends go into every scenario; uncertainties become the axes

A trend is a change you are fairly sure will continue. An uncertainty is a force that could go either way, and you cannot tell which.

Trends · fairly predictable

Electrification of transport and heating · turbines keep getting larger · old fossil plants close on known dates · climate targets already written into law · data-centre demand rising.

They go into every scenario, the same way.

Uncertainties · could go either way

Does the EU keep its climate line after 2030? Does the cost of wind keep falling, or stall? Do grid and permitting bottlenecks ease? Is the supply chain open, or used as a weapon? Do interest rates stay high?

Your two axes come from here.

The test: if you are fairly sure which way it goes, it is a trend, not an axis.

The filter

Score every force on two things: how much it matters, and how unpredictable it is

↑ impact on the question "is wind a sound investment?"
High impact · low uncertaintyA trend. Put it in every world as a baseline. Example: electrification.
High impact · high uncertaintyA critical uncertainty. Your two axes come from this corner. Examples: EU policy after 2030; the pace of cost decline.
Low impact · low uncertaintyIgnore it. Example: the price of office paper.
Low impact · high uncertaintyWatch it, do not build on it. Example: blade-recycling technology.
uncertainty →

On the Week 4 page the sliders do this scoring for you: each force gets impact 1–5 and uncertainty 1–5, and the chart shows which ones land in the top-right corner.

Step 5 · the hardest judgement

A good pair of axes passes three tests

  1. Truly uncertain. Both ends of the axis must be believable in 2040. "Strong policy" and "weak policy" both have to be possible.
  2. High impact on the question. The answer to "is wind a sound investment?" must be different at the two ends. If not, the axis is decoration.
  3. Independent of each other. Knowing how one axis turns out must tell you nothing about the other. If axis B is partly caused by axis A, one corner of the grid cannot happen, and you have two or three worlds, not four.
The independence check (the technical word is "orthogonal", at right angles): write one sentence for each of the four corners. Look hardest at the two mixed corners, one axis high and the other low. If a corner can only be described with a far-fetched story, the two axes are not independent: keep the cause and replace the effect with another uncertainty.
Before you commit

Some forces move together, so they cannot be two axes

How strongly one drives the otherEU policyInterest ratesCarbon priceCost decline
EU policy—weakSTRONGmoderate
Interest ratesweak—weakSTRONG
Carbon priceSTRONGweak—moderate

EU policy and the carbon price cannot be two axes: the carbon price is set by policy, so the corner "strong policy, low carbon price" cannot exist. High interest rates raise the cost of capital and slow the fall in costs, so those two are linked as well. EU policy and interest rates are a good pair: central banks do not set rates to help wind.

Rule: make the second axis a cause of change, not an effect of the first axis.

What a pass and a fail look like

Two examples of axis pairs

Fails · the axes move together

"EU coordination" × "delivery capacity". A Europe that coordinates badly also delivers less, so the corner "poor coordination, high delivery" needs a long story to exist. The second axis is an effect of the first.

Passes · two independent causes

"EU policy ambition" × "pace of cost decline". A strong-policy world can still have slow cost decline if supply chains seize up; a weak-policy world can still get cheap turbines from global manufacturing. All four corners can happen.

Uncertainties that pair well with policy: the pace of cost decline · supply-chain geopolitics · interest rates · the speed of electrification · how fast the grid is built.

Step 7 · numbers

Each world needs three to five numbers, called anchors

An anchor is a number for 2040 that pins a world down: the wind share of electricity, the cost per MWh, the carbon price. Without anchors a scenario is a mood; with them you can see whether the story holds together.
Anchor for 2040RenaissanceSubsidisedMarket-drivenStalled
Wind's share of EU electricity55%40%35%22%
Offshore wind cost (€ per MWh)42685585
EU carbon price (€ per tonne)1801606045

Illustrative numbers, to show the shape. Today wind is about 17–19% of EU electricity (Ember). Your own anchors need a source and a reason. A world called "fast cost decline" with a cost of €70 per MWh does not add up.

Step 6 · three checks, and early signals

Is each world a real scenario, and how would you know it is coming?

Consistent

Every detail fits the axes it sits on. "High carbon price with weak policy" fails: one causes the other.

Distinct

Four different worlds, not one story with four numbers. If all four are "growth", you have missed a possible break.

Plausible

Each world can unfold from where Europe is in 2026 without a miracle.

An early signal is something you could observe between 2026 and 2030 that tells you which world is arriving. For "Renaissance": offshore auctions clearing below €60 per MWh three rounds in a row; the EU's 2040 climate target written into law unchanged; a large offshore project finished on time and on budget. Two or three per world; in Phase 3 they become the client's early-warning dashboard (report §17).
From scenarios to strategy

Put each company's strategy into each world and see whether it survives

Take a strategy and place it in each of the four worlds, like a model plane in a wind tunnel (the industry calls this "wind tunnelling"). In each world, does the strategy thrive, survive, struggle or fail? What breaks first?

Robust

Works in all four worlds. Less to gain, less to lose.

Flexible

Keeps options open: pay a little now for the right to commit later, when the signals are clearer.

A bet

Wins big in one world and fails in the others. Only with real conviction about that axis.

Ørsted · all on offshore wind

Sold its oil and gas. A bet: wins in "Renaissance", exposed in "Stalled" and whenever money is expensive. 2023 is what failing in one world looks like.

Eni · keeps oil and gas, grows renewables in Plenitude

Robust and flexible: rarely first, rarely last. Exposed in "Renaissance", where companies that went all in take the market.
