Learning curves and their limits

Last week you committed to your indicators, your method and your hypothesis. This week you do the work. But there is a twist: the technique you are applying — Wright's Law — quietly assumes that scaling is unbounded. Every doubling of cumulative capacity reduces unit cost by some fixed percentage, for ever. That assumption is the engine driving every optimistic 2040 wind forecast in circulation. The planet says otherwise.

Core concepts · 1 and 2

Fitting your curve, and producing three projections

Wright's Law in standard form: Cost(Q) = Cost(Q₀) × (Q / Q₀)^(−b), where Q is cumulative installed capacity, b the learning parameter, and the learning rate = 1 − 2^(−b). A learning rate of 15% means every doubling of cumulative capacity reduces unit cost by 15%.

For offshore wind LCOE, published learning rates fall in the range of 9–18% depending on time window and geographic scope. Onshore wind is typically 8–12%. Solar PV is famously 20%+ over the long run — which is why solar has surprised every forecaster who ever projected it linearly. You are not committing to a published number. You are fitting one to your data, and interpreting it.

Three checks before you trust your fit

  • Visual inspection — does the log-log plot actually look linear? If it is curving, Wright's Law is not the right model.
  • Residuals — are the deviations from the curve random, or systematic? Systematic residuals mean a missing variable.
  • Sanity — does the projected 2040 cost make physical sense? An offshore LCOE below $30/MWh by 2040 should make you nervous. So should one above $120.

Conservative / central / aggressive is not a hedge

It is a structured way to surface your assumptions:

ScenarioWhat you are assumingWhen this is plausible
ConservativeLearning rate slows; supply chain binds; financing costs stay elevatedFragmented Europe, broken supply chains, high rates persist
CentralHistorical learning rate continues; modest supply-chain adjustmentRoughly the world we are in now, extrapolated
AggressiveLearning accelerates; supply-chain reorganisation succeeds; capital becomes abundantCoordinated Europe, technology breakthroughs, the rate cycle turns

Notice how these map directly onto the 2×2 you built in Phase 1. That is the point. Your three projections are not arbitrary — they are the quantified versions of your scenarios.

Core concepts · 3

Where the curve breaks — planetary boundaries

Rockström and colleagues defined nine planetary boundaries. The 2023 update concluded that six are now in the danger zone: climate change, biosphere integrity, biogeochemical flows, land-system change, freshwater, and novel entities. European wind scaling pushes against several of them. Tap each boundary:

The pattern matters: wind solves one planetary-boundary problem partly by pressing on others. That is not a reason to stop building it. It is a reason to be honest about the trade-offs when you make your final recommendation. The same logic applies to AI: Luccioni gave you the energy and carbon. Li et al. give you the water.

Core concepts · 4

The water you didn't measure last week

Last week's AI audit estimated your project's energy and carbon. It almost certainly did not include water. Li et al.'s headline numbers: training GPT-3 used ~700,000 litres of fresh water, almost all for data-centre cooling; a short ChatGPT conversation (~20–50 queries) consumes roughly 500 ml — a small bottle; and off-site water (used by the power plants generating the data centre's electricity) often exceeds on-site cooling water.

Water footprint of your project to date · extend Week 6's audit

15 ml per turn is Li et al.'s 500 ml ÷ ~30 queries, a mid-point; the off-site factor depends entirely on the generation mix — thermal plants withdraw litres per kWh, wind and solar almost none, which is itself a finding for your report. Where does the water land? Data centres cluster in regions chosen for cheap electricity and cool climates: Arizona, Iowa, parts of Spain and Ireland. Use WRI Aqueduct ↗ for the water-stress index of the region you think your provider routes through.

This matters for your final recommendation. If you are going to argue that wind helps decarbonise Europe, and you are using AI to make that argument, you should be able to say something honest about the water cost of your method.

Activities · this week's forums

Fit it, then break it

Apply your chosen analytical method to your headline indicator. Produce three 2040 projections.

Pull the historical series for your headline indicator — likely offshore wind LCOE, EU wind capacity, or wind's share of EU generation. Aim for at least 10 data points across 10+ years.
Use Claude / ChatGPT / Perplexity to fit the curve. Sample prompt: "I have annual offshore wind LCOE data from 2010 to 2024 [paste data]. Fit a Wright's Law curve of the form Cost(Q) = Cost(Q₀) × (Q/Q₀)^(−b) using cumulative installed capacity. Report the learning rate. Project 2030, 2035 and 2040 under three scenarios: 10% learning rate (conservative), 15% (central), 20% (aggressive)."
Verify the maths yourself. Don't trust the AI. Spot-check at least one calculation by hand or in a spreadsheet — or in the fitter above.
Produce a chart — log-log plot of your fit plus the three forward projections. A screenshot is fine at this stage; this is not your final Phase 2 figure.
Post 350–450 words: your learning rate and how it compares to published figures · your three 2040 projections · which scenario is closest to the central trajectory of your Phase 1 matrix, and why · one thing the AI got wrong or had to be pushed on · one sanity check that surprised you.

Reply to two peers by Sunday evening: focus on whether their assumptions are defensible, not whether their numbers match yours.

Apply the planetary-boundaries framework to your specific consulting question.

  1. Of Rockström's nine boundaries, identify the two most likely to constrain European wind scaling to 2040. Be specific about which materials, ecosystems or processes are doing the constraining.
  2. Find at least one piece of quantitative evidence for each — projected rare-earth demand vs supply, biodiversity impact studies, water intensity of steel production, end-of-life blade volumes.
  3. Ask: at what level of European wind deployment does each boundary start to bind? Be quantitative if you can. "Neodymium demand for permanent-magnet turbines exceeds X% of current global production at roughly Y GW/year of additions" is the kind of sentence you are aiming for.
  4. Post 300–400 words: your two boundaries and why these rather than others · the evidence · what this means for your aggressive 2040 scenario — does it remain plausible once you account for these constraints? · whether this changes which company (Ørsted or TotalEnergies) is better positioned, and why.

This is the activity that distinguishes a consultant from a fan of wind energy. Anyone can project the curve. Few can tell you when it breaks.

Extend your Week 6 AI audit to include water, using Li et al.'s figures and the calculator above. Distinguish on-site (cooling) from off-site (electricity) water. If you can identify which data-centre region your provider routes through (most publish sustainability disclosures), use a region-specific water-stress index — WRI Aqueduct is the standard. A 100–200-word reflection is enough. The point is honesty: you are using a tool that has a water cost, in regions that may be water-stressed. A recommendation that ignores this is incomplete.
