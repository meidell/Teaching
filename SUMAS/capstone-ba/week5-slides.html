Europe can build wind farms faster than it can build the wires to carry their power. That gap decides how much of your 2040 is physically possible.

Vote in the chat · T or W

What slows European wind more today?

T: building enough turbines. W: building enough wires to connect them and carry their power.

Tonight's answer: the wires. The International Energy Agency counts at least 3,000 GW of renewable projects waiting for a grid connection worldwide, 1,500 GW of them already advanced. That is about five times the wind and solar the world added in 2022.

IEA, Electricity Grids and Secure Energy Transitions, October 2023

IEA, 2023. EU law now caps permits for renewables at 12 months onshore and 2 years offshore inside acceleration areas (RED III, Directive 2023/2413). There is no such cap that makes a 700 km line quick.

An Ørsted example

Built, and waiting for the cable

Borkum Riffgrund 3, 913 MW, Germany's largest offshore wind farm: the turbines were up, but the grid operator's export link (TenneT's DolWin5) was not. Commercial operation slipped to early 2026.

Who builds what

In Germany and the Netherlands the transmission system operator builds the offshore connection; the developer builds the farm. Two companies, two timetables, one revenue stream.

What the developer loses

Every month of delay is a month of capital spent and no power sold. Week 4: the cost of capital is the biggest line in an offshore wind farm's costs.

As reported in the trade press, 2025. Check the current status in Ørsted's latest interim report before you cite it.

Germany · wind in the north, industry in the south

When the line is not there

The geography

Most German wind is in the north and in the North Sea. Much of the demand is in Bavaria and Baden-Württemberg. SuedLink, a 700 km direct-current line between them, was due in 2022. It is now due at the end of 2028, at about €10 bn, partly because it was moved underground after local protests.

What happens meanwhile

When the wind blows, the grid operator orders northern wind farms to reduce output and southern power plants to increase it, so the line is not overloaded. This is redispatch. The switched-off farm is compensated; the southern plant is paid to run.

The capture rate

Wind earns less than the average price

By hand · Germany, 2025

Average spot price: 8.932 ct/kWh (€89.32/MWh).
What onshore wind actually earned on average: 7.441 ct/kWh.
7.441 ÷ 8.932 = 83%. That ratio is the capture rate.

Why

All wind farms produce at the same windy hours, so they push the price down exactly when they sell. The more wind on the system, the stronger the effect. Solar is hit harder: about 50% in Germany in 2025.

A 2040 world with twice the wind and no new flexibility is a world with a lower capture rate.

Market values: Rödl & Partner from the German market-value data, February 2026; the ratio is computed. S&P Global gives about 82% for 2024.

The Iberian blackout

Spain and Portugal lost power almost entirely. The final expert report (ENTSO-E, March 2026) found no single cause: oscillations, gaps in voltage control and differing voltage practices led to fast voltage increases and a cascade of power plants disconnecting.

What it does not say

It does not blame "too much solar". Be careful with that claim in your report; it is common in the press and not what the investigators found.

Why it matters here

A grid with more wind and solar needs more control: voltage, inertia, coordination, and links to neighbours. Spain is 3.11% interconnected.

Sorting

Turbine problem or grid problem?

  1. A finished offshore farm waits a year for its export cable. Grid
  2. Blades fail on a new turbine model and must be replaced. Turbine
  3. Scottish farms are paid to stop because the line south is full. Grid
  4. Wind earns 83% of the average price. Grid: too little flexibility to move the surplus
  5. A project waits years in a connection queue. Grid

Your two companies

Who carries the grid risk?

Ørsted · a generator

Sells power into the grid from large offshore farms. Exposed to connection delays (Borkum Riffgrund 3), to curtailment where it is not compensated, and to falling capture prices where it sells at market prices. A long-term price contract shifts much of that risk away.

Eni · Plenitude

A smaller renewable fleet, spread across technologies and countries, and a retail business that sells power to customers. In Italy it works in a grid with 5.13% interconnection. The question for your report: does owning the customer reduce the grid risk, or only move it?

Week 2's balancing loop, with tonight's numbers

The loop that slows the transition

More wind → more congestion (+) → more curtailment and negative prices (+) → lower revenue per MWh (−) → less investment (+) → less wind (+).

One minus sign: an odd number, so it is a balancing loop. It does not stop growth; it slows it, and it gets stronger as wind grows.

This is the second loop your report §5 needs, next to the reinforcing learning-curve loop. Write it in the A → B (+) notation.

What weakens the loop

Five ways out, none of them quick

Build lines

Transmission and interconnectors: the real fix, 5–15 years.

Store

Batteries move power by hours; they cut negative-price hours, not north–south distance.

Shift demand

Electric heating, vehicles and industry that use power when it is cheap.

Faster permits

RED III deadlines, the Grid Action Plan, anticipatory investment: building before the farm asks.

Price by location

Prices that are lower where power is in surplus, so new demand and plants move there. Debated, and rejected in Great Britain in 2025.

For your four worlds

The grid is a scenario variable

In a strong-policy world the grid is built faster and the balancing loop is weak. In a weak-policy world farms are built where the grid cannot take them, and the loop bites.

Grid build-out can even be one of your two axes, if it is not just an outcome of your policy axis. Week 4's test applies.
