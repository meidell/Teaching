Why it matters · 2 / 15

Why strategic foresight? The case for thinking about the future

We live in a world of compounding, intersecting change — where a pandemic, a technology breakthrough, a geopolitical rupture, or a climate shock can simultaneously restructure entire industries, invalidate decades of strategy, and create vast new opportunity spaces. Organisations that are caught by surprise pay an enormous price. Those that anticipated the possibilities were already prepared.

Shell Oil — 1973

Shell was the only major oil company that had mentally rehearsed a supply shock scenario before the 1973 Arab Oil Embargo. While competitors scrambled, Shell had already prepared alternative strategies. It rose from 7th to 2nd largest oil company by revenue within three years of the crisis. The only difference: Shell had done scenario planning; its competitors had used linear forecasting.

Kodak — 2012

Kodak invented the digital camera in 1975 — yet filed for bankruptcy in 2012. Its own engineers had created the disruptive technology; its own foresight team had warned of digital's trajectory; its own scenario plans had modelled the transition. But the company failed to act on the intelligence it possessed. Foresight without strategic response is merely a well-documented failure.

Finland — 1990s to today

Finland institutionalised futures thinking at the highest level of government in 1993 — establishing the Committee for the Future in parliament. This enabled Finland to navigate the collapse of Soviet trade (which destroyed 20% of its economy overnight), invest counter-cyclically in Nokia and ICT, and build one of the world's most resilient knowledge economies. Strategic foresight at national scale created real competitive advantage.

COVID-19 — 2020

Multiple governments had pandemic scenario plans — some very detailed. The UK's Exercise Cygnus (2016) modelled a severe flu pandemic; the US had the Crimson Contagion exercise (2019); the Gates Foundation ran Event 201 simulating a coronavirus pandemic in October 2019. The tragedy was not a failure of foresight — it was a failure to act on the foresight that already existed.
The lesson across all cases: foresight is not about predicting the future — it is about preparing for multiple futures, so that whatever arrives, your organisation has already thought about it.
Two successes, two failures — and the failures are the more useful pair: Kodak and COVID both had the foresight and did not act on it. Ask the room which of the four is closest to Ørsted and which to Eni.
Definition · 3 / 15

What is strategic foresight? A discipline, a practice, and an organisational capability

Strategic foresight is the structured practice of thinking systematically about the range of plausible futures, understanding the forces that are shaping them, and using that understanding to make better decisions in the present. It is not prediction — it is the disciplined expansion of an organisation's awareness of what is possible, what is probable, and what is preferable.

1Systemic

Foresight analyses the whole system — not just the immediate competitive environment. It looks across social, technological, economic, environmental, political, legal, ethical, and demographic dimensions simultaneously, mapping how forces in different domains interact and amplify each other.

2Long-range

Foresight operates on planning horizons of 5–30 years — well beyond normal strategic planning cycles. This is precisely where it is most valuable: the most transformative changes are invisible at 3-year horizons but clearly visible at 10–20 years, giving strategists time to prepare.

3Participatory

Foresight is strongest when it involves diverse voices — across functions, hierarchies, sectors, and geographies. Diverse perspectives surface signals that any homogeneous group would miss. The process of building shared foresight is itself a source of organisational value.

4Action-oriented

Foresight is not an academic exercise. Its purpose is to improve real decisions — investment choices, product development priorities, policy designs, regulatory preparations, and strategic plans. A foresight programme that does not change decisions is a failure, regardless of its intellectual quality.

5Iterative

The future does not stay still. A foresight system that produces a single annual report and then goes quiet is not strategic foresight — it is a photograph. Effective foresight is a continuously updating radar that feeds intelligence into decisions across the full strategic planning cycle.

6Normative

Foresight is not only about predicting or understanding the future — it is about shaping it. The most powerful foresight processes help organisations define the future they want to build, not just anticipate the future they will receive. Vision and agency are integral to the discipline.
Read the definition aloud, then the three words at its end: possible, probable, preferable. Slide 5 maps the nine tools onto exactly those three.
A critical distinction · 4 / 15

Foresight vs forecasting

The most common misunderstanding about strategic foresight is that it is sophisticated forecasting. It is not. Forecasting and foresight are fundamentally different in purpose, method, and output. Understanding this distinction is essential before engaging with any of the tools in this course.

DimensionForecastingStrategic foresight
Core question"What WILL happen?""What MIGHT happen — and are we ready?"
Starting pointCurrent trends and data, projected forwardA defined range of plausible futures, derived from deep environmental scanning
Direction of logicPresent → Future (extrapolation)Future → Present (backcasting) and Present → Futures (scenario construction)
Relationship to uncertaintySeeks to reduce uncertainty by identifying the most likely single outcomeEmbraces uncertainty by mapping the full range of plausible futures and their strategic implications
OutputA point estimate or probability distribution for a single futureMultiple coherent scenario worlds, each with distinct strategic implications
Role of agencyPassive: the future is something that happens to you — predict it and adaptActive: the future is something you can shape — vision, plan, and act to create it
Planning horizon1–5 years (extrapolation is reliable only in stable systems over short periods)5–30 years (transformative change only becomes visible at longer horizons)
Best suited forOperational planning, financial modelling, supply-demand forecasting in stable systemsLong-range strategic planning, policy design, institutional reform, innovation strategy
Key insight: forecasting and foresight are complementary, not competing. Use forecasting for operational decisions in stable, short-range domains. Use foresight for strategic decisions in uncertain, long-range domains.
The capstone uses both: the Wright's Law fit in Week 3 is a forecast; the 2×2 in Week 4 is foresight. The mistake is using the first where only the second will do — projecting a learning curve to 2040 as if nothing could bend it.
The centrepiece · 5 / 15

What is scenario planning? The most widely used long-range planning method in the world

Scenario planning is a structured method for constructing multiple plausible descriptions of different futures — called scenarios — and using them to test strategies, challenge assumptions, identify risks and opportunities, and build genuine preparedness for a range of outcomes. It does not predict which future will occur; it ensures your organisation has thought deeply about each of the futures that might.

What futures are possible?

Environmental scanning (Tools 1–3) identifies the forces shaping the future across all domains — mapping what could plausibly emerge.

What futures are probable?

Expert validation (Tool 4) and cross-impact analysis (Tool 5) assess which futures are more or less likely — and how different forces combine.

What futures are preferable?

Visioning (Tool 7a) and backcasting (Tool 7b) ask which future the organisation wants to build — and how to get there from here.
Why scenario planning works — the three strategic functions

→ Stress-testing

Scenarios reveal whether your strategy succeeds only under a narrow set of conditions — or is genuinely robust across a range of futures. Strategies that depend on a single future arriving are brittle; those that work across multiple futures are resilient.

→ Assumption surfacing

The process of building scenarios makes visible the assumptions embedded in current strategy that are normally invisible — the hidden bets that organisational planning has made about which future will arrive.

→ Mental model expansion

Leaders who have genuinely lived inside multiple scenarios before they arrive respond faster, more coherently, and with better judgment when real disruptions occur — because the cognitive pathways were already activated.
Possible → probable → preferable is the order of the whole course. Stress-testing is the function the capstone's Phase 3 is marked on: is the recommendation robust across the four worlds?

STEEPLED scanning

What it is: a structured framework for comprehensively scanning the external environment across eight dimensions. Rather than focusing on one sector or domain, STEEPLED forces analysts to look across Social, Technological, Economic, Environmental, Political, Legal, Ethical, and Demographic dimensions simultaneously — ensuring that no major force of change is overlooked.

Role in the system: STEEPLED is the first and broadest scan — it generates the raw inventory of signals and forces that all subsequent tools will analyse more deeply. What it produces: a comprehensive map of the external environment, segmented by domain — the raw material for all subsequent foresight analysis.
For European wind: one force per letter is a good first exercise — auction design (P), grid-connection law (L), blade recycling (E-environmental), the just transition (E-ethical), and so on.
Tools 2 & 3 · 8 / 15

From raw signals to deep structure

Once STEEPLED has mapped the landscape, the next two tools go deeper: Tool 2 separates the truly novel signals from the noise, and Tool 3 reveals the hidden structural logic driving what you see.

Tool 2 — Weak Signal & Trend Analysis

What it is: a systematic method for identifying early, faint signals of emerging change that conventional analysis dismisses as noise — but which foresight practitioners recognise as the leading edge of significant future trends.

The key insight: most major disruptions were weakly visible years before they became dominant. The 2008 financial crisis, the rise of smartphones, the COVID-19 pandemic — all showed early signals that trained scanners could detect.

What it produces:

  • A curated Trend Radar organised by time horizon (H1: 0–3 yr, H2: 3–8 yr, H3: 8–20 yr)
  • Impact and confidence ratings for each signal
  • A watching brief on the signals most likely to shape your strategic environment
Role in the system: takes the STEEPLED inventory and applies a quality filter — separating signals worth tracking from background noise, and tracking them on an ongoing radar.

Tool 3 — Systems Thinking & CLA

What it is: two complementary methods for understanding the deep structure of the system you are scanning. Systems thinking maps the feedback loops, stocks, and flows that cause the behaviour you observe. Causal Layered Analysis (CLA) digs beneath events and trends to the deep structures and worldviews that sustain them.

The key insight: surface-level events are symptoms. Effective foresight requires understanding the deeper system dynamics and belief systems that produce those events — because intervening at depth is the only way to produce lasting change.

What it produces:

  • Causal Loop Diagrams showing reinforcing and balancing feedback dynamics
  • MICMAC driver-dependency maps identifying which variables drive the system
  • A 4-layer CLA analysis (Litany → Systemic → Worldview → Myth)
Role in the system: transforms a list of trends into a structural map — revealing root causes, leverage points, and system archetypes that shape the range of possible futures.
Tool 3 is the "From stakeholders to systems" session: the reinforcing and balancing loops, signed arrows, leverage points. That deck is the worked version of this card.
Tools 4 & 5 · 9 / 15

Validation, probability & interaction

These tools add rigour: expert validation and systematic mapping of how trends combine and collide.

Tool 4 — The Delphi Method

What it is: a structured, iterative process for reaching collective expert judgment while avoiding the biases of open group discussion. Anonymous expert panels respond through multiple rounds, seeing aggregated results each time, until consensus emerges — or until genuine disagreement is mapped with precision.

The key problem it solves: individual experts are biased; open panels are dominated by the loudest voice. The Delphi Method extracts genuine collective intelligence by separating independent judgment from social pressure.

What it produces:

  • Expert-validated probability estimates for key trends and events
  • Areas of genuine expert consensus — planning assumptions
  • Areas of persistent expert disagreement — uncertainty flags for scenario axes
  • Confidence-weighted Trend Radar entries
Role in the system: validates the scanning outputs from Tools 1–3 against expert knowledge — turning analyst hypotheses into defensible, evidence-grounded strategic intelligence.

Tool 5 — Cross-Impact Analysis

What it is: a systematic method for mapping how the occurrence of one trend or event changes the probability of all others. It produces a matrix of conditional probability adjustments — revealing the cascading, amplifying, and contradictory interactions between the forces shaping the future.

The key problem it solves: trends do not travel alone. Analysing each independently produces a misleading picture. Cross-Impact Analysis reveals which combinations of trends are mutually reinforcing (and thus form coherent scenario worlds) and which are mutually contradictory (and thus define scenario axes).

What it produces:

  • A cross-impact matrix of all key trend interactions
  • Driver-dependency classification (MICMAC: Drivers, Relay, Dependent, Autonomous)
  • Consistent event clusters — the raw material for scenario construction
  • Contradictory pairs — the scenario axis candidates for Tool 6
Role in the system: the methodological bridge between analysis (Tools 1–4) and scenario construction (Tool 6). Ensures scenarios are structurally coherent.
Persistent expert disagreement is not a failure of Delphi — it is its most useful output, because that is where a scenario axis belongs.
Tool 6 · the pivot · 10 / 15

Scenario logic: the 2×2 matrix

Scenario planning is the centrepiece of the entire foresight process — the tool that converts environmental intelligence into strategic insight. It constructs four coherent, mutually exclusive alternative futures, then stress-tests strategy against each. Everything in Tools 1–5 feeds into it; everything in Tools 7–9 flows from it.

← Axis 1 (most important + uncertain driver) →

What it does

Takes the contradictory pairs from Cross-Impact Analysis (Tool 5) and builds them into axes of a 2×2 matrix. Each quadrant becomes a distinct scenario world — four named futures that challenge your strategy from different directions.

Three strategy outputs

1. No-regret moves — act now, valuable in ALL scenarios
2. Contingent moves — prepare; deploy when trigger signals appear
3. Hedge moves — protect against the worst outcome in any scenario

Role in the system

The pivot point of the entire foresight process. Tools 1–5 feed into it. Tools 7–9 flow from it. The four scenarios become the reference world for all subsequent visioning, backcasting, futures wheel exploration, and horizon scanning.
For the capstone: axis 1 strength of climate policy, axis 2 speed of renewable cost decline (the course's worked example). No-regret, contingent and hedge are the three words a Phase 3 recommendation should use.
Tools 7a & 7b · the normative pair · 11 / 15

Visioning & backcasting: from insight to intention to roadmap

The normative pair — where foresight becomes strategy and vision becomes an actionable plan.

Tool 7a — Visioning

What it is: the first normative step in the foresight process — the transition from "what futures are possible?" to "what future do we want to create?" Visioning is a structured process for developing a shared, specific, inspiring description of the organisation's preferred future.

The key insight: without a vision, backcasting is impossible — you cannot work backwards from a destination you have not chosen. And without backcasting, planning remains reactive rather than intentional.

A good vision is:

  • Specific enough to guide decisions
  • Ambitious enough to require genuine change
  • Inspiring enough to mobilise genuine commitment
  • Shared — built with stakeholders, not delivered to them

Methods: Day in the Life (2040), Appreciative Inquiry, Letter from the Future, Newspaper Front Page, Values Excavation.

Role in the system: converts strategic intelligence into strategic intention — the organisation's explicit commitment to which future it will work to bring about.

Tool 7b — Backcasting

What it is: a planning method that works backwards from the preferred future — identifying the milestones, decision points, and interventions that must occur on the pathway from today to the vision. The reverse of forecasting: instead of extrapolating from today, it asks "what must be true at each point in time for the vision to be achievable?"

The key insight: many of the actions required to achieve a long-horizon vision must begin immediately — even though they are invisible to short-horizon planning. Backcasting makes the critical path visible.

What it produces:

  • A Milestone Map: time-sequenced necessary states across all strategic dimensions
  • Critical Path analysis: which milestones cannot be delayed without jeopardising the vision
  • Key Decision Calendar: when specific choices must be made
  • Intervention strategies: the packages of action required at each stage
Role in the system: converts vision into an operational roadmap — the living strategic document that guides investment, governance, and accountability toward the preferred future.
Backcasting from the EU's 2050 climate-neutrality target is the obvious exercise: what must be true of grid, ports and auctions in 2030 and 2040 for 2050 to be possible?
Tools 8 & 9 · 12 / 15

Consequence mapping & continuous intelligence

The final two tools bring different but complementary capabilities: Tool 8 maps the cascading consequences of any single change; Tool 9 keeps the entire foresight system permanently alive and updated.

Tool 8 — The Futures Wheel

What it is: a visual brainstorming tool that takes a single trend, event, or decision and systematically maps its first-, second-, and third-order consequences — revealing the hidden systemic implications that linear thinking misses.

Developed by futurist Jerome Glenn in 1971. Deceptively simple — anyone can do it with paper and a marker. But the structured 3-ring process produces insights genuinely invisible to first-order thinking.

The three rings:

  • 1st order: direct, predictable consequences
  • 2nd order: consequences of the consequences — less obvious
  • 3rd order: consequences of consequences of consequences — often surprising and cross-sectoral
Role in the system: sits naturally between Tools 2 and 3 (turning signals into structured consequence maps) and feeds into Tools 5 and 6 (providing variable candidates for cross-impact analysis and scenario narratives). Also used in visioning (Positive Futures Wheel) and decision analysis (Decision Wheel).

Tool 9 — Continuous Horizon Scanning

What it is: the ongoing monitoring infrastructure that keeps all other foresight tools alive and current. A systematic, institutionalised process for regularly collecting, evaluating, and sharing signals of emerging change — operating on defined rhythms and feeding intelligence continuously into the organisation's strategic planning.

Not media monitoring. Not competitive intelligence. Not an annual exercise. Continuous scanning is a permanently operating radar with defined sources, structured processing, and a direct connection to decisions.

The five cadences:

  • Daily: individual scanners monitor assigned sources
  • Weekly: signal digest to leadership (5–10 bullets)
  • Monthly: team filters; updates Trend Radar
  • Quarterly: strategic synthesis; roadmap review
  • Annually: full foresight review; scenario validity check
Role in the system: the feedback loop that converts the entire 9-tool sequence from a one-time exercise into a permanently operating strategic intelligence system.
A futures wheel on "zero bids in an offshore auction" is a ten-minute exercise that reaches the supply chain, the 2030 target and consumer bills by the third ring.

Common misconceptions · 14 / 15

What strategic foresight is not

Strategic foresight is one of the most frequently misunderstood disciplines in management. Before beginning the tool modules, it helps to name and dispel the most common misconceptions — because these misunderstandings lead organisations to apply foresight incorrectly, evaluate it unfairly, and abandon it prematurely.

It's about predicting the future

Foresight explicitly rejects the idea of predicting a single future. Its power lies precisely in modelling multiple plausible futures — so the organisation is prepared for a range of outcomes rather than betting everything on one prediction. If a foresight practitioner claims to know what will happen, they are not doing foresight.

It only matters in volatile industries

The organisations that most need foresight are often those in "stable" industries — because stability creates the dangerous illusion that the future will resemble the past. Kodak, Blockbuster, Nokia, and many others failed not because they lacked intelligence but because they felt stable enough to ignore it. No sector is exempt from disruption.

It's a one-time exercise

The most common foresight failure is treating it as a project with a deliverable — a scenario report that is produced, circulated, and then filed. Genuine foresight is a continuous organisational capability: a permanently operating scanning radar, regularly updated scenarios, and a living roadmap. One-time exercises produce insight; continuous practice produces resilience.

It's the strategy team's job

The richest foresight intelligence comes from the edges of the organisation — from customer service staff who hear unusual requests, from procurement teams who see supply chain anomalies, from frontline workers who notice behavioural changes. Strategy teams who do foresight alone produce narrow, insider views. Effective foresight is a distributed, organisation-wide capability.

It requires expensive technology or big budgets

The most powerful foresight tools — STEEPLED scanning, the Futures Wheel, scenario planning, backcasting — require nothing more than informed people, a structured process, and time to think. World-changing foresight has been done with flip charts and sticky notes. Technology amplifies capability but is not its source.

Success means being right about the future

No individual scenario is expected to "come true." Success means your organisation was prepared for the range of futures that might arise — and responded faster, with better judgment, and with less disruption when real change arrived. Shell's scenarios did not predict the 1973 crisis exactly; they ensured Shell was the only company that had mentally rehearsed it.
The last myth is the one to end on, because it is how the capstone report is marked: not on whether a scenario comes true, but on whether the recommendation survives all four.
