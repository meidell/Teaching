What Phase 1 is

Phase 1 describes the situation: where European wind energy is today, what drives it, what slows it down, who is involved, and how the future could look. You do not recommend anything yet. The recommendation comes in Phase 3.

  • Where you write: in your team report, sections 1 to 7. Each of you types in your own section; the page saves as you type.
  • How long: 1,500–2,000 words for the seven sections together, so about 250 words per section. The report page counts the words for you.
  • How to hand in: on the report page, press Hand in Phase 1. One person does it for the whole team. You can attach a PDF with figures (a drawing of your loops, your 2×2) on the hand-in page; that is optional.
  • The deadline: Sunday 8 November, 23:59 (Geneva time).

Five rules for every number

Numbers that a reader can trust

  1. Every number has a year and a source. "246 GW (WindEurope, 2025)", not "about 250 GW".
  2. EU-27 or Europe? Say which. Europe includes the UK, Norway and Turkey. In your own Forum posts, Europe is 304 GW of wind and the EU-27 is 246 GW. Use one, and say which.
  3. Electricity or energy? Say which. Electricity is one part of all the energy Europe uses. Wind's share of electricity is much bigger than its share of energy.
  4. GW is capacity, TWh is production. GW says how big the wind farms are; TWh says how much electricity they actually produce in a year.
  5. The same number everywhere. If section 1 says 246 GW, section 4 cannot say 250 GW. The data lead keeps one list of the team's numbers.
Section 1 · about 250 words

Where wind energy stands today

How much wind power does the EU have today, and how much of its electricity does it produce?

Owner: data leadFrom Week 2

Must include

  • Installed wind capacity in GW, onshore and offshore, with year and source.
  • Wind's share of EU electricity in %, with year and source.
  • How fast it grows: GW added in the last year, compared with what is needed.
You already haveYour Forum 1 posts (Week 2): five of you wrote about 300 words each with exactly these numbers. Your Week 2 canvas (open it).
Start like this"At the end of 2025 the EU-27 had … GW of wind capacity, of which … GW offshore (WindEurope, 2026). Wind produced …% of EU electricity in 2025 (Ember, 2026)."
Common mistakeMixing Europe and EU-27 figures in the same paragraph.
Section 2 · about 200 words

Electricity demand and sources in 2040

How much electricity will the EU need in 2040, and how much of it could wind supply?

Owner: data leadFrom Week 2

Must include

  • One forecast of EU electricity demand in 2040, with the organisation and the scenario name it comes from (for example the IEA, the European Commission or WindEurope).
  • Why demand grows: name two or three causes (electric cars, heat pumps, industry, data centres).
  • Your team's estimate of wind's share in 2040, and the one reason behind it.
You already haveEach of you wrote a 2040 estimate in Week 2 (your answers range from 25% to 40%). Agree on one team figure, or a range, and explain it.
Start like this"In its … scenario, the … expects EU electricity demand to reach … TWh in 2040, up from about … TWh today, mainly because … ."
Common mistakeGiving a share (40%) without the total it is a share of.
Section 3 · about 250 words

The structural forces

What keeps wind energy growing, even without new government help?

Owner: model leadFrom Week 3

Must include

  • The learning curve: your team's learning rate from the Week 3 fit (about 20%), and what it means in one sentence: each time the world's offshore wind capacity doubles, the cost per MWh falls by about 20%.
  • The data behind it: the years you used, the source (IRENA) and the R².
  • One or two other forces that do not depend on subsidies (for example rising electricity demand, companies buying green power directly).
You already haveYour Week 3 canvas: the data rows, the fitted rate and the sensitivity test (open it). The Week 3 slides explain the learning curve.
Start like this"The main force is the learning curve. Using IRENA data for 2010–2024, we find that offshore wind costs fall by about …% each time global capacity doubles (R² = …)."
Common mistakeListing subsidies or targets. Those depend on governments, so they are not structural forces; they belong in section 6.

The bottlenecks

What slows wind energy down, and which problem is the biggest?

Owner: editorFrom Weeks 2, 4 and 5

Must include

  • Three bottlenecks from this list: permits, the electricity grid, the cost of money (interest rates), the supply chain (turbines, ships, cables).
  • For each one: one fact or number, with a source.
  • Which one you think is the biggest, and why, in two sentences.
You already haveYour Forum posts on permits and grid connections. The real examples several of you found: auctions where developers made no bids at all. The Week 4 slides (the cost of money) and the Week 5 slides (the grid).

Section 5 · about 250 words + one drawing

Stakeholders and the system

Who decides, who pays, and how do their decisions feed back on each other?

Owner: coordinatorFrom Week 2 (Forum 2)

Must include

  • The 6–9 main actors and what goes between them: money, rules, electricity.
  • One reinforcing loop (it makes growth bigger) and one balancing loop (it slows growth down), written with arrows. Examples you can use or change:
    Reinforcing: more wind built → lower cost per MWh → more auctions won → more wind built.
    Balancing: more wind built → more pressure on the grid → longer waits for connection → less wind built.
  • The boundary in one sentence: what your report covers and what it leaves out (for example: "in: EU-27 offshore and onshore wind; out: solar and households").
You already haveYour stakeholder maps from Forum 2 (Week 2): five of you made one. Pick the best one as the team's map, or merge two.
Start like this"Nine actors shape European offshore wind. The most important relationship is between … and … , because … ."
Common mistakeA list of actors with no arrows. Without the loops, it is a phone book, not a system.
Section 6 · about 300 words · the whole team

Two critical uncertainties and the four worlds

Which two things could go either way, and what does Europe look like in 2040 in each case?

Owner: model lead, with everyoneFrom Week 4

The recipe, in five steps

Pick two uncertainties. Things that could really go either way by 2040 and that matter a lot for wind. They must be independent: one can be high while the other is low. Example from Week 4: EU policy ambition (weak ↔ strong) and how fast costs fall (slow ↔ fast). You may use this pair or choose your own.
Draw a 2×2 table. One uncertainty across, one down. That gives four boxes: four worlds.
Name each world. A short, memorable name, not "Scenario A".
Describe each world in 3–4 sentences with one or two numbers, for example wind's share of EU electricity in 2040 and the offshore capacity in GW.
Say which world you think is most likely, and why, in one or two sentences. This is your base case.
You already haveThe worked 2×2 example on the Week 4 page and in the Week 4 scenario slides.
Common mistakeThree scenarios called best, base and worst. That is one uncertainty, not two, and it is not what Phase 1 asks for.
Section 7 · about 200 words + a table

Indicators and datasets

Which numbers will tell us, year after year, which of the four worlds is coming true?

Owner: fifth member or data leadFrom Week 5

Must include

  • A table with three indicators. For each: today's value (year, source), what it would look like in each of your four worlds (higher, lower, or a number), and the dataset you will use in Phase 2.
  • Two or three sentences on why these three: each one should look different in different worlds.
Ideas you can use
  • Offshore wind cost per MWh (IRENA, every year)
  • Wind's share of EU electricity (Ember, every year)
  • Offshore capacity offered in government auctions, and whether developers bid (national energy agencies)
The tableColumns: Indicator · Today (year, source) · World 1 · World 2 · World 3 · World 4 · Dataset.
Common mistakeAn indicator that would look the same in all four worlds. It cannot tell you anything.
