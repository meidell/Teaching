What the exam tests, and what it doesn't

Recognise the shape of the question

What it tests

  • The conceptual frameworks — systems, stakeholders, feedback loops, scenarios, indicators, learning curves, planetary boundaries, uncertainty
  • Your ability to apply them to a new prompt — likely a different energy technology, a different geography, or a different kind of strategic decision
  • Your ability to reason under time pressure with clarity

What it does not test

  • Whether you can recite specific numbers from your team's Phase 1, 2 or 3 work — it is open book anyway
  • Whether you can quote Vinuesa, Rockström, Wack or any other author verbatim
  • Whether you remember the names of specific Ørsted projects or Eni subsidiaries

You do not need to memorise facts. You need to recognise the shape of the strategic question and respond with the right intellectual moves. The exam is directed at the material covered in the readings and on these pages.

Drill · 20 minutes

Wright's Law on a dataset you have not fitted before

Solar PV — the technology that embarrassed every linear forecaster. The rows are rounded from IRENA's utility-scale PV LCOE series and global cumulative capacity. Fit it, read the learning rate, project 2040 at the three rates, and write the three sanity checks in your head: is log-log straight, are residuals random, does 2040 make physical sense?

The fitted rate is far above the ~20–24% usually quoted for solar — because that figure is for module prices since 1976, while LCOE from 2010 to 2023 also captured cheaper financing, balance-of-system and capacity-factor gains. Same technology, different series, different rate: say which one you fitted. Then ask the Week 7 question: which planetary boundary does polysilicon press on, and when?

One practice prompt · 90 minutes

Calibrate the shape of the move

Before the real exam opens, work through this in 90 minutes — open book, as the exam is, but no AI, on paper or in the box below. The point is to calibrate the shape of the move, not to produce a polished answer.

Practice prompt. India aims to add approximately 280 GW of solar PV capacity by 2030 as part of its broader 500 GW non-fossil target. Two companies represent contrasting strategic bets: Adani Green Energy, a vertically integrated Indian renewables developer with strong domestic political connections and significant debt exposure; and Tata Power, a diversified utility with growing renewables exposure, a stronger balance sheet and a more cautious capital-allocation approach.

Apply the strategic-foresight framework to: (1) identify the two critical uncertainties shaping Indian solar deployment to 2030; (2) sketch the resulting 2×2 scenario matrix; (3) place each company in each scenario (thrive / survive / struggle); (4) recommend which company is more robustly positioned, from the perspective of a long-horizon institutional investor; (5) name two early-warning signals a client should monitor over the next 12 months.

Suggested length 1,000–1,200 words. Time limit 90 minutes. No AI assistance, no browser.

How to approach a prompt like this

The move set is the same as in the Capstone, compressed.

  • Five minutes to identify the critical uncertainties. Likely candidates for Indian solar: domestic manufacturing autonomy (the PLI scheme vs Chinese imports), grid integration and storage build-out, financing conditions (Indian interest rates, foreign capital), policy continuity (state vs federal coordination). Pick two that are genuinely uncertain and materially different in their consequences.
  • Five minutes to name the four scenarios. A memorable label and one sentence each.
  • Twenty minutes to place the companies. Adani's leverage and political exposure hurt it in tight-credit or political-change scenarios. Tata's diversification helps in stress but caps upside in boom scenarios. Be specific about why.
  • Twenty minutes to recommend. Be explicit about the perspective. A long-horizon institutional investor will likely prefer the more robust position — but you can argue otherwise if you justify it.
  • Five minutes on signals. Operational, specific, measurable within 12 months.
  • Remaining time on writing. A clear executive summary at the top is worth more than three more pages of analysis.

The exam will look like this in structure. The content will be different.

Exam logistics

Read before Monday

Allowed — it is open book

  • Your notes, the readings and this site, including the glossary
  • Your team's report and your own consulting file
  • Pen and paper for working out
  • A simple calculator — physical or device-native, not online

Not allowed

  • AI assistants of any kind — Claude, ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Copilot, anything
  • Collaboration with classmates — your team is not in the room
  • Translation tools

Open book is not a shortcut: sixty questions in two hours leaves two minutes each, and the questions ask you to apply the toolkit to a case you have not seen. The notes help you check a definition; they cannot do the move for you.

Technical readiness

  • Test your internet connection in advance
  • Charge your device or stay plugged in
  • Close unnecessary applications
  • Have a quiet space booked for the duration

If something goes wrong

  • Take a screenshot of any error message
  • Contact me immediately via the message panel here
  • Do not refresh or resubmit without instruction
Any deviation from these guidelines may result in a grade penalty or failure of the exam or the course. Questions must be answered sequentially from first to last, without skipping or revisiting. Late submissions are graded zero.

Final learning checkpoint

By the end of the exam window you should have

  • Demonstrated systems thinking applied to a new sustainability challenge
  • Built a defensible scenario matrix from genuine uncertainties
  • Applied a quantitative tool — Wright's Law, a time series or similar — to support an argument
  • Made a clear strategic recommendation with explicit conditions
  • Identified the operational signals a client would use to track the recommendation

If you have done that, the course has done its job.

A word before you begin

Ten weeks ago you were handed a question: is wind energy a sound long-term investment for Europe's transition to net-zero by 2050, and which company — Ørsted or Eni — is better positioned? You were not given an answer. You were given a method — stakeholder mapping, energy fundamentals, AI tools, scenario planning, learning curves, planetary boundaries, scenarios that bite, recommendations under uncertainty. You used the method to produce three reports. The reports are yours.

The exam is a one-shot rehearsal for the work many of you will go on to do — as analysts, consultants, investors, policymakers, executives, lawyers, founders. The questions get harder. The data gets messier. The clients want answers, not analyses. You will need to commit to positions you can defend, and revise them when the evidence demands it. You have built the muscle for that here. Trust it.

Thank you for the work. Good luck.
