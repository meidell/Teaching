The Wednesday session, hour by hour

17:15–18:15 is yours. You write on the shared canvas, a page on this site — one block per team per week, which I open on the screen at 18:15. You meet without me, run the hour from the plan below, and end it with something written in every one of your consulting files. 18:15–20:15 is with me: we start from what your hour produced, then the week's material, then the forums. Every Wednesday, from 7 October.

How the group hour runs

Six rules that make an hour without a teacher work

Start at 18:15, not 16:20Every plan is cut to sixty minutes with nothing spare. The timekeeper calls each step.
Two roles, rotatingA scribe who types on the shared page and a timekeeper who ends each step. Different people every week.
One shared pageThe Zoom whiteboard, a Google Doc, anything everyone can type on at once. Paste the link in the chat at 18:15.
The last ten minutes are writingEach of you fills the week's fields in your own consulting file, in your own words. The scribe's notes are not your notes.
Bring one thing to 18:15Every hour ends by agreeing the one question or disagreement the group hands to me. That is where the class starts.
Disagreement is the outputA group that agrees on everything in sixty minutes did not look hard enough. Write down who disagreed and why.

The ten sessions

Week 1 · · 17:15–18:15

Form the team

Eight of you, two teams of four, one report per team. This hour is for organising: who is in which team, what the team is called, who does what, and how you will work for nine weeks — written down where I can read it.

By 18:15 you have…
  • Signed in on this site (name + six-digit code) — everything else depends on it.
  • Joined a team of four on the Week 1 page, and given it a name.
  • Four roles agreed and written in the charter on the report page: coordinator, data lead, model lead, editor.
  • The charter drafted — how you meet, how you decide, your AI policy, who writes what by Week 5.
  • Your own "my role and what I bring" and "what we agreed / where I disagree" fields filled, in your own words.
  • The one question your team wants this capstone to answer, at the top of your group's block on the shared canvas (link). I open it at 18:15; there is nothing to send.
5 minSettle. Everyone opens Week 1 and signs in — name and a six-digit code you will remember. Pick a timekeeper for the hour.
10 minRound the table, one minute each: your name, your programme, what you are good at that a consulting team needs — numbers, writing, design, finding sources, keeping people on time — and the one question you want this capstone to answer.
10 minTwo teams of four. Mix what you just heard: each team needs someone who likes spreadsheets and someone who likes writing. Each of you joins a team on the Week 1 page — the picker closes a team at four — and the team agrees a name.
10 minRoles. Coordinator (runs the hour, keeps the plan), data lead (sources, numbers, the spreadsheet), model lead (the fitter, the projections, the 2×2), editor (one voice, word counts, the AI declaration). Everyone writes; the roles say who is accountable. Write them in the charter on the report page.
15 minThe charter, four short answers on the report page: when you meet besides Wednesdays · how you decide when you disagree · what you will and will not let an AI do, and how you declare it · who writes which section of Phase 1 by Week 5 (the report page lists the sections).
10 minEveryone fills their own two Week 1 fields — my role and what I bring; what we agreed and where I disagree. Then agree the one question to bring to 18:15 and put it at the top of the canvas.

Bring to 18:15: The team name, the roles, and the one question — read out by the coordinator.

Week 2 · · 17:15–18:15

Three numbers, five sources

Divide the five market metrics between you, find each one from a primary source in fifteen minutes, then grade each other's sources and convert the capacity into TWh together.

By 18:15 you have…
  • Your row in the five-metric table on the canvas: value, year, primary-source link, the group's grade.
  • The GW → TWh conversion written out on the canvas, checked by everyone.
  • Your Week 2 fields: the metric you will compare the two companies on, your 2040 guess with its assumption.
  • The group field, and the number two sources disagreed on, at the top of the block.
  • Everything above is on the shared canvas (link) — your group's block for Week 2. I open it at 18:15; there is nothing to send.
5 minScribe and timekeeper. The scribe opens a five-row table: metric · value · year · source link · authoritative / industry estimate / unclear.
15 minDivide and find. Each person takes one metric — EU wind capacity (GW), wind's share of EU electricity (%), offshore LCOE 2015 vs 2024, Ørsted's market cap, Eni's renewables target — and finds it from a primary source (Eurostat, WindEurope, IRENA, the company). Perplexity is allowed; the link you paste must be the source itself.
15 minGrade the sources. Present your row in sixty seconds. The group grades it: authoritative, industry estimate, or unclear. Any number nobody can trace to its producer goes back for another five minutes.
10 minThe conversion, out loud. Take the EU offshore capacity you found and a 50% capacity factor. Someone talks through GW × CF × 8,760 to TWh while everyone checks the arithmetic in the Week 2 converter. Then ask: what share of EU electricity is that?
10 minEach person fills their Week 2 file: the metric they will compare Ørsted and Eni on, and a first guess at wind's 2040 share with the assumption behind it. Note the range of guesses in the group.
5 minBring to 18:15: the number the group found two different values for, and which source you believed.
Fills in your file:w2_metricw2_estimatew2_group

Bring to 18:15: The metric where two sources disagreed, and why the group chose one of them.

Week 3 · · 17:15–18:15

Two teams, one curve

Each team fits Wright's Law to the same eight rows and owns the result: the data lead checks the two rows the fit leans on hardest, the model lead runs the fit and the sensitivity test, four people put the same question to four different assistants, and the coordinator turns what went wrong into one change to the team's AI policy. At 18:15 the two teams' curves are read side by side.

By 18:15 you have…
  • The team's curve in its own box on the canvas: rows used, fitted rate, R², 2040 LCOE at the fitted rate and at 15%, the 2024 cost chosen and why.
  • The 2010 and 2024 rows verified by the data lead, with links.
  • Your row in the sensitivity table, and one sentence on which rows move the fit most.
  • The AI audit: four assistants, one question, one claim checked against Ørsted's annual report, a failure mode for each.
  • The coordinator's proposed change to the AI policy.
  • Your Week 3 fields — including what you did in your role. Everything above is on the shared canvas (link); I open it at 18:15, there is nothing to send.
5 minCoordinator opens the canvas at Week 3 and gives each person one assistant for the audit — Claude, ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini or Le Chat. Model lead shares the Week 3 fitter on screen.
10 minFit together, then check the ends. Everyone reads the fitter's rate on the default rows (19.8%, R² 0.94) — same data, same tool, the numbers must match. Meanwhile the data lead checks the 2010 and 2024 rows against IRENA and GWEC and fills the verified-rows table. The team decides the 2024 cost: the fitter's $79 or the briefing's $86.
10 minSensitivity. With the team's rows in the fitter, each person moves a different year's cost by ±20% and writes the rate before and after in the sensitivity table. Agree one sentence on which rows the fit is most sensitive to. The model lead fills the team's curve box.
15 minThe audit, in parallel. Everyone asks their assigned assistant, in a fresh conversation, word for word: "What impairments did Ørsted announce in 2023, and on which projects? Give amounts and sources." Read the four answers aloud; click every link. The coordinator checks one claim against Ørsted's own 2023 annual report; the editor logs each row in the audit table with a failure mode.
10 minEach person fills their Week 3 file: rate and projections, what the AI got wrong, what they did in their role. The coordinator writes one proposed change to the charter's AI policy; the editor notes who writes the first paragraph of report §3 by Sunday.
10 minRead the other team's box. Both teams' blocks are on the canvas. Is their rate the same as yours? If not, which row or which choice explains it? Agree what to bring to 18:15.
Fills in your file:w3_ratew3_auditw3_rolew3_group

Bring to 18:15: the team's learning rate and the one choice behind it — and the most confident wrong statement any assistant made, with the source that disproved it.

18:15–20:15 · with Jan Erik, in outline

  • Wright's Law derived on the board — numbers first (83 → 166 → 332 → 664 GW), then the letters — and why a straight line on log-log paper is the whole claim. Why the curve is global when the client is European.
  • The audit: four assistants, the failure modes they showed (hallucination, outdated, out-of-distribution, confirmation), and how each was caught. The two proposed AI-policy changes.
  • Vinuesa: AI as enabler and inhibitor, and what that means for a company that uses AI to find oil.
  • What goes into report §3 this week. The learning-curve forum is due Saturday, the audit post Sunday; both stay open after the deadline.

Week 4 · · 17:15–18:15

One matrix for the group, then your own

List the forces shaping EU wind to 2040, vote on impact and uncertainty, pick the group's two axes, name four worlds in ten minutes — then each person chooses their own axes and defends them.

By 18:15 you have…
  • The scored force list on the canvas with the group's medians.
  • The group's two axes and four named worlds, each with one sentence, the orthogonality check noted.
  • Your own axes in your Week 4 file — with the reason if they differ from the group's.
  • The group field, and the rejected axis pair at the top of the block.
  • Everything above is on the shared canvas (link) — your group's block for Week 4. I open it at 18:15; there is nothing to send.
5 minScribe and timekeeper. The scribe opens a table: force · impact 1–5 · uncertainty 1–5.
10 minThe long list. Everyone adds forces until there are at least ten: policy ambition after 2030, interest rates, cost decline, grid build-out, Chinese turbines, public acceptance, hydrogen, data-centre demand, Russia, carbon price…
10 minVote. For each force everyone types two numbers in the chat at once — impact, uncertainty. The scribe takes the median. The top-right corner (high, high) is your shortlist.
10 minPick two and test them. Choose the group's two axes. Then the test: write one sentence for each of the four cells. If the off-diagonal cells need contortions, one axis is an outcome of the other — swap it.
10 minName the worlds. Four names, memorable, not "high/low". One sentence each on what Europe looks like in 2040. Say which one is the most dangerous to ignore.
10 minEach person fills their Week 4 file: their scored forces and their own two axes — which may differ from the group's. Say why if they do.
5 minBring to 18:15: the axis pair the group rejected, and the reason.
Fills in your file:w4_forcesw4_axesw4_group

Bring to 18:15: The axis pair you rejected as correlated, and the pair you kept.

18:15–20:15 · with Jan Erik, in outline

  • Ørsted and Eni: two ways to pay for the transition. Two exercises on the Week 4 calculator.

Week 5 · · 17:15–18:15

Orthogonality court

Each person presents their two axes in sixty seconds and the group tries to collapse the off-diagonal cells; survivors draw one causal loop together and name three indicators per world.

By 18:15 you have…
  • The verdict sheet on the canvas: every person's axes, pass or fail, the fix.
  • The two loops drawn on the canvas with + and − on every arrow.
  • Your four cell sentences and your indicator shortlist in your Week 5 file; your final axes posted in Forum 10 tonight.
  • The group field, and the swapped axis at the top of the block.
  • Everything above is on the shared canvas (link) — your group's block for Week 5. I open it at 18:15; there is nothing to send.
5 minScribe and timekeeper. Today the scribe keeps a verdict sheet: name · axes · pass / fail · the fix.
20 minThe court. One at a time, sixty seconds: your two axes and a one-sentence narrative for each of the four cells. The group then attacks the off-diagonal cells for two minutes: can a strong-policy world really have slow cost decline here? If the cell needs contortions, the verdict is fail and the group proposes which axis to swap for a driver.
15 minOne loop, together. On the shared page, draw the reinforcing loop (cost → adoption → scale → cost) and one balancing loop (deployment → congestion → curtailment → revenue → investment). Mark every arrow + or −. Then ask: which loop is stronger in each of the group's four worlds?
10 minIndicators. For one scenario, name three quantitative anchors and the dataset each would come from. Trackable, discriminating, forecastable — strike any that fail one of the three.
5 minEach person fills their Week 5 file: the four cell sentences as they now stand after the court, and the indicator shortlist.
5 minBring to 18:15: the axis the court made someone swap, and what replaced it. Post your final axes in Forum 10 tonight.
Fills in your file:w5_cell1w5_cell2w5_cell3w5_cell4w5_indicatorsw5_group

Bring to 18:15: The axis the court made someone swap, and the driver that replaced it.

Week 6 · · 17:15–18:15

The indicator market

Each person brings two indicators for sale; the group buys only the ones that are trackable, discriminating and forecastable — then everyone counts their Phase 1 chat turns for the footprint audit.

By 18:15 you have…
  • The indicator table with verdicts on the canvas, and the method chosen for each bought indicator.
  • Your AI footprint estimate (turns, Wh, gCO₂e) in the canvas table.
  • Your Week 6 fields: indicators with sources, what could go wrong.
  • The group field, and the longest-argued indicator at the top of the block.
  • Everything above is on the shared canvas (link) — your group's block for Week 6. I open it at 18:15; there is nothing to send.
5 minScribe and timekeeper. The scribe opens a table: indicator · source · how often published · which scenarios it separates · verdict.
20 minThe market. Everyone offers two indicators from their Phase 1. For each, the group asks the three questions in order — is it published regularly, by whom; does it move differently across the four worlds; can it be projected to 2040 — and buys or rejects. Expect to reject half.
10 minThe method. For each indicator the group bought, name the method: curve fit, time series, Monte Carlo, clustering. Say in one sentence why that one and not another.
15 minThe audit, together. Everyone opens their Phase 1 chat history and counts substantial turns. Agree a per-query figure from Luccioni and a grid factor; each person computes their Wh and gCO₂e. Compare: whose Phase 1 cost most, and why?
5 minEach person fills their Week 6 file: the 2–3 indicators with sources, and what could go wrong.
5 minBring to 18:15: the indicator the group argued longest about.
Fills in your file:w6_indicatorsw6_risksw6_group

Week 7 · · 17:15–18:15

Where it breaks

Each person takes one planetary boundary, finds one quantitative piece of evidence in fifteen minutes, and the group ranks which two would bend the aggressive 2040 projection first.

By 18:15 you have…
  • The boundary table on the canvas: one number and one source per boundary.
  • The group's two binding boundaries and the rough GW at which each binds.
  • Your Week 7 fields: fit and projections, the two boundaries with evidence.
  • The group field, and the most disputed boundary at the top of the block.
  • Everything above is on the shared canvas (link) — your group's block for Week 7. I open it at 18:15; there is nothing to send.
5 minScribe and timekeeper. Assign one boundary each: climate, biosphere integrity, biogeochemical flows, freshwater, novel entities (rare earths), land. The scribe opens a table: boundary · the constraint on wind · one number · source · binds at roughly what GW.
15 minFind one number. Neodymium demand vs supply, blade waste tonnes, water intensity of steel, bird and bat mortality studies, seabed impact — one piece of quantitative evidence per boundary, with its source.
15 minPresent and rank. Sixty seconds each. Then the group votes: which two boundaries bind European wind scaling first, and at roughly what level of deployment? Be numerical, even if rough.
10 minRe-run the aggressive scenario. Everyone opens their Week 7 fit. Does the 20% projection survive the two binding boundaries? Say what would have to be true for it to.
10 minEach person fills their Week 7 file: fitted rate and projections, and the two boundaries with evidence.
5 minBring to 18:15: the boundary the group disagreed about most.
Fills in your file:w7_fitw7_boundariesw7_group

Bring to 18:15: The boundary you ranked first, the number behind it, and who disagreed.

Week 8 · · 17:15–18:15

The cross-check table

Build one table of IEA, BloombergNEF and DNV 2040 numbers for wind share, capacity and LCOE, dividing the sources between you; then each person places their own projection in it and explains the gap.

By 18:15 you have…
  • The cross-check table on the canvas: IEA, BloombergNEF, DNV, and every one of you, with edition years.
  • Your Week 8 fields: the central claim, your cross-check position with its reason.
  • The group field, and the number nobody could find at the top of the block.
  • Everything above is on the shared canvas (link) — your group's block for Week 8. I open it at 18:15; there is nothing to send.
5 minScribe and timekeeper. The scribe opens a table with three rows (wind share %, offshore GW, LCOE $/MWh) and columns for IEA STEPS · APS · NZE · BNEF · DNV · each of you.
20 minDivide the outlooks. One person per source finds the 2040 (or nearest) numbers for Europe from the public summary or annex tables. Note the edition year and the scenario name next to every number.
15 minPlace yourselves. Each person adds their central projection and bounds. Where are you outside all three outlooks? Each outlier gives one of the two acceptable reasons: different boundary conditions, or a known blind spot of the forecaster.
10 minTwo lenses. Whoever has both a learning-curve and a time-series number for the same indicator says where they agree and where they diverge, and what the divergence means.
5 minEach person fills their Week 8 file: the central claim in one sentence and their cross-check position with the reason.
5 minBring to 18:15: the source the group found hardest to read, and the number nobody could find.
Fills in your file:w8_claimw8_crosscheckw8_group

Bring to 18:15: Where your projection sits against the three outlooks, and the reason you give for the gap.

Peer challenge, live

By 18:15 you have…

  • Your Week 9 fields: the recommendation in one sentence, the counter-argument and your answer.
  • The group field, and the recommendation that changed at the top of the block.
  • Everything above is on the shared canvas (link) — your group's block for Week 9. I open it at 18:15; there is nothing to send.
5 minScribe and timekeeper. Order the drafts; each person will be challenged by their two assigned classmates.
40 minTen minutes per draft. The author states the recommendation in one sentence and whose perspective it serves (two minutes). Challenger one: the weakest link in the argument (two minutes). Challenger two: the counter-evidence — what data or scenario, if true, undermines it (two minutes). Everyone: the question a hostile client would ask in the room (two minutes). The author answers, without defending the person (two minutes). The scribe writes down the single hardest question per draft.
10 minEach person fills their Week 9 file: the recommendation in one sentence, and the most uncomfortable counter-argument with their answer to it.
5 minBring to 18:15: the recommendation that changed during the hour, and what changed it.
