A working taxonomy — what "AI" means this week

When people say "use AI" they usually mean one of four things. Know which one you are reaching for:

MethodWhat it doesYour use case
Large language models — Claude, ChatGPTSynthesise, summarise, reason in textLiterature review, drafting, sensitivity analysis via structured prompting

Key insight: for your capstone you do not need to build these models from scratch. You need to know what they do, when to trust them, and how to use an AI assistant to apply them to your data.

Core concepts · 2

The Vinuesa framework — AI as enabler or inhibitor

Vinuesa and colleagues assessed AI's potential impact on all 169 SDG targets. Their key finding: AI can enable ~79% of targets but can also inhibit ~35%, and the evidence is often weak.

AI as enabler for wind energy (SDG 7)
Forecasting wind generation (turbine- and grid-level) · optimising turbine placement and maintenance schedules · predicting equipment failures · designing grid-integration strategies.
AI as inhibitor — the uncomfortable truth
Training and running large models consumes electricity, sometimes from fossil sources · data centres compete with other loads for renewable capacity · AI entrenches incumbents with compute advantages · AI produces confident-sounding but wrong analyses that mislead decision-makers.

Strategic implication for Ørsted vs Eni: both companies use AI — for site selection, maintenance, trading and forecasting. But Ørsted's AI is focused on making renewables cheaper; Eni's AI also optimises the search for and extraction of oil and gas. When the team analyses their AI strategies in later weeks, this distinction matters.

We go deeper on the inhibitor side in Week 6 (Luccioni et al. on compute costs) and Week 7 (Li et al. on the water footprint).

Core concepts · 3

Pattern recognition — Wright's Law and the learning curve

You met Wright's Law in Week 2: every doubling of cumulative production reduces unit cost by ~11–15%. It is the single most important pattern in the renewable energy transition — and the reinforcing loop of the toolkit (lower cost → more deployment → more scale → lower cost) written as one equation.

Numbers first

Start where the fitter's data ends: offshore wind at about $79/MWh with 83 GW installed worldwide. Pick a learning rate and climb the ladder — each rung doubles the capacity built so far and takes the same share off the cost.

The doubling ladder

Then the letters

The ladder only lands on whole doublings. The formula is the same ladder for any capacity in between:

Cost(Q) = Cost(Q₀) × (Q / Q₀)^(−b)

Q  = cumulative installed capacity (GW)
b  = learning parameter (the log-log slope)
learning rate = 1 − 2^(−b)         e.g. b = 0.234 → 15%

Why this matters for the client. If the learning rate stays at ~15%, offshore wind LCOE could fall from ~$86/MWh (2024) to ~$45–55/MWh by 2040 — cheaper than any fossil alternative. Ørsted's bet wins big. If the learning rate slows to ~5% (supply-chain limits, rare-earth costs, grid bottlenecks), LCOE stalls. Eni's hedged strategy looks smarter.

What the fitter is, and what it is notIt does exactly what you will ask your AI assistant to do in Forum 5 — an ordinary least-squares fit of ln(cost) on ln(capacity) — so you can check the assistant's learning rate against it. On the default rows it gives 19.8% (R² 0.94), at the top of the published range; delete the 2010 row and it rises to 21.1%. It is not a source: the default rows are rounded from IRENA and GWEC and the data lead must replace or verify them before they appear in a post or the report.
Two decisions for the data leadWhich 2024 cost? The briefing says ~$86/MWh; the fitter's last row, rounded from IRENA, says $79. From $86 at 83 GW, the 15% case reaches about $54 in 2040; from $79, about $50. Same year, two sources — Week 2's lesson again. Pick one, say why, and use it everywhere.
Why global capacity? Week 2's tiles counted European gigawatts. The learning curve counts the world's, because turbine makers, cable layers and installation vessels learn on every project wherever it is built — a Chinese farm lowers the cost of a Danish one. The client is European; the learning is not.
Core concepts · 4

Time-series forecasting — projecting to 2040

Given historical EU wind capacity (2000–2024), how do you project to 2040? Three approaches, increasing in sophistication:

  1. Linear extrapolation. A straight line through recent growth. Simple. Often wrong — it assumes no saturation.
  2. Exponential / S-curve fit. Technologies typically follow an S-curve: slow start → rapid growth → saturation. Better for mature markets.
  3. Scenario-based projection. Don't project one line — project three: conservative, central, aggressive. This is what real consulting firms (IEA, BloombergNEF, DNV) do.

The honest answer about AI forecasting: LLMs like Claude can help you set up these projections, compute them and explain them. But they cannot tell you which scenario is right. That is a judgment call — and that is what you are being paid for as a consultant.

Core concepts · 5

Critical evaluation — how AI gets it wrong

You must be able to audit AI outputs. The common failure modes:

FailureWhat it looks likeHow to catch it
Hallucinated dataConfident, specific numbers that don't existCross-check with primary sources (IEA, Eurostat)
Outdated knowledgeNumbers from 2022 presented as currentAlways ask the model for the year of its data
Plausible maths errorsUnit conversions (GW vs GWh), wrong capacity factorsDo the calculation yourself on the back of a napkin
Out-of-distributionNiche question → confident wrong answerBe suspicious of perfect confidence on novel questions
Confirmation biasYou frame the question → the AI agrees → you feel validatedAsk the model to argue the opposite side

The consulting standard: you must be able to defend every number. "The AI said so" is never an acceptable answer to a client.

The team audit — four people, four assistants, one question

One person auditing one assistant finds one error. Four people asking four assistants the same question in fresh conversations find where they disagree — and a disagreement is a place where at least one of them is wrong. The group hour runs it this way:

The coordinator assigns one assistant each — Claude, ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Gemini or Le Chat. Fresh conversation, no history.
Everyone asks, word for word: "What impairments did Ørsted announce in 2023, and on which projects? Give amounts and sources."
Read the four answers aloud. Which amounts differ? Which projects appear in one answer and not another? Click every link: which ones exist?
The coordinator checks one claim against Ørsted's own 2023 annual report. The editor logs: assistant · the claim · true / wrong / unverifiable · the failure mode from the table above.

Your own audit for Forum 6 is a different question of your choosing — the group hour is the rehearsal.

The recursive question — AI's own footprint

You are using AI to analyse the energy transition. AI itself consumes energy. This is not a small side-note: training a GPT-4-class model reportedly used energy comparable to hundreds of US households for a year, and inference at scale consumes more. Questions worth sitting with this week: if you use AI to write a 5,000-word report on wind energy, what is the carbon footprint of that analysis? Should sustainability consultants measure and report their own AI footprint? Does the analysis justify the emissions? Weeks 6 and 7 answer with numbers — from the editor's AI log, which is one reason to keep it from this week on.

Individual posts: the team has one curve, each of you has your own reading of it. Reply to two classmates in each forum that asks for replies — at least one from the other team, so the two curves meet before Wednesday's class.

Use AI to fit Wright's Law to offshore wind LCOE data, then project to 2040.

Collect 5–8 historical LCOE data points for offshore wind (2010–2024). Sources: IRENA, IEA, Lazard. Record cumulative global installed capacity (GW) for each year. Your team's verified rows are a fair start; say if you used them.
Give the data to your AI of choice with a prompt like: "I have historical data on offshore wind LCOE and cumulative installed capacity. Help me fit a Wright's Law learning curve of the form Cost(Q) = Cost(Q₀) × (Q/Q₀)^(−b). Estimate the learning rate. Then project LCOE for 2030, 2035 and 2040 under three scenarios: conservative (10% learning rate), central (15%), aggressive (20%)."

Post 300–400 words: your learning-rate estimate and how it compares to the published 12–18%; your three 2040 LCOE projections; which scenario you find most plausible and why — your first piece of professional judgment; and one thing the AI got wrong or had to be pushed on.

Reply to two peers by Sunday: did they get similar numbers? If not, where does the divergence come from — data, method or interpretation?

Find one thing your AI assistant gets wrong about European wind energy. This is not a trick question: every LLM has knowledge gaps, outdated data and failure modes. Your job is to find one, document it, and understand why it happened.

Suggested probes: recent offshore wind auction results (2024–2025) — check against WindEurope · Ørsted's 2023 impairments or cancelled projects — check against the annual report · a specific wind farm's capacity factor — check against operator disclosures · ask for a number, then ask again in a new conversation: same answer? · ask the AI to argue against wind investment — serious counter-case or strawman?

Post 250–350 words: the question you asked · what the AI said · what the truth is, with source · your diagnosis — hallucination, outdated data, bias, or something else? · what this changes in how you will use AI for the rest of the capstone.

This is one of the most important skills in the course. Every strong consultant knows exactly where their tools break.

Pick Ørsted or Eni. Ask your assistant: "Given current trends in offshore wind LCOE, EU policy, and this company's disclosed strategy, what are the three biggest risks to this company's 2040 positioning? Be specific and cite evidence where possible."

Then post the AI's three risks, add one risk the AI missed (and why you think it missed it), and say whether this analysis changes your preliminary view from Week 2. A low-stakes rehearsal for the kind of analysis you will do in Phase 1. If the two people in your team who pick the same company get different risks, that is worth a line in the post.
