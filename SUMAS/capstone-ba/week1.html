The client brief

Is wind energy a sound long-term investment for Europe's transition to net-zero by 2050?

This is a ten-week capstone in which your team works as a sustainability consultancy. You will not just read about the energy transition — you will build the data-driven, scenario-based analysis that real consulting firms deliver to energy companies, investment funds and policy makers, and write it as one report.

Two major energy companies represent opposite strategic approaches to Europe's energy future:

Your task

Conduct a comprehensive strategic analysis answering:

  • Will wind energy dominate Europe's electricity mix by 2040?
  • Which company is better positioned for the future: Ørsted or Eni?
  • What are the key risks, uncertainties and opportunities?
  • Under what scenarios does each strategy succeed or fail?

You deliver your answer as a professional consulting report with strategic recommendations backed by data, scenarios and systems analysis — one report per team, written on this site, in three phases, then an individual final exam. The hub has every date.

The report is modelled on last year'sThe June 2026 cohort wrote a 24-page report on this question — Europe's starting line, demand to 2040, three structural forces, the bottlenecks, a 2×2, two company playbooks. Your report page follows its structure, section by section, with a note under each heading on what they did there. They compared Ørsted with TotalEnergies; you compare it with Eni. Read theirs, then write a different one.

Team consulting, individual learning

The report is the team's; the learning is yours. Over ten weeks the team adopts five analyst roles in turn, and each of you has to be fluent in all of them — the forums, the self-checks and the exam are individual:

Data analyst2–3Energy fundamentals, market metrics, LCOE trends, project economics
Systems analyst4–5Stakeholders, grid infrastructure, demand dynamics, feedback loops
Strategy analyst6Business models, capital allocation, governance, transition pathways
Technology analyst7Turbine evolution, supply chains, learning curves and their limits
Foresight analyst8–9Scenario matrices, strategy stress-tests, robust options, the recommendation
A note on AI usage — encouraged, and required. You will learn to frame questions that extract insight from an assistant, to verify its output against primary sources, to use it for calculation and what-if modelling, and to document your use transparently. The standard: you must understand and be able to defend every analysis, calculation and conclusion, whether you produced it alone, with AI, or a teammate did. "The AI said so" is never an answer a client accepts — and neither is "my teammate wrote that part".
The plan

Ten weeks, three phases, one report

Every week has a Thursday session (an hour you run yourselves, two with me), one or two forum posts, and a few sections of the report that the week's material feeds. Three times the team hands a phase in from the report page and gets written feedback before the next is due.

How the grade is built

The phase marks are the team's. Forums and the exam are individual. The exam is open book: your notes, the readings, this site and your report — but no AI and no classmates.

Tonight · in the group hour

Form your team

Eight of you, two teams of four. Mix what you heard in the introductions: each team needs someone who likes spreadsheets and someone who likes writing. Sign in first — a team is a list of signed-in names — then join one; the picker closes a team at four. Give it a name together, and tell the page who holds which role.

The four roles

  • Coordinator — runs the group hour, keeps the plan, is the one I message when something is late.
  • Data lead — sources, numbers, the spreadsheet; every figure in the report has a year and a source because of this person.
  • Model lead — the Wright's Law fitter, the projections, the 2×2 and the stress test.
  • Editor — one voice across four writers, the word counts, the AI declaration, the hand-in.

Everyone writes sections. The roles say who is accountable for what; they can rotate at Phase 2 if you want them to.

Tonight · 15 minutes

The team charter

Four short answers, written together on the report page, above the report itself. Not graded; I read it before Week 2 and I will hold you to it in Week 5.

How we meet. Besides Thursday 17:15 — when, where, how long. A team that only meets in the group hour will not make Phase 1.
How we decide. What happens when two of you disagree about an axis, a number or a sentence. The coordinator decides? A vote? The editor's call on words, the model lead's on numbers?
Our AI policy. What you let an assistant do (draft, summarise, calculate, find sources) and what you never let it do (write a conclusion you cannot defend). How you declare it — every phase ends with an AI declaration.
Who writes what by Week 5. Phase 1 is sections 1–7 of the report. Put a name on each one now; change it later if you must, but never leave a section with no name.
