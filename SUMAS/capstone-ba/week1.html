The client brief

Is wind energy a sound long-term investment for Europe's transition to net-zero by 2050?

This is a ten-week capstone in which you work as an independent sustainability consultant. You will not just read about the energy transition — you will build the data-driven, scenario-based analysis that real consulting firms deliver to energy companies, investment funds and policy makers.

Two major energy companies represent opposite strategic approaches to Europe's energy future:

Ørsted (Denmark)
Transformed from an oil & gas company into the world's largest offshore wind developer. A pure-play bet on renewables.
TotalEnergies (France)
An integrated energy major balancing a massive fossil-fuel portfolio with growing renewable investments. A hedged transition strategy.

Your task

Conduct a comprehensive strategic analysis answering:

  • Will wind energy dominate Europe's electricity mix by 2040?
  • Which company is better positioned for the future: Ørsted or TotalEnergies?
  • What are the key risks, uncertainties and opportunities?
  • Under what scenarios does each strategy succeed or fail?

You deliver your answer as a professional consulting report with strategic recommendations backed by data, scenarios and systems analysis — in three phases, then a final exam. The hub has every date.

Individual consulting, collaborative learning

Unlike a group project, you work individually — building your own complete analysis from several expert perspectives, the way a boutique consulting firm works: small teams where each consultant has to be fluent in finance, technology, policy and strategy. But you are not alone. Weekly forums — on this site, where you share findings, debate interpretations and review each other's work. AI tools (Claude, ChatGPT, Perplexity) as your analytical assistant. And a peer challenge in Week 9 where two classmates stress-test your draft before you submit.

A note on AI usage — encouraged, and required. You will learn to frame questions that extract insight from an assistant, to verify its output against primary sources, to use it for calculation and what-if modelling, and to document your use transparently. The standard: you must understand and be able to defend every analysis, calculation and conclusion, whether you produced it alone or with AI. AI amplifies your thinking; it does not replace it. "The AI said so" is never an answer a client accepts.

Concept highlights · strategic forecasting essentials

Five ideas you will use every week

Use this section to build your analytical foundation. Work through anything unfamiliar — each one comes back, with more weight, in later weeks.

1 · The cone of uncertainty

Strategic decisions in energy, infrastructure and technology unfold over 10–30 years. Traditional planning assumes stability; modern systems face rapid technological change, geopolitical shocks and climate disruption. The cone says: short term (1–3 years), many variables remain predictable; medium term (5–10), key uncertainties emerge; long term (15–30), multiple divergent futures become possible.

Explore · drag the horizon

Implication: rather than predict one future, strategic forecasting explores multiple plausible futures and designs strategies that remain robust across them. Shell's scenarios methodology ↗ is the classic reference.

2 · Trends vs uncertainties

Not all unknowns are equal. Trends are relatively predictable developments — population ageing, urbanisation, digitalisation, solar cost curves. Uncertainties are high-impact factors with unclear trajectories — geopolitical conflict, technological breakthroughs, policy regime changes, social acceptance. The strategic matrix: high impact + low uncertainty → monitor and plan around; high impact + high uncertainty → build your scenarios on these; low impact → acknowledge, don't over-analyse.

Try it · trend or uncertainty? (European wind to 2040)

3 · Scenario planning

A foresight method pioneered by Shell in the 1970s. It does not predict the future — it prepares you for several. The process:

Define the question. What strategic decision requires long-term clarity?
Identify key uncertainties. What factors are both impactful and unpredictable?
Build scenarios. Construct 2–4 distinct future narratives.
Test strategies. How does each current strategy perform in each world?
Identify robust actions. Find the moves that work across several scenarios.

Example axes for wind energy — X: strength of climate policy (weak → strong); Y: speed of renewable cost decline (slow → fast). Four distinct futures to explore. You will build your own in Week 4.

4 · From stakeholders to systems

Most strategic analyses stop at stakeholder mapping: who are the actors? Systems thinking goes deeper — who are the actors (inventory) → how do they influence each other (interactions) → what are the feedback loops (dynamics). Two from wind energy:

Reinforcing loop (growth)
Falling LCOE → more investment → more deployment → more scale → lower LCOE → …
Balancing loop (constraint)
Faster deployment → grid congestion → curtailment → lower revenues → slower investment → …

Understanding these dynamics reveals where interventions create leverage — or where bottlenecks limit progress. Donella Meadows' systems-thinking primer ↗.

5 · The Doughnut

Traditional economics optimised for GDP growth. Kate Raworth proposes a different goal: humanity thriving within planetary boundaries. The doughnut has two rings — a social foundation (12 life essentials: food, water, energy, health, education…) and an ecological ceiling (9 planetary boundaries: climate, biodiversity, freshwater…). The goal is dynamic balance between shortfall and overshoot.

Applied to wind: the ecological-ceiling pressures are turbine manufacturing (rare earths, steel), seabed impacts and end-of-life blade disposal; the social-foundation enablers are energy access, affordability and a just transition for fossil-fuel workers. Strategic question: can Ørsted and TotalEnergies operate within the "safe and just space"? Week 7 returns to the ceiling with Rockström's numbers.

Case study

Ørsted's strategic transformation

In 2006, DONG Energy (Danish Oil and Natural Gas) was 85% fossil-fuel dependent. By 2017 it had rebranded as Ørsted and divested its oil and gas assets to become the world's largest offshore wind developer.

The pivot · tap a year
Tap a year above.

Mechanisms in action

  • Strategic commitment — bet the company on the learning curve continuing
  • Policy alignment — leveraged EU and Danish renewable mandates
  • Execution excellence — built project-development and supply-chain capabilities
  • Financial discipline — kept investment-grade credit through the transition

Reflection questions

  • What enabled Ørsted to make this transition when other utilities didn't?
  • Was this a strategic choice or regulatory necessity?
  • What were the biggest risks — and how did they manage them?
And the sequel the case leaves outIn late 2023 the same company took roughly $4 billion of impairments and cancelled Ocean Wind 1 and 2 in New Jersey; in 2025 it cancelled Hornsea 4 and raised DKK 60bn of new equity. Hold both halves of the story in your head from day one: the transformation and the reset. Week 4 uses the 2023 episode as its cautionary tale about forecasting without scenarios.

Sources: Ørsted Annual Report 2023 and Sustainability Report 2023, orsted.com/investors ↗.

Starting line · 2026

Where wind stands today

Before forecasting 2040, anchor on now. Europe is mid-transition: renewables rising fast, coal fading, gas still holding the grid together. Two different numbers get confused in every first draft:

Two different numbers, often confused

In the EU's total energy mix — heat, transport, industry, everything — renewables are about a fifth. In electricity alone they are roughly half, and in 2025 wind and solar together out-generated fossil fuels for the first time. Which number you quote changes the story; say which one you mean, every time.

A handful of North Sea nations hold most of it. Offshore's appeal is technical: stronger, steadier winds allow far larger turbines — today's standard is the 14–15 MW platform, nearly double the output per foundation of five years ago. The trade-off is cost: harsher conditions, complex grid connection, and higher installation and maintenance bills than onshore. Sources: Eurostat 2025 · WindEurope 2025 · European Commission 2025.

Activities · this week's forums

Three posts, all on this site

Forums are where this course happens. Each post is marked (the 10% participation grade is built from them), each one is read by me and by the class, and I reply in the thread. Sign in once with your name and six-digit code — the same login carries your progress between devices.

Write ≈ 250 words introducing yourself to the class:

  • Your professional background or study area
  • Your experience with energy, sustainability or consulting (if any)
  • Why this capstone project interests you
  • One specific question you hope to answer about wind energy or strategic forecasting

Find and document three data points about European wind energy. Examples: current EU wind capacity (GW); wind's share of EU electricity generation (%); offshore wind LCOE, 2015 vs 2024 ($/MWh); Ørsted's market capitalisation; TotalEnergies' renewable investment target.

For each data point: state the metric and value, cite the source with a link, and write one sentence on why it matters. A simple table or list is the right format. Then read one classmate's three and check one of their numbers against a second source — say what you found.

After the readings: create a stakeholder map of the European offshore wind energy system.

  1. Identify at least 8 key actors — governments, companies, investors, grid operators, NGOs, consumers, manufacturers…
  2. Map their relationships: who influences whom? Where are the power dynamics?
  3. Identify one surprising connection you discovered
  4. Write 300–400 words explaining the map (describe it in text, or attach an image link), the most important relationships, one key tension or conflict between stakeholders, and how the mapping changed your initial understanding of wind energy

AI assistance encouraged. Try: "Help me identify the key actors in the European offshore wind energy system and map their relationships." Document which tools you used at the end of your post. Then reply to two classmates: name an actor they left out, or a relationship you read differently.

Evaluation: quality of analysis, depth of insight, engagement with the concepts — and whether your replies gave a classmate something to act on.
