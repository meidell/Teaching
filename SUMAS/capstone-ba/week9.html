The consulting recommendation

The final content week. You have a system analysis, a fitted curve, three projections, an industry cross-check, and four scenarios that map the strategic space. This week you do the one thing that distinguishes consulting work from analysis: you make a recommendation. Phase 3 is not Phase 1 plus Phase 2 stapled together with a conclusion. It is a single, integrated, forward-looking report that uses your earlier work as evidence for a specific claim.

Read

Wack, P. (1985) Scenarios: Uncharted Waters Ahead. Harvard Business Review, 63(5), 73–89.
hbr.org ↗ (through the library if paywalled). The founding case study of corporate scenario planning: Wack ran scenarios at Shell in the early 1970s and helped the company anticipate the 1973 oil shock when its competitors did not. Focus on the distinction between first-generation scenarios (descriptions of possible futures) and second-generation scenarios (ones that actually change how decision-makers think and act); his claim that the strategist's job is to "rattle the manager's microcosm"; the two failures he warns against — scenarios that confirm what executives already believe, and scenarios so far outside their experience they get dismissed; and the role of predetermined elements versus genuine uncertainties.

If your scenarios pass Wack's test — they force a re-perception of the present, not just a description of the future — you are doing real consulting work.

Watch

Scenario planning: tune in to possible futures, risks and actions — search YouTube; several good 15–20-minute explainers exist, including from McKinsey and the Oxford Scenarios Programme.

Core concepts · 1

From analyst to advisor

You have been an analyst for eight weeks. This week you become an advisor. The difference is small but consequential. An analyst describes the world. An advisor commits to a position the client should take in that world. An analyst can hedge across all four scenarios. An advisor names the one most likely to unfold and the one most dangerous to ignore. That commitment is uncomfortable. It is also what the client is paying for.

Wack's framing is useful here. First-generation scenarios read like a documentary — interesting, plausible, but the executive nods, sets the report aside, and changes nothing. Second-generation scenarios "rattle the manager's microcosm" — they make the executive uncomfortable, force a re-perception of the present, and change the decision. They are scenarios that bite.

The test for your Phase 3: would the CEO of Ørsted or Eni, reading your scenarios, change anything? If not, you have a first-generation report. Push harder.

Core concepts · 2

The robust-strategy test

Place each company into each of your four scenarios. Score how they fare. This is the analytical move that distinguishes a consultant from a fan. A company that thrives in one scenario and fails in three is a high-variance bet. A company that survives in all four is a more robust position — but may forgo upside.

Score it · name your four scenarios, then rate each company in each

Thrive = 3 · Survive = 2 · Struggle = 1 · Fail = 0. The totals are a conversation starter, not a verdict: a 9 built from 3-3-3-0 and a 9 built from 2-2-2-3 are different recommendations for different clients.

There is no objectively correct recommendation. It depends on what the client values: a pension fund prioritising capital preservation will favour robustness; a growth-focused investor will accept variance for upside; a government planning industrial policy will weigh different factors entirely. Be explicit about whose perspective you are advising from. "From the perspective of a long-horizon institutional investor prioritising capital preservation, [Company X] is better positioned because…" — that is a recommendation a client can use.

Two playbooks — read before you score, then decide if you agreeØrsted — win by executing. In a world where the binding constraint is execution capacity, not project access, thirty years of regulatory relationships across eight jurisdictions are the moat; the levers are portfolio over flagship (400–800 MW projects that fit supply-chain limits), cutting LCOE and diversifying revenue (PPAs alongside CfDs, capital recycling), and locking vessel and turbine slots as Europe's largest component buyer. The threat is Chinese state-backed developers with structurally lower costs — a defence that must be institutional, not cost-based. Eni — win by integrating. Sell value, not volume: firm, dispatchable, decarbonised power bundled with storage and long-term PPAs, which a pure-play cannot match; the 7.5 GW German North Sea hub with RWE for procurement and O&M synergies; LNG and oil cash flows that let it invest counter-cyclically. The internal risk is capital-allocation drift back to hydrocarbons when their margins beat renewables — fixable only by a board-level minimum commitment. The market does not need both firms to play the same way.
Core concepts · 3

Early-warning signals

A recommendation is only useful if the client can act on it as the world evolves. Which means you need to tell them what to watch for. For each of your four scenarios, identify two or three early-warning signals — indicators that, if they move in a particular direction over the next 24–36 months, would tell the client this scenario is becoming dominant. Examples:

  • "If Chinese OEMs win more than 15% of new EU offshore auctions in the next 18 months, the High-China-Integration scenario is unfolding."
  • "If EU offshore wind LCOE remains above $90/MWh through 2026, the Stalled Transition becomes the central case."
  • "If Plenitude's installed renewable capacity misses Eni's own plan for two consecutive years, the hedged transition strategy is not being funded."

The point is operational specificity. Your recommendation, your signals, and the company's actual decision-making should be linked tightly enough that a client could build a quarterly review around them.

Writing the integrated report

4,000–5,000 words — a workable structure

SectionWordsWhat goes here
Executive summary~400One page. The recommendation, the conditions under which it holds, the signals to watch. A busy executive reads only this.
The system~700Distilled from Phase 1. Stakeholders, drivers, feedback loops, scenario matrix — tightened and updated with what you have learned since.
The evidence~900Distilled from Phase 2. Indicators, learning curve, three projections, industry cross-check. Charts that matter.
The scenarios~700Your four 2040 worlds, made vivid in Wack's sense. Each in 150–180 words.
The robust-strategy test~800The scoring matrix. How each company fares in each scenario. The pattern that emerges.
Recommendation and signals~600Your recommendation, the perspective it serves, the conditions under which it holds, the signals to watch.
Limitations~300What you don't know. What could invalidate the recommendation. Where the next phase of work would go.
  • No section should duplicate Phase 1 or Phase 2 verbatim. Every sentence earns its place in this document.
  • Make the charts count. Five excellent charts beat fifteen average ones.
  • Write for someone who has 20 minutes. They read your executive summary, scan your scenarios, look at one chart, and decide whether to take you seriously. Design accordingly.

What to include

  • The seven sections above, or a defensible variant
  • Charts that earn their space — from Phase 2 and any new ones synthesising the scenario analysis
  • A complete reference list integrating sources from all three phases
  • A short AI declaration at the end — which assistants, for what purposes, with a reflection on the energy and water footprint you estimated in Weeks 6 and 7

Activities · this week

Draft, challenge, submit

Activity 1 — Draft recommendation (post by Wednesday). Share your draft recommendation in 400–500 words: your recommendation in one clear sentence · the perspective it serves (institutional investor, government, pension fund…) · the conditions under which it holds · your two or three strongest pieces of evidence · the single most uncomfortable counter-argument you can imagine, and how you would answer it. This is not your full Phase 3. It is the spine of your argument, exposed for stress-testing.

Activity 2 — The peer challenge (replies by Friday). I assign challenger pairs on Thursday morning, across the two teams; each of you challenges two members of the other team and is challenged by two. Each challenge is a reply of 200–300 words covering: the weakest link — which step in their argument is most vulnerable? · the counter-evidence — what data or scenario, if true, would undermine their recommendation? · the honest question — what would a hostile client actually ask in the room? Challenges are graded on the quality of the challenge, not on whether the recipient agrees. This is how strategy consultancies actually work internally.

A note on tone. Challenge the argument, not the person. The best peer challenges are simultaneously the hardest on the work and the kindest to the author. Aim for both.
