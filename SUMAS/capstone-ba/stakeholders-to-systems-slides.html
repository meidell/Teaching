A vote · chat, one letter

Who decides whether an offshore turbine gets built?

  • A — the developer's board (Ørsted, Eni)
  • B — the government that runs the auction
  • C — the bank or pension fund that lends the money
  • D — the grid operator who has to connect it
  • E — the coastal community and the court it can go to

Nobody alone. Every one of them can stop it, and none of them can build it by themselves — which is why a list of actors is not yet an analysis.

Let the letters pile up in the chat, then press → to reveal. Whatever wins the vote, name one real project that the other four stopped or delayed: an auction with no bidders (the UK's 2023 round, zero offshore bids at the price offered), a grid connection years late, a court case on a permit, a financing that fell through when rates rose. The point lands by itself: the answer is a system, not an actor. That is tonight.
The idea

Three questions, three levels of analysis

1 · InventoryWho are the actors?A list, grouped. Governments, companies, investors, operators, communities, NGOs, consumers. Where most analyses stop.
2 · InteractionsHow do they influence each other?Arrows between actors. Money, rules, legitimacy. Who can say no; who has to say yes.
3 · DynamicsWhat are the feedback loops?Chains of cause and effect that close on themselves — and therefore grow, limit, or overshoot. Where leverage lives.

Toolkit idea 4 · report section 5 · the glossary entries reinforcing loop, balancing loop, causal-loop diagram

Draw it as a staircase in words: each step contains the one before. You cannot draw a loop between actors you have not listed; you cannot find a loop in a map with no arrows. Most consulting reports stop on step one and call it a stakeholder analysis. Section 5's guidance says it in one line — "a stakeholder list without loops is a directory".

High power · low interestKeep satisfied. The finance ministry, the defence ministry over a sea area, a TSO with other priorities. Dormant until something wakes them.

The classic Mendelow grid; it is in every strategy textbook and it is level 1, not level 2 — it still has no arrows. Level 1 · Inventory · our two companies

Ørsted and Eni are actors in each other's system

Ørsted

A pure-play developer. Everything it holds is a project pipeline and the capability to build it; everything it needs — capital, permits, grid, vessels — is held by someone else. High interest in every row of the table.

Eni (Plenitude)

An integrated major with a renewables arm. Its capital comes from oil and gas; its shareholders, regulators and NGOs relate to the parent, not the wind farm. It can wait for an auction that Ørsted cannot afford to skip.

Same system, two very different positions in it. The question of the course — which is better positioned? — is a question about where each sits in the map, not only about their numbers.

This is the slide that ties the session to the report. Ørsted is exposed to every arrow; Eni is hedged by its parent. Neither is "better" yet — in a slow-policy world the hedge wins, in a fast-cost world the pure play does, and that is Week 4. Tonight only plant the thought: position in a system is a strategic variable.

Level 2 · Interactions

An arrow is a verb: who can make whom do what

Money

Lends, buys, subsidises, prices. A lender sets the hurdle rate a project must clear; an auction sets the price a developer can earn.

Rules

Permits, connects, zones, bans. A government opens the sea; a TSO decides when the cable arrives; a court can stop both.

Legitimacy

Approves, objects, reports, shames. NGOs, science, the press, and the town meeting decide what counts as acceptable — and governments feel it.

Draw an arrow only when you can say the verb. "Investors → developers" means nothing; "pension funds set the cost of capital that decides which auction bids are possible" is an interaction.

The verb discipline is the whole of level 2. Students draw arrows between every pair and call it a map; ask for the verb and half the arrows disappear. Three kinds is a simplification that serves: money, rules, legitimacy. Note that legitimacy flows into rules — the town meeting reaches the minister — which is already a chain, and chains are what level 3 closes.

Level 2 · Interactions

The connection you did not expect

  • Fishing fleets → the permit timeline. Not through the minister: through the environmental impact study a court can reopen.
  • Pension funds → NGOs. The same funds that lend to wind farms are asked by their own members to divest from oil — which reaches Eni's board before any regulator does.
  • Data centres → the auction price. New demand changes what a megawatt-hour is worth in 2035, which changes what a developer can bid today.
  • Vessel owners → the 2030 target. A handful of installation vessels in the world; their order books are a ceiling on how fast anyone can build.

Forum 2 asks you for one of these. "Surprising" means: not on the direct path from government to developer.

These four are offered, not asserted as facts to be quoted — each is a mechanism, and the forum marks a student who finds one with a source. The vessel point is the one that converts most students to systems thinking: the binding constraint on a continent's energy plan can be a dozen ships. If someone asks for the number, say it is theirs to find and cite.
Level 2 · Interactions

Three tensions, built into the system

Developers vs grid operators

The turbine is built in three years; the cable and the substations take longer. Whoever finishes first waits, and waiting is paid for by someone.

Auctions vs manufacturers

A government that buys on price alone gets cheap bids — and then a supply chain that cannot build them at that price. Zero-bid rounds are the symptom.

National targets vs local consent

The target is set in the capital; the pylons, the port and the view are in a town that did not vote for it. Compensation is the usual bridge; a court the usual detour.

A tension is two actors pulling on the same variable in opposite directions. Report section 5 wants at least one, named.

Make the room say which variable each tension pulls on: the first is connection date, the second is strike price, the third is permit time. Naming the variable is the bridge to level 3 — a variable that two actors push on is exactly where a balancing loop forms. Next slide puts them to work on it.

Level 3 · Dynamics

A map is a photograph. The system is a film.

Add time to the arrows and something happens that no actor intended: chains of cause and effect close on themselves.

  • A chain: more capacity → lower cost per unit → more capacity. Each link is an arrow you already drew.
  • A loop: a chain that returns to where it started. From then on it drives itself.
  • Two kinds only. One reinforces — each turn makes the next bigger. One balances — each turn pushes back toward a limit.
The claim of the whole evening: the most important things in a system are not actors but loops, because a loop keeps running after every actor has gone home. Two kinds only, and every loop you will ever draw is one of them. Next two slides: the course's own two, drawn.
Level 3 · Dynamics · the growth engine

The reinforcing loop

Offshore wind's LCOE fell by about 70% in the fourteen years to 2024 — Week 2's number. That is this loop, turning.

Walk it clockwise, starting anywhere, saying "more … so more …". Every arrow is a plus: more of the tail gives more of the head. Four pluses, no minus — reinforcing. Then the uncomfortable half: a reinforcing loop also runs backwards. Higher cost → less investment → less deployment → less learning → higher cost. 2022–23 was that: rates and steel prices rose, bids failed, write-downs followed. Same loop, other direction. That is why it is called reinforcing and not "growth".
Level 3 · Dynamics · how to read one

Sign every arrow, then count the minuses

+ on an arrow

More of the tail gives more of the head (and less gives less). Investment → deployment is a plus.

− on an arrow

More of the tail gives less of the head. Curtailment → revenue is a minus.

  • Even number of minuses (including none) → the loop is reinforcing, R
  • Odd number of minuses → the loop is balancing, B

Sterman's notation; the glossary's causal-loop diagram. Phase 1 wants two loops minimum, every arrow signed.

This is the one mechanical rule of the evening and it must be said twice. Students want to label a loop "good" or "bad"; the sign rule stops that. A balancing loop that holds a grid stable is good; a balancing loop that caps deployment is the course's whole problem — same kind of loop. Test the rule on the next slide.
Level 3 · Dynamics · the constraint

The balancing loop

One minus — curtailment cuts revenue — so the loop pushes back. The faster you build, the harder it pushes.

Walk it: more deployment, more congestion, more curtailment, less revenue, less investment, less deployment. One minus, odd, balancing. Then connect it to Exercise 2: this is the developer-versus-TSO tension drawn as a loop, with time added. Ask which actor sits on each arrow — the TSO on the congestion arrow, the market rules on the curtailment-to-revenue arrow (is curtailed output compensated or not? a rule, and therefore a lever: slide 23).
Level 3 · Dynamics

Every reinforcing loop eventually meets a balancing one

Early: R dominates

Cost falls, investment floods in, capacity grows faster each year. The curve bends upward. 2010–2020 offshore wind.

Later: B catches up

Grid, ports, vessels, permits, public patience — one of them binds. Growth slows toward a limit. The curve bends over: an S.

The 2040 question is not "will the R loop keep turning" but "which B loop binds first, and when".

This is the hinge of the course: the learning curve (Week 3) is the R loop in numbers; the limits (Week 7) are the B loops. Say now that an analyst who projects the R loop to 2040 without naming a B loop has written a brochure. The report's section 4, bottlenecks, is a list of candidate B loops; section 5 is where they are drawn.
Level 3 · Dynamics

Delays — why systems overshoot

  • A permit takes years; a grid connection, often longer; a vessel is ordered years before it sails. The signal arrives late.
  • So actors keep pushing after the system has already turned: capacity ordered at 2021 prices, delivered into 2023 interest rates.
  • A loop with a long delay oscillates: boom, write-down, pause, boom. Ørsted's 2023 impairments on its US projects are the textbook case.

In a causal-loop diagram a delay is two short lines across the arrow. Mark them: a loop with a delay behaves differently from the same loop without one.

Keep the mechanism, not the figures: Ørsted bid for US projects under one set of costs and rates and reached final investment decisions under another, and the delay between the two is what the write-down measured. If a student asks for the number, send them to the 2023 annual report — it is Week 4's cautionary tale and they should cite it, not hear it from me. The oscillation point matters for Phase 3: a robust strategy is one that survives the trough, not one that bets on the peak.
Check · chat, R or B

Find the loop

  • 1 — More turbines in a sea area → more objections → longer permits → fewer turbines B · name the variable permit time: objections → permit time (+), permit time → turbines (−) — one minus
  • 2 — More projects → more orders → factories expand → cheaper turbines → more projects R · all plus
  • 3 — Higher power prices → more wind built → more wind output → lower prices at windy hours → lower revenue per turbine → fewer built B · the cannibalisation loop — odd minuses
  • 4 — More wind on the grid → more need for storage → more storage built → less curtailment → more revenue → more wind R · a B loop turned into an R loop by one investment — that is leverage
One at a time: everyone types R or B, then → reveals. Loop 1 is deliberately awkward — whether a link is + or − depends on how you name the variable (permit time vs permit speed), and the lesson is: name variables so that "more" is unambiguous, then sign. Loop 3 is the one Phase 3 will care about: price cannibalisation is the balancing loop that no learning curve can beat. Loop 4 is the bridge to the next slide — storage changes a sign, and changing a sign is the strongest thing an actor can do.
Level 3 · Dynamics · where to push

Leverage — Meadows' ladder, shortened

  1. weakParametersa subsidy rate, a tariff, a tax
  2. ↑Buffers and delaysgrid capacity in reserve; permit time
  3. ↑The strength of a loopa contract for difference that guarantees revenue
  4. ↑The rules of the systemhow an auction is designed; who pays for curtailment
  5. ↑The goal of the systemnet-zero by 2050; energy independence
  6. strongThe paradigm it comes fromwhat counts as prosperity — the doughnut

Donella Meadows, Leverage Points (1999) — the toolkit's link. Twelve rungs in the original; six here.

Read upward. The lower rungs are where governments spend most effort — adjusting a subsidy by a few percent — and where the system barely notices. The upper rungs are where a system actually changes: the rule that says curtailed output is compensated turns a B loop off; a target of 2050 pulls every actor's planning horizon. The paradigm rung is the doughnut, three slides on. Keep the pace: this slide and the next are one idea in two parts.
Level 3 · Dynamics · where to push

Leverage in European wind — four real levers

LeverRungWhat it does to a loop
Contracts for differenceloop strengthGuarantees the revenue arrow, so the R loop keeps turning when prices fall at windy hours
Who pays for curtailmentruleCompensated: the B loop is cut at the revenue arrow. Uncompensated: it bites the developer
Grid build-out and permitsdelayShortens the delay that makes the system overshoot; the lever every actor asks for and nobody owns alone
The 2050 target, in lawgoalChanges every actor's horizon at once — the lender's, the manufacturer's, the town's

For the report: an intervention you recommend should name the loop it acts on and the rung it sits on.

Ask which lever Ørsted can pull and which Eni can: a developer can lobby for the first two and bid under them; neither company owns the grid or the target. That asymmetry — the strongest levers belong to the state — is why a strategy for a company has to be robust across policy worlds rather than assume one. Week 4's matrix has policy on an axis for exactly this reason.
Level 3 · Dynamics

The boundary — what is in, and what you leave out on purpose

Inside

European offshore wind to 2040: the actors on your table, the two loops, the grid, the auctions, the supply chain, the two companies.

Outside — and say so

Global steel prices, Chinese turbine exports, US policy, the price of gas. They reach in as exogenous shocks — arrows from outside the boundary — but you do not model what drives them.

A system map without a boundary is a map of everything, which is a map of nothing. Section 5 asks for the boundary in one paragraph.

Students either include the world or forget to say what they excluded. The rule: one paragraph, "in scope / out of scope / enters as a shock". The price of gas is the best example — it sets the power price that the whole R loop depends on, and it is driven by things no wind analyst models. Name it as an input and move on.
Level 3 · the outer frame

The doughnut — the loops have a floor and a ceiling

Ecological ceiling · nine boundariesThe R loop pushes against it: steel and rare earths for turbines, seabed and bird impacts, blades at end of life. Growth that crosses the ceiling is not the goal.
Social foundation · twelve essentialsThe loops have to lift it: energy access and affordability, jobs for the workers the fossil fleet releases, consent from the towns that host the pylons.

Raworth's question for both companies: can the loop you are betting on run inside the safe and just space — and if it cannot, which B loop will the ceiling itself become?

Top rung of the ladder: the paradigm. The doughnut reframes the R loop — growth is not the goal, thriving within limits is — and it also predicts B loops that the market does not: a blade-disposal rule, a seabed moratorium, a just-transition levy. Two minutes; this is the slide to cut if you are late, because the toolkit page carries it.

AI

An AI is a good co-mapper and a bad cartographer

Use it for

Brainstorming actors you forgot. Suggesting verbs for an arrow. Proposing a loop and letting you sign it. "List the actors in European offshore wind and who can stop a project" is a fine prompt.

Never let it

Tell you a loop is reinforcing — count the minuses yourself. Give you a number without a source you opened. Write the boundary: the choice of what to leave out is the analyst's.

Declare what it did, in the post and in section 18. Then show the one thing it got wrong.

The course standard, restated for this week. The typical AI failure on this task is confident loops with no signs and actors with no holdings — exactly level 1 dressed as level 3. Catching that is a good audit entry for Week 3.
