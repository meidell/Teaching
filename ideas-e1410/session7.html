Section 7.1 · The brief

Delta Assure: 23 days, $18, and a board that wants both cut

The case

Crop-loss assessment from space

Delta Assure is an agricultural micro-insurer based in Can Tho, Vietnam. It writes 340,000 smallholder rice policies across the Mekong Delta, on plots averaging 0.8 hectares. When a farmer claims for flood, drought or pest loss, a field agent travels to the plot and estimates the percentage of the crop lost.

That process works, and it is slow and expensive: 23 days on average to settle a claim, at about $18 per assessment, across roughly 68,000 claims a year. For a farmer whose income has just been destroyed, 23 days is the difference between replanting this season and not.

The proposal. Assess loss automatically from Sentinel-2 satellite imagery (free, 10-metre resolution, five-day revisit) combined with provincial weather-station data. Target: settle 90% of claims within 72 hours at under $2 per claim, agreeing with a field assessment to within ±10% on 85% of claims.

Deadline
9 months — live before the next monsoon season
Team
7 people, to be assembled
Budget
$850,000 build
Sponsor
COO · board wants loss-adjustment expense cut 60%

The data. Six years of historical claims — about 410,000 assessments — where the label is the field agent's loss estimate. Plus the satellite archive and the weather series. The regulator is insurance supervision at the Ministry of Finance, which must be satisfied that a claim decision can be explained to the farmer who receives it.

Three things the team already knows and has not resolved. Two of the six years were unusually dry, so the historical record is not a representative sample of normal seasons. The labels are human judgments — two agents assessing the same plot do not produce the same number. And remote districts have historically been visited less often, so they are under-represented in exactly the data the model would learn from.

Your first decision

Before anything else, the board will ask when it pays for itself.

You have the numbers you need: 68,000 claims a year, cost falling from $18 to $2 per assessment, against an $850,000 build. Estimate the payback period.

Your estimate
24months
Locked. On those numbers, about 9 months. 68,000 claims × $16 saved = $1,088,000 a year; $850,000 ÷ $1,088,000 × 12 ≈ 9.4 months. Which is why this proposal will be approved, and why you should be careful. That calculation is wrong in the way most AI business cases are wrong: it prices the build and ignores the operating loop. Satellite ingestion, monitoring, drift detection, annual retraining and the field-agent audits you will need for the regulator are real recurring costs — call it $180,000 a year, and the honest figure becomes $908,000 net, or a payback nearer 11 months. Still excellent. But the version you present determines whether month 13 is a success or an overspend.

Two more things worth noticing before you go further. The 60% expense cut the board wants is not the same target as the ±10% accuracy on 85% of claims the team is building toward — nobody has yet checked that hitting the second delivers the first. And a claim settled wrongly is not a metric; it is a farmer who cannot replant.

Section 7.2 · Diagnose

Which toolkit does each problem need?

How to read a brief

The skill is triage, not recall

A real brief arrives as a jumble: some sentences are objectives, some are constraints, some are risks wearing the costume of details. An experienced project manager reads it once and sorts it — this is a scoping problem, that is a resourcing problem, this one is a lifecycle risk that nobody has noticed yet.

You now have six toolkits. Their one-line summaries, as a reference sheet for the rest of this session:

S1 · Foundations
is this an AI problem, and what does success mean?
S2 · Planning
lifecycle, requirements, WBS, schedule
S3 · Resources
people, infrastructure, cost, estimation
S4 · Risk
identify, analyse, mitigate, govern
S5 · Delivery
agile, Scrum, metrics, stakeholders
S6 · Monitoring
KPIs, drift, QA, sustained value

Half of project management is naming the problem correctly. A resourcing problem treated as a scheduling problem produces a later deadline and the same failure.

Section 7.3 · The data

410,000 assessments, and not one clean label

The trap

"We have six years of data" is a claim, not a fact

The most valuable thing a project manager can do in week one of an AI project is refuse to be reassured by a row count. Delta Assure has 410,000 historical assessments. Take that apart:

  • The label is a human estimate. "60% of the crop lost" is one agent's judgment on one day. Two agents on the same plot disagree. So the model's ceiling is not the truth — it is agreement with a noisy human process, and your ±10% target is measured against that noise.
  • Two of six years were unusually dry. A third of the training data describes conditions that are not typical. A model fitted on it will be quietly miscalibrated for a normal season, and nobody will notice until the first normal season.
  • Remote districts are under-represented because they were visited less. The model will therefore be least accurate precisely where farmers are poorest, most remote and least able to contest a decision. That is a fairness problem, a regulatory problem and a reputational problem in one sentence.
  • Satellite imagery has gaps. A five-day revisit is not five-day coverage — the Mekong Delta is cloudy, and monsoon flooding is exactly when cloud cover is worst. The moment you most need an image is the moment you are least likely to have one.

That last point deserves emphasis, because it is the kind of thing that never appears in a brief and decides projects. The proposal's whole value is fast settlement after a weather event. If cloud blocks imagery for the week following a flood, the system cannot do the one thing it was funded to do. Somebody has to check that before month seven.

Ask three questions of any dataset: who made the labels, what conditions do they describe, and who is missing. The third one is where the ethics and the regulator live.

What a good response looks like

Turning data problems into project decisions

None of the above is a reason to stop. It is a reason to change the plan. Reasonable responses: commission a double-assessment study where two agents independently assess several hundred plots, so you can measure label noise and set an honest accuracy target; re-weight or supplement remote-district data before training rather than after; treat the dry years as a separate scenario rather than pooling them; and check historical cloud-cover statistics for the monsoon window in week two, not month seven.

Notice that all four are cheap, all four are decisions a project manager can drive without touching a model, and all four become impossible to retro-fit later.

Section 7.4 · Decisions

Nine months, seven people, and a monsoon

The constraint that shapes everything

The deadline is not negotiable, which changes what "good" means

Delta Assure must be live before the next monsoon, because that is when claims arrive. A deadline set by weather cannot slip, and that single fact should reorganise the whole plan:

  • Scope becomes the flexible variable. If something must give, it is not the date and not the quality floor — it is coverage. Launch on three provinces, not twelve.
  • A minimum viable model earns its place. A simple index-based model that settles the clear-cut cases and routes ambiguous ones to a field agent delivers most of the value at a fraction of the risk — and gives you a real baseline for A/B comparison.
  • Human-in-the-loop is the safety net and the fallback. The field-agent process still exists. Keeping it for contested and low-confidence cases is both the mitigation and the rollback plan.
  • Phased funding maps naturally onto this. Release tranche two when the label-noise study completes and cloud coverage is confirmed; tranche three when the pilot province hits the accuracy floor.

When the date is fixed by something outside your control, stop negotiating the date. Negotiate the scope, and make the fallback a design feature rather than an admission.

The explainability requirement is a design constraint

Read the regulator's sentence again

The regulator must be satisfied that a claim decision can be explained to the farmer who receives it. That is not a documentation task to be handled at the end. It rules some options out entirely and it should be visible in the architecture from week one — which is the Session 4 lesson arriving as an engineering decision.

"Your claim was assessed at 40% loss because the vegetation index on your plot fell 38% against the district average in the two weeks after the flood, and rainfall at the nearest station was 210 mm above normal" is an explanation a farmer can hear, contest and act on. A deep model's raw output is not. If explainability is genuinely binding, an interpretable model is not a compromise — it is the requirement.

Section 7.5 · The recommendation

Three answers, and only one weak one

What a recommendation actually is

Go, go-with-conditions, or no-go

Every case analysis ends in a decision, and there are only three shapes it can take. The middle one is almost always the right answer and almost always the hardest to write, because it requires you to name the conditions precisely enough that someone could hold you to them.

Go
the case is sound and the risks are managed — proceed as proposed
Go with conditions
proceed, but only with specific changes and named gates that must be passed
No-go
a fundamental assumption fails and no amount of resourcing fixes it

For Delta Assure, a defensible answer is go with conditions, and the value is entirely in the conditions. For example: reduce launch scope to three provinces; complete the label-noise and cloud-cover studies before tranche two releases; set the accuracy target against measured inter-agent agreement rather than an assumed ±10%; keep field assessment for low-confidence and contested claims; commit an annual operating budget of around $180,000 before approval, not after; and report on remote-district accuracy separately from the aggregate.

Notice what makes that strong: every condition is checkable, and every one traces back to a specific weakness in the brief. A recommendation that says "proceed with caution and monitor risks closely" is not a recommendation. It is a way of appearing to have an opinion.

The weak answer is not "no-go". The weak answer is a "go" with no conditions, or conditions nobody could ever verify.

Instrument it

What you would actually watch after launch

A recommendation without a monitoring set is unfinished, because it gives the board nothing to hold you to. Session 6's three categories, applied here: model performance (agreement with field assessment, and its distribution — not just the mean), operational (imagery availability, settlement time, throughput during a claims surge), and business outcome (loss-adjustment expense, farmer contest rate, and whether farmers actually replant in time).

That last one is the metric the whole project exists for and the one least likely to appear on a dashboard. It is also the one that would persuade a regulator.
