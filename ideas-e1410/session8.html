Section 8.1 · The hook

What is your assignment actually marked on?

Your decision

Most students optimise for the wrong criterion.

Your final case study is worth 50% of this course, and it is marked against four criteria with different weights. Almost everyone assumes the marks are mostly for coverage — describing everything, completing every section, showing you did the reading.

Before we look at the actual rubric, estimate: what percentage of the marks come from critical thinking — that is, from analysis, judgment and argument rather than description?

Your estimate
25%
Locked. It is 30% — the single largest weight. The full rubric: critical thinking 30, research 25, application 25, structure 20. Read that carefully, because it tells you how to spend your time. Critical thinking and application together are 55% of the mark, and neither is earned by describing a framework. They are earned by taking a position, defending it against the obvious objection, and showing what the framework produced when you applied it to your project. A submission that summarises all six sessions accurately and takes no position is heading for a mediocre mark — not because it is wrong, but because it never answered a question.
Why this session exists

You have already done the hard work. Across seven workshops you specified a project end to end — problem, plan, resources, risk, delivery, operation — and you rehearsed the whole analysis on an unfamiliar case in Session 7. The raw material for the final assignment already exists in your workbook.

What remains is the part nobody teaches: turning a set of correct answers into a piece of writing that argues something. That is tonight.

Your workbook is evidence. Your assignment is an argument. Marks come from the second one — and the commonest failure in a graded case study is a complete answer with nothing at stake.

Section 8.2 · Review

Six sessions, one argument

The arc

What we actually did

Each session answered one question, and each answer created the next question. That structure is not decorative — it is the lifecycle of a real AI project, and it is a defensible skeleton for your assignment:

  • S1 · Foundations — Is this an AI problem, and what would success mean? AI projects are probabilistic, data-dependent and experimental; success needs both a technical and a business definition.
  • S2 · Planning — How do we structure work whose output is unknown? Lifecycle frameworks, functional / non-functional / data requirements, WBS, and a schedule that admits uncertainty.
  • S3 · Resources — What does it take to run? The model is a small part of the system; five roles, four infrastructure layers, five cost categories, four estimation techniques, phased funding.
  • S4 · Risk — What can go wrong? Five risk categories, qualitative before quantitative, four mitigation strategies, and governance that decides whether deployment is permitted at all.
  • S5 · Delivery — How does the work actually run, and what do we say about it? Hybrid agile, sprints that deliver validated learning, and metrics translated into consequences.
  • S6 · Monitoring — How do we keep it working? Three KPI categories, three kinds of drift, QA as continuous governance, and real cases showing that people and process decide the outcome.
The four through-lines

If you remember nothing else

Underneath the frameworks, the same four ideas kept reappearing. These are the ideas worth arguing from in your assignment, because they connect everything:

Data decides
quality, labels and representativeness set the ceiling — no algorithm recovers from bad data
Uncertainty is structural
not sloppiness — so estimate honestly, fund in phases, and declare it rather than hide it
Humans stay in the loop
augment rather than replace; a named person is accountable for every outcome
Value needs governance
monitoring, documentation and ownership are what make a working model a lasting one

Notice that none of the four is technical. That is the course's actual claim: the hard problems in AI project management are problems of data, uncertainty, people and governance — and a project manager who understands those four can be useful on an AI project without writing a line of model code.

Every case study in Session 6 succeeded for the same reasons: a targeted pilot, a human still deciding, real governance, and a number a board recognised. None of them won on model sophistication.

One last idea, and it is the one that outlives the course

Cultural debt — what your organisation chooses to celebrate

The 2015 paper behind Session 3's "the model is 5% of the system" ends on something that is not technical at all. Sculley and colleagues call it cultural debt, and it is the quiet reason the other kinds accumulate.

Teams celebrate accuracy. A new architecture, a percentage point on a benchmark, a launch. What almost nobody celebrates is:

  • deleting a feature that was never earning its place
  • simplifying a model that worked but nobody understood
  • making a result reproducible
  • reducing complexity
  • improving monitoring

Every item on that list is something you ask for, and every one of them is invisible in a status report. If a team is only ever rewarded for the first kind of work, technical debt accumulates silently while everyone looks fast — and it arrives, eighteen months later, as a system nobody can change safely.

Which is a project-management problem wearing an engineering costume. You cannot fix it with a tool. You fix it by what you put on the agenda, what you write in a status report, and whose work you name in front of the sponsor.

You get the engineering culture you reward. If "we deleted three features and can now explain the model" never appears in a status report, nobody will ever do it.

Section 8.3 · Mid-term

60 questions, and the pairs that catch people

The format

What to expect, and how to prepare

The mid-term is 60 multiple-choice questions on the UMEF LMS, worth 50% of the course. Its scope is Sessions 1–4: foundations, planning, resources and risk. The checkpoint quizzes in each session were deliberately written in the same style, which makes your preparation concrete rather than vague:

  • Redo the four checkpoints (1.6, 2.6, 3.6, 4.6). You have seen the explanations once; the second pass is where they stick.
  • Learn the numbered lists. Five roles, four infrastructure layers, five cost categories, four estimation techniques, five risk categories, four mitigation strategies. MCQ exams love a closed list.
  • Practise distinguishing near-neighbours — the section below. Most lost marks come from confusing two concepts, not from ignorance of either.
  • Read the question for what it is asking, not for the topic it mentions. "Which technique estimates the probability of finishing on time?" and "which technique tells you which variable dominates?" both sound like risk analysis and have different answers.

Check the LMS for the definitive exam date, duration and any retake rules — those are set by IDEAS, not by this site.

The trap

Seven pairs worth ten minutes each

These are the distinctions that decide MCQ scores. Say each difference out loud before the exam:

  • Data drift vs concept drift — the inputs changed, versus the input-to-outcome relationship changed.
  • Overfitting vs data leakage — the model memorised the training data, versus information that would not exist at prediction time got into training. Both look like "great in testing, bad in production".
  • Precision vs recall — of what we flagged, how much was real; versus of all that was real, how much we caught.
  • Avoidance vs reduction — change the approach so the risk cannot occur, versus keep the approach and add controls.
  • Functional vs non-functional requirement — what the system does, versus how well it must do it (latency, availability, explainability).
  • Parametric vs analogous estimation — a measurable rate × usage, versus a comparable past project.
  • Monte Carlo vs sensitivity analysis — the probability of hitting a target under combined uncertainty, versus which single variable moves the outcome most.

Section 8.4 · The final

Eight sections and four criteria

The structure

You have already written the first draft of every section

The final assignment is a written case study of your own AI project, worth 50%. Its eight sections map directly onto the session workshops — which is why Compile my project ↗ can assemble your draft for you:

  • §1 Context & the business problem — from S1. The problem in plain language, and the stakeholders.
  • §2 Why AI, and the data reality — from S1. Why not conventional software, and what the data actually looks like.
  • §3 The plan — from S2. Lifecycle, requirements, WBS, methodology and why it fits.
  • §4 Resources & budget — from S3. Team, infrastructure, cost drivers, estimation, contingency.
  • §5 Risk — from S2 and S4. The register, the priority risk, the strategy.
  • §6 Delivery, adoption & monitoring — from S5 and S6. Cadence, MVM, metrics, KPIs, drift, retraining.
  • §7 Responsible AI & governance — from S4 and S7. Bias, compliance, explainability, ownership.
  • §8 Success criteria & reflection — from S1 and S6. How you would know it worked, and what you would do differently.

Use that as the skeleton, then make each section argue rather than report. The order is deliberate: it walks a reader through a decision, which is what a case study is for.

The rubric

Where the marks are — and what each criterion actually rewards

Critical thinking · 30
judgment, trade-offs named, a position defended against the obvious objection
Research · 25
real sources, cited, and used to support a claim rather than decorate one
Application · 25
frameworks applied to your project with specifics — numbers, names, constraints
Structure · 20
clear sections, logical flow, readable prose, consistent referencing

Three practical consequences. First, research is 25% — the four reading companions plus the "Go deeper" links across the sessions are your citable material, and the MIT Sloan cases from Session 6 are strong sources for comparison. Second, application is 25%, which means generic sentences that would fit any project earn nothing; specificity is the mark. Third, structure is only 20% — a beautifully organised description still caps out low, because the other 55% needs a position.

If a paragraph would read identically in someone else's assignment, it is costing you marks under both application and critical thinking.

Section 8.5 · Writing

The same content, twice

Four ways to lose marks

All four are fixable in a second draft

  • Descriptive, not analytical. Explaining what CRISP-DM is instead of what choosing it did to your project. The test: does the sentence contain a judgment?
  • Unsupported claims. "AI will reduce fraud losses significantly." By how much, on what basis, compared to what? Every number needs a source or a stated assumption.
  • Generic, not applied. Sentences that would fit any project in any industry. The test: could you delete your project's name and have the paragraph still make sense? If yes, rewrite it.
  • Disorganised. Risk discussed in four different sections, no clear thread, inconsistent citations. Cheap to fix, and 20% of the mark.
Worked example

A weak paragraph and a strong one, on the same material

Weak

"Data quality is very important in AI projects. Poor data quality leads to poor model performance regardless of algorithm sophistication. Our project will use three years of transaction data. We will ensure data quality through validation and cleaning processes. Data governance will also be implemented according to best practice."

Strong

"Although MekongPay holds roughly 180 million transactions, confirmed-fraud labels exist for only 14 months, and two analysts labelling the same case do not agree. The binding constraint is therefore label quality, not data volume — which means the ≥90% recall target cannot be validated until inter-analyst agreement is measured. I have accordingly moved a double-labelling study into month one and made tranche-two funding conditional on its result. This inverts the sponsor's assumption that the data was the project's strongest asset, and it is the single change most likely to prevent a mid-project reset."

The second version is not longer because it says more things — it is longer because every clause does work. It has specifics (180 million, 14 months, ≥90%), a judgment (label quality is the binding constraint), a consequence (the target cannot be validated yet), a decision (study in month one, funding gate), and a counter-position (it contradicts the sponsor). That is critical thinking and application, visibly earned.

Every paragraph should contain at least one thing that could be wrong. If nothing in it is contestable, it is description.

A method for the second draft

Four passes, an hour each

  • Pass 1 — the argument. Write your recommendation in one sentence at the top. Then check every section supports it. Delete what does not.
  • Pass 2 — specificity. Hunt for generic sentences. Add a number, a name or a constraint to each, or cut it.
  • Pass 3 — the objection. For your main claim, write the strongest counter-argument you can, then answer it in the text. This single move earns critical-thinking marks more reliably than anything else.
  • Pass 4 — evidence. Check every claim has a source, an assumption, or a number. Fix the referencing while you are there.

And a last honest point about how to use tools: an AI assistant is genuinely useful for pass 4 and for structure, and it is actively harmful for pass 3, because the counter-argument has to be yours to be defensible in a viva or a follow-up question. Whatever you submit, you should be able to defend every sentence in it out loud — and that, incidentally, is the accountability principle from Session 4 applied to your own work.

Session 8 · plan the final

Eight questions to finish well

1.Your argument in one sentence — what does your case study actually claim? (Not what it covers.)
2.Your strongest counter-argument — what would a sceptical examiner say against your recommendation, and how do you answer it?
3.Your weakest section (§1–§8) — name it, and say what you will do to fix it.
4.Three sources you will cite, and the specific claim each one supports.
5.One paragraph you will rewrite from generic to specific — paste the generic version here, then rewrite it in your document.
6.Your honest rubric self-assessment — where are you strongest, and where are you losing marks?
7.Your writing schedule — which dates you write on, and when the second draft gets its four passes.
8.Reflection: what will you take from this course into your actual work — and what do you now think you were wrong about before it? One angle worth taking, if you want a stronger answer than most: cultural debt (section 8.2). Name one thing your organisation currently does not reward — deleting a feature, simplifying a model, making a result reproducible, better monitoring — and say what you would change about how work is reported so that it gets done.
Before you submit

A short checklist: the argument is stated in the opening; every section supports it; no paragraph would fit another student's project unchanged; every claim has a number, a source or a stated assumption; the counter-argument appears in the text; referencing is consistent; and you could defend any sentence out loud. Then submit on the LMS by the published deadline.
