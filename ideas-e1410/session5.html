Session 5 · Advanced Project Management in AI

Delivering with agility

Sessions 1–4 built a plan, resourced it and stress-tested it. Now you have to actually run it — with a team whose work is experimental and stakeholders who were promised a system. This session covers why Waterfall struggles with AI, Lean thinking and minimum viable models, Scrum adapted for machine-learning work, and the single most underrated project-management skill in AI: explaining a probabilistic result to someone who wanted a yes. Plan ~50 min for the lesson and its self-check.

Section 5.1 · The hook

"It's 98% accurate." Everyone relaxes.

Your decision

You have to present the fraud model to MekongPay's COO on Monday.

Here is what the model does, measured on real traffic. Fraud is 0.2% of all transactions. The model catches 90% of the fraud, and it wrongly flags 2% of legitimate transactions. Both targets met. Overall accuracy works out at about 98%.

The fraud-operations team will investigate every transaction the model flags. So before you walk into that room, answer the question they will actually ask: out of every 100 transactions the model flags, how many are genuinely fraudulent?

Your estimate
70/ 100
Locked. The answer is about 8. Take 100,000 transactions: 200 are fraud, 99,800 are legitimate. The model catches 90% of the fraud — 180 true alerts. It wrongly flags 2% of the legitimate ones — 1,996 false alerts. So it raises 2,176 alerts, of which 180 are real: precision ≈ 8%. The model is 98% accurate and 92% of its alerts are wrong. Both statements are true, and only one of them describes your operations team's Monday morning — they will open roughly 2,176 investigations to find 180 frauds. Nothing here is a modelling failure. This is what a rare event does to any classifier, and it is why "98% accurate" is not a sentence a project manager should ever say on its own.
Why this session exists

Two things break AI projects at this stage, and neither is technical. The first is process: running experimental work through a plan that assumes predictable output. The second is communication: a technically successful model that nobody trusts, adopts, or correctly understands.

This session is about both — how to run the work (agile, Lean, Scrum) and how to talk about it (metrics, uncertainty, expectations, conflict). They are the same problem seen from two sides: AI produces probabilistic results on an unpredictable schedule, and organisations are built for neither.

The number was never wrong. The sentence around it was. Most of what a PM does on an AI project is build the correct sentence.

Section 5.2 · Agile & Lean

Why sequential planning breaks — and where it survives

The mismatch

Waterfall assumes three things AI projects cannot offer

The Waterfall model runs requirements → design → development → testing → deployment, each phase finishing before the next begins. It works well when requirements are stable, the technology is well understood, and outputs are predictable. AI projects violate all three:

  • Model accuracy cannot be predicted in advance. You cannot commit in a requirements document to a number you will only discover by experiment.
  • Data-quality problems emerge during development, not during planning.
  • Feature engineering requires repeated iterations — the useful inputs are found, not specified.
  • Business objectives evolve as insights emerge — the first model teaches you what you should have asked for.
  • Validation may force redesign of earlier stages, which a sequential plan treats as failure rather than learning.

Push experimental work through a strictly sequential structure and you get the classic three symptoms: delays, rework and scope instability.

The experimentation loop

What the work actually looks like

Machine learning is built by repeating a cycle, where each pass generates the insight that shapes the next:

1 · Data prep
clean, join, structure
2 · Features
engineer the inputs
3 · Train
fit the model
4 · Evaluate
measure honestly
5 · Improve
decide the next experiment

Agile's core principles — iterative cycles, frequent feedback and testing, cross-functional collaboration, continuous improvement, flexibility in responding to change — describe this loop almost exactly. That is not a coincidence of vocabulary; it is why agile fits. And frequent feedback is doing real work here: model performance needs continuous evaluation, stakeholders need to confirm predictions are useful, data problems need to surface early, and bias concerns tend to appear during testing rather than before it.

Lean AI and the minimum viable model

Learn whether it works before you build it properly

Lean thinking maximises value while minimising waste. In AI that means rapid prototyping, early testing on small datasets, eliminating unnecessary complexity, and prioritising the features that deliver business value — so you do not spend six months on a model that was never going to matter.

Its sharpest tool is the minimum viable model (MVM), the AI equivalent of an MVP: basic predictive capability, a limited dataset, a fast build, and real stakeholder testing. The goal is explicitly not a good model. The goal is to find out whether the solution creates value at all — before committing the full budget.

A minimum viable model is a cheap answer to an expensive question. Building one badly on purpose is often the most professional decision available.

The trap

"Agile" is not a licence to stop planning

Most organisations running AI well use a hybrid: sequential where the work is knowable, iterative where it is not. The usual split is problem definition and governance → structured planning; model development → agile experimentation; deployment and integration → structured rollout.

And agile in AI has genuine costs you should name in advance: experimentation timelines are hard to estimate, the work depends on scarce specialists, technical and business teams miscommunicate, accuracy expectations need constant management, and experimental workflows sit awkwardly inside enterprise governance. None of that is solved by a stand-up. It is solved by a project manager acting as facilitator between the technical team and the decision-makers — which is the actual job.

Section 5.3 · Scrum

Sprints when the outcome is unknown

The framework

Scrum was designed for complex, uncertain work — which is convenient

Scrum runs short time-boxed sprints with continuous stakeholder feedback, self-organising teams and incremental delivery of value. Its elements — product backlog, sprint planning, sprint execution, sprint review, sprint retrospective — give an AI team a way to inspect and adapt on a fixed rhythm. Three roles carry it:

Product Owner
maximises value: defines the ML-driven business objectives, prioritises model features, aligns technical work to organisational goals
Scrum Master
facilitates the process and removes obstacles — including the experimentation and infrastructure blockers specific to AI
Development team
data scientists, ML engineers, data engineers, domain experts — collectively delivering increments of model capability

Note what the Scrum Master's job becomes on an AI project: much of the impediment list is GPU quota, data access approvals and environment reproducibility. Unblocking those is not administrative trivia — it is the difference between four experiments this sprint and one.

AI sprints deliver learning, not features

The single biggest adaptation you have to make

In AI projects, sprints focus on learning and model improvement rather than finished software. Typical sprint goals: improve prediction accuracy, test a new algorithm, clean or expand a dataset, evaluate robustness. Typical sprint outputs: experimental results, model prototypes, performance evaluations. A workable internal structure for an AI sprint:

  • 1 · Hypothesis definition — what do we believe, and what would change our mind?
  • 2 · Data preparation — what this experiment needs.
  • 3 · Model training — the run itself.
  • 4 · Evaluation and validation — against the metric agreed up front.
  • 5 · Documentation of results — including the negative ones, which is where most teams fail.

The project-management consequence is uncomfortable and non-negotiable: not every sprint will produce an improved model. Every sprint should produce insight that guides the next iteration. A sprint that rules out an approach has done its job. If your governance model cannot record that as progress, your governance model will quietly push the team into hiding failed experiments.

Judge an AI sprint on validated learning, not code completion. "We disproved our best hypothesis and know why" is a successful sprint.

Backlog & review

Two artefacts that need AI-specific discipline

An AI product backlog holds data-acquisition tasks, feature-engineering improvements, model-architecture experiments, integration work and benchmark evaluations. Unlike a software backlog it contains research tasks and exploratory experiments, which are inherently less predictable. That makes refinement essential: clarify data requirements, define the evaluation metric, identify infrastructure needs, estimate experiment complexity. The rule to enforce — every backlog item carries a clear objective and a measurable success criterion. An item reading "improve the model" is not a backlog item; it is a wish.

Sprint reviews then change character. Instead of "was the feature completed?", stakeholders ask "did the experiment improve the model or generate useful insight?" — evaluated on accuracy, precision and recall, F1, latency. That keeps decisions evidence-based, and it has a side benefit: it trains your stakeholders in the vocabulary of section 5.4, one sprint at a time.

Scrum also handles uncertainty structurally: short cycles limit exposure, rapid experimentation generates evidence, and course correction is cheap. What Scrum cannot do is make progress linear — that part you have to explain, repeatedly.

Section 5.4 · Metrics

Turning statistics into consequences

Four metrics, in business language

Your job is the second column

Technical teams report accuracy, precision, recall, F1 and AUC-ROC. Almost none of that means anything to an operations director. Translation is not simplification — it is stating what the number does to someone:

  • Accuracy — the share of all predictions that were right. Business meaning: nearly useless on its own when the thing you are predicting is rare. Section 5.1 is the proof.
  • Precision — of the cases we flagged, how many were genuinely fraud. Business meaning: how much of your investigation team's time is wasted, and how many innocent customers get blocked.
  • Recall — of all the fraud that happened, how much did we catch. Business meaning: the losses you actually prevent.
  • Latency — how long a prediction takes. Business meaning: whether it can run inside a live payment at all, or only in a nightly batch.

And name the trade-off out loud, because it is the decision your sponsor owns rather than your data scientist: raising recall lowers precision. Catch more fraud and you investigate more innocent transactions. There is no setting where both improve, so someone senior has to say which error is more expensive. That conversation is a project-management deliverable.

Precision and recall are not statistics. They are "how much of my team's day is wasted" and "how much money we lose" — and a sponsor can only choose between them if you say it that way.

Communicating uncertainty

Language that survives being repeated

AI results are probabilistic, and PMs must communicate that clearly without appearing to hedge. Three practices:

  • Careful probability language. Instead of "model accuracy is 92%", say: "on historical data the model predicted the outcome correctly in 92% of cases, which means errors remain possible." Same number, honest frame.
  • Explicit limitations. Say where the model performs poorly: limited or biased data, unusual cases outside the training set, environments that change over time.
  • Scenario-based explanation. "Out of 100 similar cases, the model will misclassify around eight." Concrete, countable, and it survives being repeated to a third party without distortion — which abstract percentages do not.

Frame uncertainty as a managed risk, not a weakness. You have a risk register from Session 4 precisely so that "we don't know yet" comes with "and here is how we're handling it".

The executive structure

Four sentences, in this order

Senior leaders are rarely interested in algorithmic detail. They want the business problem, the value created, and the risks of relying on it. So structure every update the same way: 1 · business objective → 2 · key model insight → 3 · operational impact → 4 · risks and limitations.

Worked example, in one breath: "Churn prediction now identifies 30% of potential churn cases two months earlier, which lets us run targeted retention campaigns; it is less reliable for customers with under six months of history." Objective, insight, impact, limitation. Nothing about the algorithm — the subject is decision support, not model design.

Section 5.5 · Stakeholders

Expectations, ecosystems and honest conflict

The trap

Four ways AI communication reliably breaks

These are not hypothetical failure modes; they are the four you should expect in every project, and they are all caused by technical language landing in a non-technical ear:

  • Accuracy read as certainty — "90% accurate" heard as "right almost every time". Section 5.1 shows how far off that can be.
  • Predictions read as guarantees — a probabilistic estimate treated as a definitive answer, then acted on without a fallback.
  • Technical confidence read as reliability — a "confidence score" heard as proof the model is dependable.
  • Iteration read as failure — repeated experimentation cycles look to an executive like a team that is not making progress.

The implication is a habit, not a technique: anticipate the misunderstanding and clarify meaning before it is needed. If you wait to be asked, you are already correcting a belief rather than forming one.

Expectation management

Three beliefs to correct in week one, not month five

Stakeholders commonly arrive believing that AI will be near-perfect, that it will fully replace human decision-making, and that once deployed it will stay stable indefinitely. Each has a specific correction, and all three are cheaper to make during initiation than after launch:

  • AI augments decision-making rather than replacing it — which is also why the human-in-the-loop control from Session 4 is a feature, not a compromise.
  • Performance changes over time because of drift — Session 4's lifecycle risk, arriving as a communication problem.
  • Models require continuous monitoring and retraining, which means an operating budget after go-live — Session 3's fifth cost category, arriving as a funding conversation.

Everything in this course connects here. Expectation management is not a soft skill bolted on at the end; it is where your technical decisions become organisational reality.

The stakeholder ecosystem

More parties than a traditional IT project, with less shared vocabulary

An AI initiative typically involves business leadership, data science, IT infrastructure, legal and compliance, customers and end users, and regulators or policymakers. Standard tools apply — power–interest matrices, influence mapping, communication planning — with the usual goal: the right information reaching the right stakeholder at the right time.

Two documentation artefacts are worth adopting explicitly, because they make communication durable rather than dependent on you being in the room:

  • Model cards — a short document stating a model's purpose, intended use, performance and limitations.
  • Datasheets for datasets — what the training data is, how it was collected, and what it does not represent.

Add the visual layer — confusion matrices, performance dashboards, trend-monitoring graphs — plus sprint reviews focused on model outcomes and documented decisions and assumptions. Together they buy you traceability, accountability and shared understanding, which is also most of what an auditor asks for.

Conflict is structural, not personal

Four professional groups with genuinely different objectives

Data scientists prioritise model accuracy. Engineers prioritise scalability and reliability. Business leaders prioritise speed and return. Compliance prioritises risk mitigation and fairness. These are all correct priorities, which is exactly why they collide — and why you should stop treating the collision as a personality problem. Four strategies that work:

  • Clarify shared goals — make sure everyone can state the same primary business objective.
  • Decide on evidence — settle arguments with experiments, metrics and pilot results rather than seniority.
  • Facilitate dialogue — get technical experts and business leaders talking directly, with you translating.
  • Manage trade-offs openly — accept that accuracy gains cost money, complexity or time, and make the trade explicit.

Successful AI projects run on balanced compromises, not perfect solutions. Your job is not to win the argument between accuracy and speed — it is to make sure the right person makes that trade knowingly.
