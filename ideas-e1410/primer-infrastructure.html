Foundations · infrastructure primer

Where the model actually runs

Session 3 asks you to choose an infrastructure and say why. This is the detail behind that answer — what the cloud actually is, which services an AI project genuinely touches, what each one costs you, and the five decisions that are yours rather than your engineers'. Written against Microsoft Azure, because it is what most of your organisations run; the last section maps every concept onto AWS and Google Cloud.

FormatReading primer — no video. About 25 minutes.
AssumesNothing technical. If you have never opened a cloud console, start here.
Lands inSession 3 · infrastructure & budget · Session 4 · compliance · Session 6 · monitoring
Counts?Optional. Ticked when done, never counted against you.
Read this first. Cloud vendors rename things and re-price them constantly — Azure Active Directory became Microsoft Entra ID, the ML tooling has been folded into Azure AI Foundry, and every price below is deliberately expressed as a relationship rather than a number. The concepts here are stable and will outlast the names. Never quote a figure from a course page in a real business case — get it from the current Azure pricing calculator, in your region, on the day.
1 · What "the cloud" actually is

Someone else's datacentre, rented by the second, with an API in front of it

That is the whole idea. What makes it different from renting a server in 2005 is that the unit is small and the wait is gone: you ask for eight GPUs at 09:14 and they exist at 09:15, and when you release them the billing stops. Everything else — the service names, the portal, the certifications — is packaging around that.

Three words do most of the work, and getting them wrong is what puts projects in front of a legal team:

RegionA cluster of datacentres in one geography — West Europe, Sweden Central, Southeast Asia. This is where your data physically sits, and therefore whose law applies to it. Not every service exists in every region, and GPU capacity in particular is regional and often scarce.
Availability zonePhysically separate buildings within one region, with independent power, cooling and network. Spreading across zones survives a building failure. It costs a little more and is invisible until the day it isn't.
Region pairAzure pairs each region with another for disaster recovery, usually in the same geography. Relevant when someone asks what happens if West Europe goes down.

And four words describe how everything is organised — this hierarchy is the one thing a project manager should genuinely memorise, because it is the map of who can spend money and who can see data:

TenantYour organisation's identity boundary, run by Microsoft Entra ID. One tenant, everyone's logins.
Management groupA folder of subscriptions, used to apply policy centrally ("no resource may be created outside the EU").
SubscriptionThe billing boundary. One invoice, one set of quotas. Ask early whether your project gets its own — sharing one with three other teams is how cost attribution dies.
Resource groupThe lifecycle boundary. A container for things that live and die together. Delete the group, everything in it goes. This is your exit plan, and it should exist on day one.
On your project → If you take one action from this section: find out which subscription your project bills to, whether it is shared, and what tag identifies your spend on it. If nobody can answer, your budget is not being tracked — it is being averaged.
2 · The services an AI project actually touches

Azure has several hundred services. An AI project of the kind you are planning touches roughly ten. Here they are in the order the data moves, each with what it does, what it replaces, and the question you should ask about it — which is a different question from the one your engineers are asking.

Store it

Azure Blob Storage & Data Lake Storage Gen2

Object storage: a effectively unlimited bucket you put files in, priced per gigabyte-month plus a small charge per operation. Data Lake Storage Gen2 is the same service with a hierarchical namespace turned on — real folders rather than a flat list of names — which makes analytics engines dramatically faster and is what you want for a data lake.

Storage has access tiers: hot (frequent reads, highest storage price, cheapest access), cool, cold, and archive (pennies to store, hours to retrieve and a per-gigabyte retrieval charge). Lifecycle rules move data down the tiers automatically as it ages.

The convention worth adopting is a medallion layout: a raw zone that is never edited, a curated zone of cleaned and conformed data, and a serving zone of the specific tables consumers read. Keeping raw immutable is what lets you re-derive everything when a business rule turns out to be wrong.

Ask: which tier is our training data in, and does anything move it to archive automatically? A retrieval charge on a lake somebody tiered down is a classic surprise invoice.
Move and shape it

Azure Data Factory · Event Hubs · Databricks

Data Factory is the orchestrator: pipelines that copy from a source, transform, and land the result on a schedule or a trigger, with a large library of pre-built connectors to databases and SaaS systems. It is the ETL/ELT workhorse and the thing that breaks when an upstream schema changes.

Event Hubs is the streaming front door — a large, ordered buffer that accepts millions of events and lets consumers read at their own pace. It speaks the Kafka protocol, so a team with Kafka experience is not starting over. Use it when the decision is genuinely real-time; a nightly batch is simpler and adequate far more often than people admit.

Azure Databricks is managed Spark: distributed transformation and feature engineering over data too large for one machine, plus notebooks your data scientists will probably want anyway. It is a first-party service on Azure but a separate product with its own billing unit on top of the VMs.

Ask: what happens to the pipeline when a source system changes a column? If the answer is "it fails and someone notices", you have found the risk for your register — and its early-warning trigger.
Analyse it

Synapse Analytics · Microsoft Fabric

The warehouse layer, where modelled data is queried with SQL by analysts and BI tools. Synapse is the established product; Microsoft Fabric is the newer consolidation — one storage foundation called OneLake with warehousing, engineering, real-time and Power BI on top, sold as capacity units rather than per-service.

For most AI projects this layer matters less than people expect. Models usually train from files in the lake, not from the warehouse. Where it does matter is the business side of monitoring: the KPI that proves the model was worth building normally lives here, not in the ML tooling.

Ask: where will the business metric be computed, and by whom? If the answer is "the data team, eventually", your Session 6 monitoring plan has no business KPI in it.
Train the model

Azure Machine Learning

The workbench. An AzureML workspace holds your datasets, experiments, models and endpoints. Two kinds of compute matter and confusing them is expensive:

Compute instance — a single personal VM for development, attached to one person's notebook. It bills whenever it is running, including overnight, including while its owner is on holiday. Auto-shutdown is a setting, not a default habit.
Compute cluster — a pool for training jobs that autoscales between a minimum and a maximum node count. Set the minimum to zero. The cluster then spins up for a job and disappears afterwards, and the difference between a minimum of zero and a minimum of four is the single largest avoidable line in most AI infrastructure budgets.

GPU families are named by purpose: NC-series for general compute and training, ND-series for large distributed deep learning with the fast interconnect that multi-node training needs, NV-series for visualisation and inference. Bigger is not automatically better — a model that fits comfortably on a mid-range GPU trains no faster on a flagship one, it just costs more per hour.

Spot (low-priority) capacity gives a steep discount on the same hardware in exchange for the right to reclaim it at short notice. For training with checkpointing it is close to free money; for serving a live endpoint it is unusable. Experiment tracking is MLflow-compatible, so runs, parameters and metrics are recorded in a standard format rather than a proprietary one.

Ask: what is the minimum node count on the training cluster, and is auto-shutdown on for every compute instance? Two settings, and together they are frequently a five-figure annual difference.
Serve the predictions

Managed endpoints · AKS · Container Apps

A trained model is a file. Serving is what turns it into something a business system can call. AzureML offers managed online endpoints — an HTTPS address, autoscaling, authentication and logging handled for you — and batch endpoints for scoring large files on a schedule, which is what most projects actually need and far cheaper than a live endpoint nobody calls at night.

Online endpoints support a traffic split across deployments: send 10% of live requests to the new model, watch it, then shift the rest. That is a canary release, and it is the concrete implementation of the validation gate from Session 6 — the thing whose absence makes roughly one retrain in five ship a worse model into production.

Azure Kubernetes Service gives full control and full operational burden. It is the right answer for a platform team running many models with hard latency requirements, and the wrong answer for a first project scoring forty thousand claims a day — that is a managed endpoint, and choosing Kubernetes for it buys months of work and an on-call rota you did not budget.

Ask: does this decision need to be real-time at all? Batch scoring overnight is cheaper, simpler and enough for a surprising share of the projects that are specified as real-time.
If you are using foundation models

Azure OpenAI Service & Azure AI Foundry

Access to large language and multimodal models inside your own Azure tenant, with your network and compliance boundary around them, rather than through a public consumer API. Two purchasing shapes: pay-as-you-go per token, which is elastic and unpredictable, and provisioned throughput, where you reserve dedicated capacity for a fixed monthly price and get consistent latency. Pilots want the first; anything with a latency commitment eventually wants the second.

Most enterprise use is retrieval-augmented generation: your documents are indexed — commonly in Azure AI Search — and relevant passages are passed to the model with the question, so answers are grounded in your content rather than the model's memory. The hard part is almost never the model; it is the quality and permissions of what you retrieve. If a document is in the index, everyone who can query the index can effectively read it.

Ask: whose documents are in the index, and does the retrieval respect the same permissions as the source system? This is the most common serious finding in enterprise GenAI reviews.
Ship it repeatably

MLOps — pipelines, registry, infrastructure as code

AzureML pipelines chain the steps — prepare, train, evaluate, register — so a retrain is one reproducible run rather than someone's afternoon. The model registry versions models with the data and code that produced them, which is what makes "why did it decide that in March?" answerable. Azure DevOps or GitHub Actions automate the promotion from test to production, and Bicep or Terraform define the infrastructure itself as files in a repository.

The project-management point: infrastructure as code is what makes a second environment cheap. If building a staging environment is a two-week manual exercise, nobody will build one, and every test will happen in production.

Ask: can we rebuild this whole environment from a repository? The answer determines whether your disaster recovery plan is real or aspirational.
Control who sees what

Entra ID · managed identities · Key Vault · Private Link

Microsoft Entra ID (the service formerly called Azure Active Directory) is identity. RBAC grants roles on a scope — this group can read this storage account. A managed identity gives a service its own identity so that code authenticates as itself, with no password or key anywhere in the source. Key Vault holds the secrets that genuinely must exist. Private Endpoints put a service on your private network so it is unreachable from the public internet at all.

This is the section that arrives late and hurts. Security review typically appears in month five of a six-month project, discovers that everything talks over public endpoints with shared keys, and the remediation is not a configuration change but an architecture change. Put it in the WBS in week one, as a work package, with the security team named as its owner.

Ask: are we using managed identities or access keys? "Keys, for now" is a real answer, and it is also a dated commitment you should hold someone to.
Know when it breaks

Azure Monitor · Log Analytics · Application Insights

Azure Monitor collects metrics and logs from everything; Log Analytics is where you query them; Application Insights instruments the application itself — latency percentiles, failure rates, dependency calls. Alert rules fire on thresholds and route to a person, a ticket or a channel. AzureML adds model-specific monitoring for data drift and prediction drift on top.

Two things to insist on, both from Session 6: an alert must have a named recipient and a required action, or it is a notification; and the metrics must be computed per segment, not only in aggregate, or a model failing badly for one customer group stays invisible behind a healthy average.

Ask: who is paged at 02:00, and what are they expected to do? If nobody can answer, monitoring exists on paper only.
Keep the bill honest

Tags · budgets · reservations · Azure Policy

Tags are key-value labels on every resource, and they are the only reason anyone can later answer "what did this project cost?". Agree the tag scheme before the first resource exists. Budgets in Cost Management raise an alert at a threshold — they do not stop spending, which surprises people. Azure Policy does enforce: it can refuse to create a resource outside an approved region or without required tags.

On purchasing: pay-as-you-go is the flexible default; reservations and savings plans trade a one- or three-year commitment for a substantial discount on predictable baseline load; spot capacity is the deep discount for interruptible work. A sensible shape is spot for training, pay-as-you-go while the load is unknown, and a reservation once a year of production has shown you the floor.

Ask: what tag identifies our project, and is there a budget alert on it? Both take ten minutes at the start and are effectively impossible to retrofit.
3 · The five decisions that are actually yours

Your engineers will choose the SKUs. These five are project decisions with legal, financial and schedule consequences, and they are routinely made by default because nobody realised they were decisions.

DecisionWhy it is yoursWhat goes wrong
RegionDetermines whose law governs the data, what latency users see, and whether the GPUs you need even exist there.Chosen for GPU availability, discovered by legal in month five. Moving a populated data lake between regions is a project, not a task.
Network isolationPublic endpoints with keys, or private networking with managed identities. It is an architecture choice, not a setting.Deferred to "hardening later". Security review then rejects it and the remediation lands on the critical path.
EnvironmentsHow many, and can they be rebuilt from code. Determines whether testing is real.One environment. Every change is tested in production, and the first bad retrain is discovered by users.
Purchasing modelSpot, pay-as-you-go, reservation. A commercial commitment, so it is signed by someone in your governance chain.Three-year reservation bought before the workload is understood, or on-demand pricing for training that could have run on spot.
ExitWhat happens to data, models and pipelines if this stops — and what it costs to take them elsewhere.Never considered. Egress charges and re-platforming turn a cancellation into a second project.
On your project → Session 3's workbook asks for your infrastructure choice and the deciding constraint. The deciding constraint is almost always one of these five — and naming which one is what turns "cloud, single provider" into an answer that survives a challenge.
4 · How the money actually works

Six meters, and which one dominates

Every Azure charge is one of a small number of shapes. Knowing which shape a service bills in tells you what makes the bill move.

MeterApplies toWhat makes it move
Per GB-monthStorageVolume × tier. Almost always small, and almost always the thing people worry about.
Per operationStorage reads/writes, API callsMillions of tiny files. A badly-shaped lake can cost more in transactions than in storage.
Per vCPU / GPU-hourVMs, clusters, endpointsWall-clock time, not useful work. An idle GPU bills identically to a busy one.
Per GB egressData leaving the region or the cloudOther teams wanting copies. Ingress is free; leaving is not.
Per tokenFoundation modelsPrompt length × traffic. Long retrieved context multiplies quietly.
Per capacity unitFabric, provisioned throughputA fixed monthly floor whether or not anyone uses it.

The pattern from Session 3 holds on every real budget I have seen: people dominate, compute is second, storage is a rounding error, and the line nobody planned for is either labelling or egress. The counter-intuitive one is utilisation — because you pay for wall-clock hours, a data pipeline too slow to keep the GPU fed arrives on the finance report as a compute problem, and gets "solved" by buying a bigger GPU that then idles more expensively.

Put numbers on it → The cost model implements exactly this. Set the accelerator, the utilisation and the pricing mode, and it shows which line dominates your project and what it costs to be wrong about it.
5 · A worked architecture

MekongPay fraud triage, end to end

The case from the sessions, as it would actually be built. Nothing here is exotic; this shape covers a large share of enterprise AI projects.

Transactions arrive

The core banking system publishes to Event Hubs. Ordered, buffered, replayable — if the downstream breaks for an hour, nothing is lost.

Land raw, immutably

A stream job writes to the raw zone of Data Lake Storage Gen2, partitioned by date, never edited. A lifecycle rule tiers anything over 180 days to cool.

Clean and conform

Data Factory orchestrates; Databricks does the heavy joins and feature engineering, writing the curated zone. Data quality tests run here and fail the pipeline loudly rather than passing bad data on.

Train

An AzureML pipeline pulls a versioned dataset onto a GPU compute cluster with minimum nodes zero, on spot capacity, tracks runs in MLflow, and registers the winning model with the exact data version that produced it.

Gate

Offline evaluation against a held-out period, then deployment to a managed online endpoint taking 10% of traffic. Promotion to 100% is a decision, not an automatic consequence of a better offline score.

Serve and integrate

The case-management system calls the endpoint through API Management. Authentication by managed identity; no keys in application code.

Watch

Application Insights for latency and errors, AzureML monitoring for data and prediction drift, segmented by region and merchant category. Alerts route to a named on-call engineer with a documented action.

Contain and account

Everything in one resource group, tagged to the project, inside a subscription with a budget alert. Private endpoints throughout. The exit plan is: delete the resource group.

7 · The same ideas on the other two clouds

Most of your students' organisations are on Azure; some are not, and several are on more than one. The vocabulary changes, the concepts do not — so if your project runs on AWS or Google Cloud, read the row, not the column.

The genuine differences are commercial and organisational rather than technical: what your enterprise agreement already covers, which cloud your identity system lives in, and which one your people have actually operated. For a project manager those three outrank any feature comparison.

8 · Glossary
RegionA geography where your resources physically live. Determines applicable law.
Availability zoneIndependent building within a region. Survives one datacentre failing.
SubscriptionThe billing boundary. One invoice, one quota set.
Resource groupThe lifecycle boundary. Delete it and everything inside goes.
TagA label on a resource. The only way to attribute cost later.
SKUThe specific size and grade of a resource — and therefore its price.
Blob / object storageFiles in a bucket, priced per GB-month plus operations.
Access tierHot, cool, cold, archive. Cheaper to store, more expensive and slower to read.
Hierarchical namespaceReal folders on a lake. Makes analytics engines much faster.
MedallionRaw → curated → serving zones. Raw stays immutable so you can re-derive.
Compute instanceOne person's development VM. Bills while running, including overnight.
Compute clusterAutoscaling pool for jobs. Set the minimum node count to zero.
Spot / low-priorityDeep discount, can be reclaimed. Fine for training, useless for serving.
Reservation1- or 3-year commitment for a discount on predictable load.
Managed identityA service's own identity, so no key ever appears in code.
Private endpointPuts a service on your private network, unreachable from the internet.
RBACRole-based access control — who may do what, on which scope.
Online vs batch endpointLive HTTPS scoring versus scheduled scoring of files.
Traffic splitSending a fraction of live requests to a new model. A canary release.
Model registryVersioned models with the data and code that produced them.
EgressData leaving the region or the cloud. Charged per GB; ingress is free.
TokenThe billing unit for foundation models. Prompt length is a cost driver.
RAGRetrieval-augmented generation — grounding answers in your own documents.
Infrastructure as codeThe environment defined in files, so it can be rebuilt.
