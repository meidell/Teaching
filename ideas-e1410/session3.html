Session 3 · Advanced Project Management in AI

Resourcing AI projects

You have a plan. Now you need the three things that make it real — and all three are scarce: people, infrastructure, and money. This session covers who belongs on an AI team and what each role actually does, the infrastructure a project manager must understand without configuring it, and how to budget work whose cost genuinely cannot be pinned down in advance. Section 3.1 · The hook

"So we need five data scientists?"

Your decision

The COO approves a team of five. You have to say who they are.

MekongPay's COO has signed off: six months, a team of five, cloud budget to be agreed. Your first instinct might be to hire five data scientists and let them build the fraud model. That instinct would sink the project — because the model is the small part.

Before you allocate anyone, an instinct check: in a real production machine-learning system, what share of the whole system is the actual machine-learning code — the model itself, as opposed to everything around it (data plumbing, serving, configuration, monitoring)?

Your estimate
40%
Locked. In the classic study of production ML systems (Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems, Sculley et al., 2015), the ML code is only a tiny fraction — commonly illustrated as around 5% — of a real deployed system. The other ~95% is data collection and verification, feature extraction, configuration, serving infrastructure, resource management, process-management tools and monitoring. That single picture explains this whole session: your people, your infrastructure and your money go mostly into the machinery around the model. A team of five data scientists would build a beautiful model that never runs in production.
Why this session exists

Session 2 gave you a plan. A plan without resources is a wish. This session makes the plan executable along three axes — who does the work (human resources), what they work on (technical infrastructure), and what it costs (budgeting under genuine uncertainty).

Resource the system, not the model. The model is 5% of the machine — staff and fund the other 95% or it never ships.

Section 3.2 · People

The team and who owns what

Five roles, one delivery chain

AI projects are multidisciplinary by necessity, not by fashion

An AI initiative fails the moment one link in this chain is missing. The standard team is:

Data engineer
pipelines, storage, reliable access to data
Data scientist
patterns, algorithms, train & evaluate models
ML engineer
deploy, scale, serve, automate retraining
Domain expert
what the variables mean; validates outputs
PM / product owner
objectives, constraints, stakeholders, vendors

Read that left to right and you have the delivery chain: data arrives, becomes a model, becomes a running service, is judged useful by someone who knows the business, and is steered by someone accountable for scope, time, cost and quality.

The commonest staffing mistake is hiring modellers and forgetting the two roles either side of them — the data engineer who feeds the model and the ML engineer who ships it.

The project manager's own job

You are the translation layer

  • Translate business problems into structured, measurable objectives — "predict the probability of customer churn within 30 days", not "use AI to improve customer service".
  • Coordinate across technical and business teams, who use the same words to mean different things.
  • Manage experimentation and uncertainty rather than a deterministic build.
  • Balance the four constraints — scope, time, cost, quality — knowing that AI quality is a probability, not a pass/fail.

And the business stakeholders have a real job too: they supply operational context, define the decision the model supports, flag regulatory constraints, and judge whether outputs are actually useful. A model can be technically accurate and operationally irrelevant — only the business side can catch that.

The trap

Vendors, and who owns what you built

Most AI projects lean on outsiders: AI consultancies, cloud providers, data providers, specialist development partners. Before signing, a project manager must pin down four things — data ownership and usage rights, intellectual property in the models and algorithms, service-level expectations, and long-term maintenance responsibility. Get these wrong and you discover after go-live that your fraud model, or the labelled data it was trained on, is not legally yours to keep using.

Section 3.3 · Infrastructure

What a PM must understand — and needn't configure

The stack, in project terms

You will never set up the cluster. You will absolutely own its consequences.

Nobody expects a project manager to configure a GPU cluster. But infrastructure decisions drive your timeline, your budget and your risk register, so you must understand what each layer is for:

  • Data storage & pipelines — ingestion, cleaning, feature engineering, data lakes/warehouses, and continuous updates for retraining.
  • Compute — CPUs for general processing and smaller models; GPUs for training deep networks in parallel. Compute is usually your most volatile cost line.
  • Frameworks & development environments — TensorFlow, PyTorch, scikit-learn. Standardising them is what lets a team collaborate and reproduce each other's work.
  • Deployment platforms & MLOps — automated training pipelines, deployment workflows, experiment tracking, performance monitoring, retraining.
  • Version control for models — model versions, the exact datasets used, parameter configurations, evaluation metrics. Without it you cannot reproduce, audit or defend a result.

The three big cloud platforms — AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud — package most of this: scalable compute on demand, managed ML services, integrated storage, prebuilt AI APIs. That converts capital investment into operating expense, which changes how you have to budget.

Why it lands on your desk

Four project consequences of infrastructure choices

  • Timelines & iteration speed — slow training turnaround means fewer experiments per sprint, which means a later date to acceptable accuracy.
  • Dependencies — data availability, compute capacity and tooling gate each other. An idle team waiting for GPU quota is a schedule risk, not a technical detail.
  • Cost & scalability — training costs scale with experimentation; serving costs scale with traffic. Two very different curves in your budget.
  • Integration with existing IT — the classic killer. Models that work in a notebook fail against a fifteen-year-old core banking system.

Infrastructure is not the technical team's private business. It is your schedule, your budget and your integration risk wearing a different hat.

Section 3.4 · Budget

Where the money actually goes

Five cost categories

AI budgets do not look like IT budgets

Traditional IT estimation models routinely underestimate AI costs, because AI spending is driven by data, experimentation and scarce talent rather than by a fixed build. Every AI budget breaks into five areas, and they interact across the lifecycle:

  • Data acquisition & preparation — buying external datasets, collection, labelling and annotation, cleaning, governance and compliance. Usually the most under-budgeted line, and often the largest.
  • Infrastructure & storage — cloud storage, ETL and pipelines, distributed environments, warehouses, security.
  • Compute & model training — GPU/TPU time, training runs, hyperparameter tuning, retraining during development. Scales with how many experiments you run.
  • Personnel & specialised talent — data scientists, ML and data engineers, domain experts, PMs. Because of talent scarcity, often the single biggest share of the budget.
  • Deployment & operations — production infrastructure, monitoring, retraining and updates, integration, and user training and adoption support. This one outlives the project.

Two of the five — data preparation and post-deployment operations — are the ones sponsors forget. They are also the two that decide whether the model keeps working.

The trap

Why the number moves after you commit to it

AI cost uncertainty is structural, not sloppiness. It comes from data availability and quality surprises, model performance variability, experimentation cycles and failed models (you pay for the experiments that didn't work), changing business requirements, and regulatory constraints discovered late. A budget that admits none of this is not a confident budget — it is a fragile one.

So the discipline is: estimate honestly, fund in phases, and track continuously.

Section 3.5 · Estimation

Estimating what you cannot yet know

Four techniques, used together

No single method survives an AI project. Combine them.

  • Analogous estimation — start from a comparable past project. Fast, cheap, and only as good as the analogy.
  • Bottom-up estimation — cost each task in the WBS and sum. Most accurate, most effort, and needs a WBS you trust (you built one in Session 2).
  • Parametric estimation — drive cost from a measurable rate: GPU-hours × price, records labelled × cost per label, engineer-months × rate. Ideal for compute and labelling.
  • Scenario-based estimation — cost a best / expected / worst case for the experimental stages. The honest way to talk about the parts nobody can promise.

Combining techniques improves reliability: parametric for compute and labelling, bottom-up for the engineering, analogous as a sanity check, scenarios for the modelling phase where the truth is a range.

Contingency you can defend

Phase the funding, track the spend

  • Experimentation buffers — explicit contingency on the phases whose duration is genuinely unknown, not a vague percentage smeared across the whole project.
  • Phased funding milestones — release money at checkpoints (data ready → prototype validated → pilot live), so a failing project stops cheaply.
  • Cost tracking dashboards — cloud compute in particular can escalate silently between monthly reports.
  • Budget review at each checkpoint — re-forecast with what you have learned instead of defending the original number.

And strategically: align spend with business value, prioritise high-impact use cases, evaluate cost–benefit trade-offs explicitly, and scale infrastructure gradually rather than buying for the end state on day one.

Phased funding is how you make an uncertain budget safe: the sponsor risks one phase at a time, and you earn the next one with evidence.
