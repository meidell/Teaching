One accuracy number hides two completely different failures, with two different owners and two different costs.

Before you watch

Four boxes, not one number

For a yes/no prediction there are exactly four outcomes, and the matrix lays them out: the model said yes and was right, said yes and was wrong, said no and was right, said no and was wrong.

Accuracy collapses all four into a single figure. That is convenient and it is precisely why it misleads — it lets two very different systems report the same number.

The two errors are not interchangeable

A false negative is fraud you let through: money leaves, and you reimburse. A false positive is a legitimate customer blocked: a support call, an apology, and an analyst's afternoon.

Those land on different budgets and different people. No single number can tell you which one you are buying, which is why the matrix — not the headline — is what you ask for at a stage gate.

What you should be able to say afterwards
Takeaways
  • Ask for four numbers, not one. "Show me the confusion matrix" is the most useful sentence a non-technical PM can say in a model review.
  • Name the owner of each error. False negatives belong to Finance and Risk. False positives belong to Customer Support. They will not agree on the threshold, and that disagreement is a decision for you to broker, not for the data scientist to settle quietly.
  • Accuracy is unusable when classes are imbalanced. If 0.2% of transactions are fraud, a model that approves everything is 99.8% accurate and worth nothing.
On MekongPay → Look at the matrix above. Accuracy is 98.0% — comfortably past what most sponsors would sign off. And yet the fraud team receives 2,176 alerts of which 1,996 are false. The model is not broken. The reporting metric is.
