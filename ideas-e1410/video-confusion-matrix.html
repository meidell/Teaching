Accuracy, precision and recall — and the one number that hides them

Three ways of being right, computed from the same four boxes. An ordinary day of email is enough to show why only one of them usually gets quoted — and why it is the wrong one.

Before you watch

One ordinary day of email

A thousand emails arrive at your company today. 100 of them are spam; the other 900 are real. Your filter moves 80 messages into the spam folder.

Of those 80, 70 really were spam. The other 10 were real emails — and one of them was an invoice from your largest client. Meanwhile 30 spam messages sailed straight into everyone's inbox.

Every judgement the filter made falls into one of four boxes. That grid is the confusion matrix, and it is the only honest picture of what happened:

Filter says SPAM
Filter says FINE
Actually spam
70True positives — spam, correctly quarantined.
30False negatives — spam sitting in your inbox.
Actually fine
10False positives — real mail in the spam folder, including that invoice.
890True negatives — real mail delivered, correctly.

Now the three measures everyone quotes — all three computed from those same four numbers:

  • Accuracy — everything it judged correctly, divided by everything. (70 + 890) / 1,000 = 96%. This is the number that ends up on the slide.
  • Precision — of the messages it quarantined, how many really were spam. 70 / 80 = 87.5%. In plain words: when it says “spam”, how often is it right?
  • Recall — of all the spam that actually arrived, how much did it catch. 70 / 100 = 70%. In plain words: of everything it should have found, how much did it find?

96% accurate. A client invoice is in the spam folder and thirty spam messages are in the inbox. One number, two quite different problems, and neither of them visible.

Same numerator, different denominator

Precision and recall count the same thing on top: the 70 messages the filter got right. Everything that separates them is what sits underneath.

  • Precision divides by what the filter flagged — 80. It is a question about the filter's claims: when it raises its hand, can I trust it?
  • Recall divides by what was really out there — 100. It is a question about coverage: did it find everything?
  • Accuracy divides by everything — 1,000. Which means that when spam is only a tenth of the mail, accuracy is mostly measuring how good the filter is at leaving ordinary email alone. A filter that quarantines nothing at all scores 90%.

That last line is the one to carry into your own project: the rarer the thing you are hunting, the more accuracy flatters you and the less it tells you.

Precision and recall pull against each other. There is no setting of the filter that maximises both — so somebody has to choose, and that somebody is you.

What you should be able to say afterwards
Takeaways
  • Accuracy answers a question nobody asked. When the thing you are looking for is rare, it is dominated by all the easy cases you got right, and it hides both failures.
  • Precision = of what I flagged, how much was real. This is the one that decides whether the people working your alert queue can survive it.
  • Recall = of what was really there, how much did I find. This is the one that decides how much of the problem you are still missing.
  • The two errors have different owners. Missed spam is an IT and security problem; quarantined invoices are a finance and customer problem. They will not agree on the setting, and brokering that is management work, not modelling work.
  • Ask for the matrix, not the headline. “Show me the confusion matrix” is the single most useful sentence a non-technical project manager can say in a model review.
