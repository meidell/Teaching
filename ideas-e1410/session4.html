Session 4 · Advanced Project Management in AI

Managing AI risk

You have a plan and the resources to run it. This session is about everything that can still go wrong — and the uncomfortable truth that most AI projects fail for reasons nobody wrote down early enough. We cover the five risk categories unique to AI, how to analyse and prioritise them, the four mitigation strategies, and the governance and compliance frameworks that increasingly decide whether a model may be deployed at all. Plan ~50 min for the lesson and its self-check.

  • Explain why data is the primary risk driver, and how drift makes AI risk a permanent condition rather than a phase.

Section 4.1 · The hook

The model works. That was never the risk.

Your decision

Two weeks from launch, someone asks a question nobody has answered.

MekongPay's fraud model hits its targets in testing: 91% of fraud caught, 1.8% false alarms, 140 ms. The team wants to ship. Then compliance asks: "When we decline someone's transaction, can you tell them why?" Nobody on the team can. That single question can hold a working model out of production for months.

Regulators have made this expensive. An instinct check before we start: under the EU AI Act, the maximum fine for the most serious violations is up to what percentage of a company's total worldwide annual turnover?

Your estimate
2%
Locked. The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) sets its top penalty at €35 million or 7% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher, for engaging in the prohibited practices in Article 5. Most other breaches — including the obligations placed on high-risk systems — top out at €15 million or 3%. For comparison, GDPR's ceiling is 4%. The number matters less than what it signals: AI risk is no longer only a technical or commercial question. It is a legal one, and it is priced.
Why this session exists

Most AI failures are not caused by a wrong model. They are caused by risks that nobody recognised early enough — data that turned out to be unusable, a model that decayed quietly after go-live, a bias nobody tested for, an explanation nobody could produce. All of those are cheap to handle in month one and brutal in month six.

You will not design the algorithm. But you own the register, the checkpoints and the conversation — which is to say you own whether these risks are found early or late.

In AI projects, risk management is not a document you produce once. It is the discipline of noticing early — and the only part of the job where being pessimistic on schedule is a professional virtue.

Section 4.2 · Identification

Five categories, and why data leads

AI risk vs. the risks you already know

Everything from traditional project risk still applies. Then there is more.

Schedule overruns, budget constraints and integration problems do not disappear because a project is called AI. They are joined by a second family of risks that behave differently — they are probabilistic, data-dependent and they do not stay fixed after delivery:

Traditional
schedule · budget · integration — deterministic, visible, testable
Data-dependent
quality, availability, representativeness, labelling
Probabilistic
model behaviour on inputs it has never seen
Societal
bias, ethics, regulation, reputation, trust

So an AI project combines technical, organisational and societal risk. That is why risk identification here has to be cross-disciplinary: a data scientist will not spot the regulatory exposure, and a lawyer will not spot the data leakage.

The five categories

A checklist you can run in a one-hour workshop

  • Data risks — missing or incomplete datasets, noisy or inconsistent records, incorrect labelling, non-representative samples, too little history. Also access: privacy restrictions, fragmented systems, third-party dependencies, acquisition cost, collection delays.
  • Algorithmic risks — bias, overfitting, poor generalisation to new data, data leakage, over-complex models with unstable predictions.
  • Operational risks — integration with legacy systems, latency and scalability limits, infrastructure and GPU capacity, reproducibility, missing monitoring pipelines.
  • Lifecycle risks — data drift and concept drift, seasonal and behavioural change, gradual performance decay.
  • Regulatory & ethical risks — privacy-law violations, opacity in automated decisions, inability to explain an output, non-compliance with emerging AI regulation.

Data access problems are the great disguised risk of AI projects: they arrive on your desk as a schedule slip, six weeks after they became inevitable.

The trap

The risk that only exists after you succeed

Traditional software does not get worse on its own. An AI system does, because the world it learned from keeps moving. Data drift is when the input distribution changes — new customer segments, a new device mix, a new payment channel. Concept drift is worse: the relationship between inputs and outputs changes, because the thing you are predicting has itself changed. Fraud is the textbook case: the moment your model blocks one technique, adversaries move to another. Your model is not degrading because it was built badly. It is degrading because it worked.

Which is why a launch date is not the end of risk management, and why "who monitors this in month eighteen, and with what budget?" is a question to answer before go-live, not after.

Section 4.3 · Analysis

From a long list to a short one

Two dimensions, two approaches

A register with forty risks and no ranking is a filing exercise

Risk analysis evaluates two things: probability (how likely is it) and impact (how bad if it happens). Two families of method get you there:

Qualitative
expert judgment, categorical scales — probability–impact matrices, risk categorisation, scoring workshops, stakeholder assessments
Quantitative
numerical models — Monte Carlo simulation, sensitivity analysis, expected monetary value, statistical performance analysis
In practice
qualitative early, when uncertainty is high and data is thin; quantitative later, on the risks that survived the first cut

The sequencing matters. Running a Monte Carlo simulation in week two of an AI project is precision theatre — you do not yet have distributions worth sampling. A two-hour scoring workshop with the right five people in the room is worth more.

The probability–impact matrix

The one tool you will use in every project

Score each risk on probability and impact, place it on a grid, and act by band: low — monitor only; moderate — mitigation plan required; high — immediate management attention. Worked examples from an AI project:

  • High impact / high probability — data bias affecting model predictions. This is where your mitigation budget goes first.
  • High impact / low probability — regulatory non-compliance. Rare, survivable-only-once; you plan for it even though it probably won't happen.
  • Low impact / high probability — minor data-pipeline delays. Annoying, constant, and a trap for attention.

The value is not the grid. It is that the grid forces a conversation in which a data scientist and a compliance officer have to agree on a number, out loud, in front of the sponsor.

Quantitative analysis, used where it earns its keep

Three techniques worth knowing by name

  • Monte Carlo simulation — run the schedule or budget thousands of times with uncertain inputs sampled from distributions, and read off the probability of finishing by a given date or cost. This is how you answer "what are our odds of hitting six months?" with a number instead of an opinion.
  • Expected monetary value — probability × financial impact, summed. It converts a risk register into a number a CFO can compare against the mitigation cost.
  • Sensitivity analysis — vary one input at a time and see how much the output moves. In AI it does double duty: it tells you which project variables dominate the outcome, and which data features the model leans on hardest.

That second use is the one PMs underrate. If a credit-risk model turns out to be highly sensitive to reported income, and reported income is the least reliable field in your dataset, you have just found where to spend your data-quality budget. Sensitivity analysis is how you turn "improve data quality" into a specific, defensible task.

Analyse to decide, not to document. If a risk analysis changes nothing about where money, people or attention go, it was not analysis.

Section 4.4 · Mitigation

Four strategies — and where bias gets fixed

Avoid · reduce · transfer · accept

Every risk on your register gets exactly one of these

  • Avoidance — change the scope or approach so the risk cannot occur. In AI, the classic move is choosing a simpler, interpretable model over a high-performing black box, because the explainability risk disappears rather than being managed.
  • Reduction — put controls in place to lower probability or impact: data validation pipelines, held-out test sets, staged rollout, a human in the loop on high-value decisions.
  • Transfer — move the risk to someone better placed to carry it: managed cloud AI services, insurance, contractual service levels with a vendor.
  • Acceptance — acknowledge it and monitor, when mitigation costs more than the exposure. Acceptance is a legitimate decision; silent acceptance is not. Write it down, name an owner, set a review date.

Choosing between them is a constraint problem, not a moral one — you pick based on cost, schedule, capability and appetite. What is not optional is that every high and moderate risk has a named strategy and a named owner.

Bias mitigation has three windows

Before training, during training, after prediction

Algorithmic bias is when a system produces systematically unfair outcomes for particular groups. It comes from historical data reflecting real inequality, imbalanced training sets, proxy variables that stand in for sensitive attributes, feature-selection choices, human labelling habits and algorithm design. You can act at three points in the lifecycle:

Pre-processing
fix the data before training — balance datasets, remove or mask sensitive attributes, generate synthetic data for under-represented groups
In-processing
fix the training — fairness-aware models, constrained optimisation with fairness as an explicit objective
Post-processing
fix the outputs — recalibrate predictions, adjust thresholds across groups

Two warnings worth carrying into a real project. First, removing a sensitive attribute does not remove bias — proxies remain, and postcode has been standing in for ethnicity in credit models for decades. Second, "fairness" is not one thing: several reasonable definitions of it are mathematically incompatible, so which fairness you are optimising for is a stakeholder decision, not a technical default. Getting that decision made, documented and signed is your job.

Bias is not only a technical defect. It is an ethical, legal and reputational risk that happens to be implemented in code — which is why it belongs on the risk register, not just in the model review.

Making it continuous

Mitigation that lives in the delivery rhythm

  • Continuous risk monitoring — the register is revisited on a cadence, not at milestones only.
  • Periodic model performance reviews — before and after go-live, against the thresholds you promised.
  • Stakeholder risk communication — sponsors learn about exposure from you, early, in their language.
  • Governance checkpoints before deployment — an explicit gate, with the authority to say not yet.

In an agile AI cycle — Session 5's territory — risks are reassessed after each iteration, because each iteration produces new evidence about what is actually uncertain.

Section 4.5 · Governance

Who may deploy, and on whose authority

Four principles

The vocabulary every AI governance framework shares

Dozens of frameworks exist; they converge on the same small set of principles. The OECD AI Principles (2019) are the common reference point:

  • Transparency — an AI decision must be understandable to the people affected by it and to those accountable for it.
  • Fairness — avoid bias; outcomes should be equitable across groups.
  • Accountability — a named human is responsible for outcomes. "The model decided" is not an answer.
  • Privacy — personal data is protected, with a lawful basis for its use.

Governance is how those words become project artefacts: an oversight committee with real authority, documented decisions, audit trails for development and deployment, and clear role assignment for outcomes. For a project manager it is mostly unglamorous discipline — write down what you assumed, who approved it, and when.

The regulatory landscape

Risk-based rules, and why classification comes first

  • The EU AI Act — a risk-based framework. Some practices are prohibited outright; a defined set of high-risk uses carry heavy obligations (risk management, data governance, documentation, logging, human oversight, accuracy and robustness); limited-risk systems mainly owe transparency; everything else is largely unregulated. Your first compliance task is therefore not to comply — it is to establish which tier you are in.
  • GDPR — data protection and privacy, plus rights around solely automated decisions that significantly affect people. It applies to your training data as much as to your predictions.
  • Sector regulation — finance and healthcare carry the highest exposure. For MekongPay, the State Bank of Vietnam is the binding authority, alongside Vietnam's personal-data rules (Decree 13/2023).

A worked example of why classification matters: the EU AI Act's high-risk list covers AI used to evaluate creditworthiness — but it explicitly excludes systems used to detect financial fraud. A fraud-detection model like MekongPay's therefore sits outside that particular high-risk category, while remaining fully subject to data-protection law, automated-decision rules and its financial regulator. Two systems in the same bank, built by the same team, can land in different tiers. Read the classification; do not assume it.

Regulation here is moving quickly and differs by jurisdiction — treat this as the map, and verify the current text for any real project.

Early compliance planning is not bureaucracy — it is the cheapest schedule protection available. Compliance discovered late is rework; compliance designed in is a launch date you can defend.

Explainability

XAI is a stakeholder tool before it is a technical one

Explainable AI techniques make model outputs interpretable. SHAP attributes a prediction across its input features; LIME approximates a complex model locally with a simple one to explain a single case; attention visualisations show what a model attended to. They serve four different audiences at once:

  • Engineers — debugging, and catching a model that is right for the wrong reason.
  • Domain experts — sanity-checking whether the drivers make business sense.
  • Regulators and auditors — evidence for how a decision was reached.
  • Executives and customers — trust, and an answer to "why was I declined?"

That last one is where the session started. It is also why explainability belongs in your requirements from Session 2 rather than in a remediation sprint two weeks before launch.

Compliance runs the length of the project

Three phases, three obligations

  • Data acquisition — verify consent, lawful basis and privacy compliance before the data lands in your lake. Retro-fitting consent is usually impossible.
  • Model development — document assumptions, limitations, datasets, parameters and evaluation results. This is what an audit actually asks for.
  • Deployment & operation — monitor adherence to ethical and legal standards, with drift detection and fairness dashboards as standing instruments.

Tooling helps — data-lineage and audit-tracking tools, monitoring platforms with drift and fairness dashboards — but the hard parts are organisational: regulations that keep changing, systems too complex for any one person to hold, and interdisciplinary teams who need to be coordinated. The practices that work: embed ethics early, keep documentation current, establish cross-functional oversight, train the team continuously.

Session 4 · stress-test the project

Seven questions to build the register

1.Your top three risks — one each from three different categories. Name the category for each.
2.Your single highest-priority risk — and where it sits on the probability–impact matrix.
3.Your strategy for that risk — avoid, reduce, transfer or accept — and what you would actually do on Monday morning.
4.Your bias exposure: which group could be treated unfairly, how you would detect it, and at which stage you would fix it (pre-, in- or post-processing)?
5.Your regulatory and compliance constraints — which rules apply, which regulator cares, and what that obliges you to produce.
6.Explainability: who needs to understand a decision your system makes, and how will you give them that?
7.Governance: who owns risk on this project, at which checkpoints is it reviewed, and who has authority to say "not yet" before deployment?
