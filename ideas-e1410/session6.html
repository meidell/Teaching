Session 6 · Advanced Project Management in AI

Keeping it alive

Every previous session led to a launch. This one is about the part nobody budgets for: the operational life of the system. Traditional software is static after deployment; an AI system is not. We cover the KPIs that tell you whether it still works, the three kinds of drift and how to detect them, quality assurance as a continuous governance function rather than a testing phase, and a set of real corporate implementations — what they did, and what a project manager should take from each. Plan ~50 min for the lesson and its self-check.

Section 6.1 · The hook

Who built Toyota's models?

Your decision

Not the data-science team. The factory staff.

At Toyota, factory-floor employees — not a central AI unit — were enabled to build their own machine-learning models for problems they understood better than anyone else. It is a small, unglamorous intervention: give the people closest to the process the tools and the training, then integrate the output into their existing workflow.

Before we go further, put a number on it: roughly how many staff-hours per year did Toyota report saving from this?

Your estimate
2000hrs / yr
Locked. The reported figure is more than 10,000 staff-hours a year (Google Cloud, 2024). Notice what produced it. Not a more sophisticated model, not a bigger GPU budget — but the right people, an integrated workflow, and a system that kept working. That is the argument of this entire session. The technical achievement of the previous five sessions gets you a model that works on the day it launches. What determines whether it still delivers value in month eighteen is monitoring, quality assurance, documentation and ownership — the least glamorous chapter in project management, and the one that decides whether any of the rest mattered.
Why this session exists

Traditional software systems are static after deployment. AI systems are dynamic and continuously evolving — because the data they depend on keeps moving. That single difference creates four problems that land on a project manager's desk after everyone else has declared victory:

  • Data patterns change over time.
  • Model performance degrades in production.
  • Business conditions shift, so the objective itself moves.
  • Ethical and regulatory risks emerge post-deployment, not before it.

Monitoring is what turns AI from a one-time project outcome into a managed operational system. Without it you have not delivered a capability — you have delivered a moment.

Section 6.2 · Monitoring

What you watch, and who is accountable

The activities

Monitoring starts at deployment and never stops

It is not a phase; it runs for the operational life of the system. Five activities make it up:

  • Track model accuracy and performance metrics against the thresholds you committed to.
  • Observe data inputs and distribution patterns — often the earliest warning you get.
  • Monitor system reliability and response times.
  • Identify anomalies and unexpected outcomes.
  • Initiate retraining when the evidence says it is time.

From a project-management perspective, all of this exists to ensure one thing: continued alignment between how the model behaves and what the organisation is trying to achieve. Those two can drift apart even when the model's accuracy is stable, because the business objective moved.

Three KPI categories

A monitoring plan needs all three, or it will mislead you

Model performance
accuracy · precision / recall · F1 · ROC-AUC
Operational
response time · system uptime · prediction latency
Business outcome
revenue impact · cost reduction · user adoption · decision improvement

Most teams instrument the first category thoroughly, the second adequately and the third not at all — which is how you end up with a technically healthy model that nobody uses. User adoption is a monitoring metric, and if you are not watching it you will discover the problem from a budget review rather than from a dashboard.

The project manager's specific obligation here is translation, again: ensuring technical metrics connect to business value. A 3-point recall improvement means nothing to a board until it is expressed as fraud losses prevented.

The trap

Silent failure

The dangerous case is not a model that crashes — you find out about that immediately. It is a model that keeps returning confident, plausible, wrong answers. Nothing alerts. Latency is fine. Uptime is 100%. And the decisions being made on its output are quietly getting worse.

Which is why monitoring compares training performance against production performance, watches for differences between test datasets and live data, looks for unexpected prediction behaviour, and tracks confidence scores over time. A model that performed well in testing can fail silently in production, and silence is not evidence of health.

Your role

Five things the PM owns after go-live

  • Define monitoring KPIs jointly with the technical team — not delegated, not inherited.
  • Establish monitoring schedules and review checkpoints with dates in a calendar.
  • Coordinate data science and operations, who now share a system and rarely a vocabulary.
  • Escalate when performance deteriorates — with a pre-agreed threshold, so escalation is not a judgment call made under pressure.
  • Ensure continued compliance with the ethical and regulatory standards from Session 4.

The tooling is mature and worth knowing by name: MLflow, Amazon SageMaker Model Monitor, Azure Machine Learning monitoring, Google Vertex AI monitoring — offering automated performance tracking, data-drift alerts, experiment tracking and model version control. Tools are cheap. The decision about what constitutes "bad enough to act on" is yours.

Section 6.3 · Drift

Three ways a working model stops working

The three types

Different causes, different detection, different fixes

Model drift is performance degrading over time — driven by changing customer behaviour, economic or environmental shifts, changes in data distribution, and new patterns absent from the training data. It comes in three distinguishable forms, and confusing them leads to the wrong remedy:

  • Data drift — the input distribution changes. Example: your customer demographics shift as you expand into a new region. The relationship you learned may still hold; you are simply seeing inputs you were not trained on.
  • Concept drift — the relationship between inputs and outcomes changes. Example: consumer behaviour during an economic downturn, so the same inputs now imply a different outcome. Retraining on new data is the only real answer.
  • Prediction drift — the outputs shift even though the inputs look stable. Often the first symptom you can actually see, and a reason to investigate rather than a diagnosis in itself.

MekongPay's case is the textbook illustration of the second: a fraud model trained on past transaction patterns becomes less effective as new fraud strategies emerge. Nothing about the model degraded. The world adapted to it.

Detection

Statistical and operational, used together

  • Distribution comparison tests — compare the live input distribution against the training distribution.
  • Population Stability Index (PSI) — a standard, widely used single-number measure of how far a distribution has moved. Common in financial services, which makes it a natural fit for MekongPay.
  • Kullback–Leibler divergence — an information-theoretic measure of the difference between two distributions.
  • Performance-degradation alerts and threshold monitoring — the operational layer: if recall drops below an agreed floor, something happens automatically.

Advanced setups run automated drift-detection pipelines. The project-management point is simpler than the statistics: early detection is what makes retraining a scheduled task rather than a crisis. You do not need to compute a PSI yourself. You do need to have decided, in advance and in writing, what number triggers what action and who is woken up.

The continuous improvement loop

Six steps, running forever

1 · Monitor
performance in production
2 · Detect
drift or anomalies
3 · Collect
new labelled data
4 · Retrain
on the current world
5 · Validate
the updated model
6 · Redeploy
and return to step 1

This is normally implemented through MLOps pipelines, and step 5 is the one teams skip under pressure. A retrained model is a new model: it needs the same validation as the original, and it can be worse. "We retrained it" is not a quality claim.

Budget the loop, staff the loop, schedule the loop. An AI system without a funded improvement loop has a shelf life nobody has told the sponsor about.

Section 6.4 · Quality assurance

QA as governance, not as a testing phase

Why AI QA is different

Everything traditional QA relies on is missing

Traditional software QA
deterministic outputs · rule-based logic · stable behaviour after release
AI system QA
probabilistic outputs · performance depends on training data · behaviour changes with new data · models degrade
The implication
quality must be monitored across the lifecycle, not verified once before deployment
So QA becomes
a continuous project-governance function — planning, risk, governance, post-deployment monitoring

What QA is protecting is broader than accuracy: reliable and stable performance, consistent outputs across datasets and environments, traceability and accountability, regulatory and ethical compliance, and reduced risk of deployment failure and reputational damage. Quality in AI therefore has four dimensions, and a project that measures only the first is not measuring quality:

  • Technical — accuracy, precision/recall, model robustness.
  • Data — completeness, bias detection, consistency.
  • Operational — latency, scalability, reliability.
  • Governance — transparency, explainability, auditability.
The trap

CACE: changing anything changes everything

Here is why AI quality assurance cannot be scoped the way software QA is. In ordinary software, a change is local: you edit one function, you test that function and the things that call it, and the rest of the system is untouched by construction. That guarantee is what makes regression testing affordable.

Machine-learning systems do not offer it. Sculley and colleagues gave the problem a name in the same 2015 paper behind Session 3's "the model is 5% of the system": CACE — Changing Anything Changes Everything. Add one feature and the weights on every other feature shift, because the model re-balances all of them during training. Remove one and the same thing happens. It applies to hyperparameters, sampling strategy and convergence thresholds too — not just inputs.

The consequences are project consequences, not modelling ones:

  • "We only changed one thing" is not a containment argument. It is the sentence people say just before a regression nobody predicted. Every change needs the whole system re-validated, not the changed part.
  • Improving a component can degrade the system. The paper's sharpest example: ensembles work partly because their members' errors are uncorrelated. Make one member better and its errors correlate more with the others — and the ensemble gets worse. Your engineer did good work and the system regressed.
  • The damage is silent and delayed. Effects surface weeks later, in a slice of traffic nobody was watching, far from the change that caused them.

So the defences are the ones this session is about: monitor slice by slice rather than in aggregate, keep a full-system validation gate rather than a component one, and — this is the budgeting point — price re-validation into every change request. When Session 2 told you to document, price and approve a change, this is a large part of what you are pricing.

In software, "we changed one small thing" is a reassurance. In machine learning it is a warning — because there is no such thing as a local change.

Validation

Four strategies, and one that deserves its own paragraph

  • Train–test split — divide the dataset, train on one part, test on the other. The baseline.
  • Holdout validation — an independent dataset reserved for final evaluation and touched once.
  • Cross-validation — multiple training and testing cycles for a more reliable estimate.
  • External validation — testing on a completely new dataset, which is the closest thing to an honest preview of production.

K-fold cross-validation is the workhorse: split the data into K subsets, train on K−1 of them, test on the one left out, repeat K times, average the results. It gives you a more reliable performance estimate, better use of limited data and lower variance in evaluation — which matters enormously when your labelled dataset is small, as it usually is.

It is also your main defence against the two classic failure modes: overfitting (the model memorises the training data and fails on new data) and underfitting (the model is too simple to capture the pattern). The other defences — regularisation, larger and more diverse datasets, feature selection — all cost time or money, which is exactly why these are project risks and not just modelling choices.

Plan validation during project design, not after model development. Retro-fitting a holdout set is impossible once the team has already looked at the data.

A/B testing

Validated on data is not the same as valuable in production

Once a model passes validation, real-world testing begins. An A/B test runs version A (the existing system) against version B (the new AI model) with users randomly assigned, and measures what actually changed: conversion rates, prediction accuracy in the wild, customer engagement, operational efficiency.

This is how you evaluate business impact before full deployment — and it is the honest test, because it compares against the thing you are actually replacing. A model that beats a held-out test set but loses to the existing rules engine has answered the wrong question. For MekongPay, the comparison is the legacy rules engine, run in parallel, on live traffic.

Reproducibility & documentation

The part that makes an audit survivable

A model should produce the same result when retrained under identical conditions. That requires tracking training datasets, preprocessing steps, model parameters, random seeds, software versions and hardware environments. Without reproducibility, quality verification and auditing are simply not possible — you cannot verify a result you cannot recreate.

Since an AI project may run hundreds of experiments, this needs systematic experiment tracking and model versioning: model versions, dataset versions, hyperparameters, performance metrics, training logs. Industry tools: MLflow, Weights & Biases, DVC, Kubeflow. And three documentation artefacts to require by default:

  • Model documentation — purpose, training methodology, performance metrics.
  • Dataset documentation — sources, collection methods, potential biases.
  • System documentation — deployment architecture, monitoring procedures, maintenance plans.

The structured QA sequence that ties this section together: 1 · data validation → 2 · model validation → 3 · deployment testing → 4 · real-world performance evaluation → 5 · continuous monitoring — integrated into project planning, risk management, governance and post-deployment monitoring rather than bolted on at the end.

Section 6.5 · Case studies

What real implementations actually did

Case · Colgate-Palmolive

Generative AI for product concept development

Scope. Generative AI used for product concept development and market research — employees query data, generate copy and imagery, and test concepts digitally.

Approach. The AI was hosted in an internal hub with mandatory training, and framed explicitly as augmenting employee creativity rather than automating it.

Outcomes. Faster, higher-quality concept testing; improved employee productivity and creativity.

Through a PM lens. Scope — a targeted deployment in R&D that deliberately avoided broad automation. Stakeholders — R&D scientists, AI and data teams, leadership. Risks — misuse of AI outputs, adoption resistance. Timeline and budget — a pilot in selected teams, with real investment in tools and training. Lessons — small high-impact pilots, workflow augmentation, and employee engagement as a first-class deliverable.

Source: MIT Sloan Management Review, 2025.

Case · Sanofi

AI-guided investment and project decisions

Scope. AI guides managers in optimising investment and project decisions — supporting decision-making rather than replacing the decision-maker.

Approach. Combines generative and predictive AI for decision support, and — the interesting part — adjusts the hierarchy of decision rights to maximise impact.

Outcomes. Better-informed investment choices and reduced sunk-cost bias.

Through a PM lens. Scope — high-value financial and project decisions. Risks — over-reliance on the AI, and model misalignment with actual objectives. Approach — phased deployment with investment in infrastructure and training. Lessons — human–AI collaboration is the design goal; governance is what makes the output trusted; pilots reduce risk. Note what Sanofi changed beyond the technology: who is allowed to decide what. Most AI projects change a tool and expect an organisational outcome; this one changed the organisation.

Source: MIT Sloan Management Review, 2025.

Case · Liberty Mutual

AI assistance for claims adjusters

Scope. AI assists claims adjusters in triaging calls and resolving inquiries — and rather than issuing a single recommendation, it generates multiple "choice sets".

Approach. The AI informs human decisions and reduces cognitive load; the adjuster still decides.

Outcomes. Improved call-resolution efficiency and reduced employee cognitive load.

Through a PM lens. Scope — operationally focused on claims handling. Risks — employee resistance, systems integration. Approach — pilot with selected teams, then gradual rollout. Lessons — AI should enhance human decision-making, and pilots validate impact before scaling. The design choice worth stealing is the choice set: presenting options rather than an answer keeps the human genuinely in the loop instead of nominally supervising a recommendation they always accept.

Source: MIT Sloan Management Review, 2025.

Case roster

The same pattern, across industries

A wider set of reported implementations, useful as a menu of what AI is actually being deployed to do:

  • Employee productivity & workflow — Toyota: factory staff build ML models, saving 10,000+ staff-hours a year. Uber: summarising communications and surfacing context. Rivian: centralising answers to reduce repetitive internal queries.
  • Data, search & operational intelligence — BMW Group: fleet optimisation and transportation analytics. Domina (Colombia): predicting package returns, improving delivery access by 80%. Teradata: categorising text and voice feedback to extract trends.
  • Customer experience — LUXGEN: an AI agent reducing service workload by around 30%. Alaska Airlines: conversational search for travel planning. Volkswagen of America: the myVW assistant with multimodal guidance. NoBroker: multilingual support handling 25–40% of calls.
  • Creative, marketing & media — Virgin Voyages, Figma, Square Enix, VideoShow: personalised campaigns, brand-approved asset generation, and video from prompts.

AI applied to project management itself

Five places it is already changing your own job

  • Planning & scheduling — predicting task durations, optimising resource allocation.
  • Risk management — flagging potential risks, simulating what-if scenarios.
  • Stakeholder & communication support — generating status reports and dashboards.
  • Knowledge management — summarising meeting notes, maintaining the project repository.
  • Decision support — prioritising tasks and projects, aiding resource trade-offs.

Claimed impact: time saved, fewer human errors, better data-driven decisions. Apply this session's own scepticism — these are tools whose output needs the same validation, monitoring and accountability as any other model. A generated status report is a prediction about your project, not a record of it.

Closing insight

Four lessons that hold across every case above

  • Start small, scale smart — high-impact pilots first, enterprise ambition second.
  • Augment, don't replace — every successful case above kept a human deciding.
  • Governance matters — it is what produces adoption, trust and compliance.
  • Measurable outcomes — ROI, efficiency and productivity are the ultimate success metrics, not model accuracy.

AI success is not about technology. It is about people, processes and governance — and data quality, workflow integration and governance are what determine whether the impact lasts.

Session 6 · build the operating plan

Nine questions to make it survivable

1.Your three KPIs — one model-performance, one operational, one business-outcome.
2.Which type of drift is your biggest threat, why, and how would you detect it?
3.Your alert threshold — the number that trips, and what happens automatically when it does.
