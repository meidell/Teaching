Fall Week 10 · Quantitative Methods I · HEG Genève

The integral adds up a quantity that changes, and the total is an area

When a price or a sales rate stays the same all year, one multiplication gives the total: 10 000 barrels a day × CHF 80 × 365 days. When it changes every day, you have to add up 365 different amounts. This week shows how. You cut the area under the curve into thin rectangles and add them up (the Riemann sum). You get the exact total from an antiderivative (the fundamental theorem). You measure the area between two curves. Then you use these areas to put a franc value on what buyers and sellers gain in a market (consumers' and producers' surplus). The last part reverses the chain rule, so that you can integrate a function inside another function.

The deck's definition

The Riemann sum adds up thin rectangles; its limit is the definite integral.

Riemann sum. Cut the interval [a, b] into n pieces of equal width (b − a) ⁄ n. Over each piece, draw a rectangle whose height is the value of f at the middle of the piece. Add up the n areas, width × height.
Definite integral. The number that the Riemann sum approaches when n grows without limit. a and b are the limits of integration: a is the lower limit, b the upper limit.
∫ba f(x) dx = the limit of the Riemann sum when n → ∞
🔍Each symbol names a part of a rectangle. The sign ∫ is a long S, for "sum": the rectangles are added up. f(x) is the height of one thin rectangle. dx is its width, a very small piece of the x-axis. a and b say where the adding starts and where it stops. So the expression reads: add up height × width for every thin rectangle from a to b. The units follow: a rate in CHF per day, added over days, gives CHF.
On the coffee → If cold brew sells s(t) = 200 + 12t cups a day, t days after the launch, then the month's sales are the area under that line from day 0 to day 30. Exercise 2 computes it, and turns the cups into francs.

Section 10.2 · The fundamental theorem

An antiderivative gives the exact area, without any rectangles

The deck's theorem

If F is an antiderivative of f, the definite integral is F at the upper limit minus F at the lower limit.

An antiderivative of f is a function F whose derivative is f: F′ = f. For f(x) = x², one antiderivative is x³ ⁄ 3, because the derivative of x³ ⁄ 3 is x². The basic rule reverses the power rule: xn → xn+1 ⁄ (n + 1), for every n except −1.

∫ba f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)
🔍The theorem turns an infinite sum into one subtraction. The left side is the limit of the rectangles of section 10.1. The right side needs only two values of F. The recipe has four steps: (1) find F, (2) compute F(b), (3) compute F(a), (4) subtract F(a) from F(b). Any antiderivative works: F + 7 gives (F(b) + 7) − (F(a) + 7), and the 7 cancels.

The deck's properties

Below the axis an area counts as negative, and five rules make integrals easier to compute.

Where f is below the horizontal axis, its values are negative, so its rectangles have a negative height. The integral is therefore the area above the axis minus the area below it. It can be negative, or 0 when the two parts are equal: the deck's ∫1−1 x³ dx = 0.

PropertyWhat it says
∫ab f = −∫ba fSwapping the two limits only changes the sign.
∫aa f = 0From a to a there is no width, so there is no area.
∫ k·f = k·∫ fA constant factor can be moved in front of the integral.
∫ (f ± g) = ∫ f ± ∫ gIntegrate each term of a sum on its own.
∫ca f = ∫ba f + ∫cb fThe area from a to c is the area from a to b plus the area from b to c.
The integral and the area are not always the same numberFor f(x) = x² − 1 on [0, 2], the integral is 8 ⁄ 3 − 2 = 2 ⁄ 3. But the curve is below the axis from 0 to 1 (an area of 2 ⁄ 3) and above it from 1 to 2 (an area of 4 ⁄ 3). The area that is actually enclosed is 2 ⁄ 3 + 4 ⁄ 3 = 2. When a question asks for an area, find where f crosses the axis and integrate each part separately. Exercise 4 asks exactly this.

Section 10.3 · The area between two curves

The area between two curves is the integral of the top curve minus the bottom curve

The deck's rule

Find where the curves cross, decide which is on top, and integrate the difference.

Step 1, the limits. The region ends where the two curves meet. Set f(x) = g(x) and solve.
Step 2, the top curve. Take one x between the two limits and compute both curves there. The larger value belongs to the top curve.
Step 3, the area. Integrate the top curve minus the bottom curve between the limits.
A = ∫ba ( f(x) − g(x) ) dx,  with f on top
🔍The difference f − g is the height of one thin rectangle that stands between the curves. Its bottom is on g, its top is on f, so its height is f(x) − g(x). That height is the same wherever the x-axis is: the rule also works when both curves are below the axis. Top minus bottom always gives a positive height, so the area comes out positive.

Section 10.4 · Consumers' and producers' surplus

The area between a market curve and the price measures what buyers and sellers gain

J
"We sell the house blend at CHF 5 a cup, the price we chose in Week 8. Many of our customers would have paid more than 5. Marketing asks how much value customers keep because the price is 5 for everybody. They want the answer in francs a day."
Jane · Head of Insights, XY Coffee Co.
The deck's definitions

A buyer gains the difference between what they were ready to pay and the price. A seller gains the price minus the lowest price they would have accepted.

Demand curve Pa(q). The highest price at which buyers take q units. It falls when q rises. (a stands for acheteur, the buyer.)
Supply curve Pv(q). The lowest price at which sellers produce q units. It rises when q rises. (v stands for vendeur, the seller.)
Equilibrium (q₀, p₀). The quantity and price where the two curves cross: buyers want exactly what sellers offer.
CS = ∫q₀0 ( Pa(q) − p₀ ) dq
🔍Consumers' surplus (CS) is the total that buyers save because everybody pays the same price p₀. One buyer saves the price they were ready to pay, Pa(q), minus the price they pay, p₀. Adding these savings up for every buyer from the first to the q₀-th gives the area between the demand curve and the price line. The deck also calls it the extra revenue that sellers could have made if they had charged each buyer their own highest price.
PS = ∫q₀0 ( p₀ − Pv(q) ) dq
🔍Producers' surplus (PS) is the total that sellers gain above the lowest price they would have accepted. The seller of the q-th unit receives p₀ and would have accepted Pv(q). The area between the price line and the supply curve adds these gains up. Both surpluses are areas, so both are in francs: price (CHF per unit) × quantity (units).

Section 10.5 · More integration formulas

To integrate a function inside another function, use the chain rule backwards

The deck's three rules

Each rule applies when the integral contains a "block" u(x) and the derivative of that block.

The chain rule says that the derivative of a function of a block is the outside derivative times the derivative of the block, u′. So when an integral shows something of a block, multiplied by u′, you can undo the chain rule. Treat the block like a single letter.

∫ ua · u′ dx = ua+1 ⁄ (a + 1) + C,   a ≠ −1
🔍The power rule for integration. You see a block raised to a power, times the derivative of the block. Raise the power of the block by 1 and divide by the new power. C is any constant: a constant disappears when you differentiate, so every antiderivative can carry one. a = −1 is excluded because you would divide by 0; that case is the logarithm rule.
∫ eu · u′ dx = eu + C
🔍The exponential rule. You see e to the power of a block, times the derivative of the block. The answer is e to the power of the block. It reverses (eu)′ = eu · u′ from Week 5.
∫ u′ ⁄ u dx = ln(u) + C,   u > 0
🔍The logarithm rule. You see a fraction whose numerator is the derivative of its denominator. The answer is ln of the denominator. It reverses (ln u)′ = u′ ⁄ u. When u can be negative, write ln|u|.
The factor in front is the usual difficultyOften the integral contains only part of u′: half of it, or a third. Then multiply by the missing factor's inverse. In ∫(x² + 1) ex³+3x dx, the block x³ + 3x has u′ = 3x² + 3 = 3(x² + 1): the integral has one third of u′, so the answer is ⅓ ex³+3x + C. Check every answer by differentiating it: you must get back the function inside the integral.
