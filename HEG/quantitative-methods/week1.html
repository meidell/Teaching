Section 1.1 · Why numbersslides 6–8

Why numbers drive decisions

J
"Welcome. Before you touch a spreadsheet, understand what I am paying you for. Everyone in this building has an opinion about the price of a bag of beans. You are the one person whose job is to turn that argument into a number the board can act on — and to be honest about how sure you are."
Jane · Head of Insights, XY Coffee Co.
The situation

Four people, four opinions, one price. Nobody can prove anything.

Theo wants to raise the price of the house blend from CHF 4.50 to CHF 5.00. He says customers are loyal and nobody will notice. The café manager says she will lose a third of the morning queue. Marketing says raise it to 5.50 and make it a premium product. The accountant just wants the margin.

All four arguments are plausible. None of them is checkable. That is the entire problem, and it has a shape: they are arguing about a relationship — how does the number of cups sold change when the price changes? — without ever writing that relationship down.

A quantitative method is a way of writing a business relationship down so that it can be argued with.

That is all it is. Not maths for its own sake. The formula is only ever a sentence in a shorter language. "Every 50 centimes on the price costs us about 120 morning cups" is a sentence; q = 1 200 − 240p is the same sentence, written so you can do something with it.

The five steps

Every piece of analysis in this course follows the same loop.

It is worth learning by name in week one, because for the rest of the year you will always be standing at one of these five points — and most of the confusion students feel comes from not knowing which one.

The modelling cycle · click a step
Click each of the five. They must all be visited before the section counts as done.
Model vs data — the distinction Jane will test you on

A model is what you assume. Data is what you observed. Confusing them is the expensive mistake.

Both semesters of this module divide cleanly along this line, and it is worth seeing now:

  • The model half — calculus, financial mathematics, probability, matrices. You write down a relationship and then reason about it: where is profit highest, what is this project worth today, how likely is a bad quarter. The reasoning is exact; the assumption is the risky part.
  • The data half — descriptive statistics and forecasting. You measure what actually happened and summarise it honestly. The measurement is the solid part; the generalisation is the risky part.

A model with no data is a daydream in algebra. Data with no model is a pile of numbers nobody can decide anything from. The job is both, which is why this module teaches both, in that order.

On the coffee → "Every 50 centimes costs us 120 cups" is a model. Last Tuesday's till receipts are data. Until somebody puts the receipts against the model, the price meeting is just four people talking.

Section 1.2 · Reading mathematics

How to read a formula out loud

Before any algebra

Nobody is born knowing what Δ means.

Mathematical notation is a language, and like any language it was invented by people, on purpose, to save writing. It has perhaps thirty words. You are about to meet most of them in one afternoon, which is a lot — and it is the real reason a page of algebra feels impenetrable.

Here is the thing worth knowing on day one: if you cannot say a formula out loud, you cannot think with it. You will stare at it, your eyes will slide off, and you will conclude you are bad at mathematics. You are not. You are being asked to read a sentence in an alphabet nobody taught you.

A formula is a sentence. This section teaches you to pronounce it.

Why this is not a detour → In a meeting nobody will ask you to solve a quadratic. They will put a slide up with R = p × q on it and expect you to say what it means. Reading is the skill that gets used; solving is the skill that gets examined. You need both, and this course is the only place you are handed the first one explicitly.

Why letters at all?

Because the rule has to work for a number you have not been given yet.

This is the question first-year students ask and almost never get answered. Why not just use numbers?

Because a rule is worth more than an answer. "Our café costs CHF 5 400 on a 1 200-cup day" is an answer — one fact, useful once. C(q) = 3 240 + 1.80q is a rule: it answers the same question for every possible day, including the days that have not happened. The letter q is not a mystery to be solved. It is a gap left deliberately open, so that you can fill it with whatever number turns up.

  • Some letters are things you choose or observe — q for quantity, p for price, t for time, n for a count. They are chosen as reminders, not at random.
  • Some letters are fixed numbers you have not been told — the a, b, c in ax² + bx + c. They stand still while x varies. These are called coefficients or parameters, and confusing them with the variable is the single most common source of "I don't know where to start".
  • Some letters are specific numbers with names — e is 2.71828…, exactly as π is 3.14159…. It is not an unknown. It is a number whose decimal expansion is a nuisance to write.
The habit that saves youBefore computing anything, write down what each letter is, with its unit, in the margin: "q = tonnes roasted per day". Ten seconds. It is the difference between doing algebra and doing business — and in an exam it is the difference between a wrong answer and a wrong answer that still earns marks for method.
Three habits that prevent most errors

Not talent. Procedure.

Name every letter first
With its unit. q = tonnes per day, not just "q". Most "I got a weird answer" moments are a units problem wearing a disguise.
Substitute inside brackets — always
To find G(−1) where G(x) = 3 − x², write 3 − (−1)², never 3 − −1². The brackets are not decoration: (−1)² = +1, and without them you will compute 3 − 1 = 2 by luck or 3 + 1 = 4 by accident. You meet exactly this in homework 1.5.
Put the answer back into the original line
You solved 3x + 1 = 5x + 7 and got x = −3? Then check: 3(−3) + 1 = −8 and 5(−3) + 7 = −8. Equal, so you are right. Five seconds, and it catches nearly every sign error you will make this year. Do it in the exam.

First-order equations

One unknown, one power of it, one answer.

Let a, b ∈ ℝ with a ≠ 0. The first-order (or linear) equation is

ax + b = 0  ⟹  x = − b ⁄ a
🗣"ay ex plus bee equals zero, therefore ex equals minus bee over ay." The ⟹ is a one-way arrow: the second line follows from the first.

The condition a ≠ 0 is not pedantry. If a = 0 the x disappears and you are left with b = 0, which is either true for every x or for none — and in a business model that is exactly the signal that the quantity you are solving for has no effect on the thing you are measuring. Worth recognising rather than panicking at.

3x + 1 = 5x + 7the example from the deck
3x − 5x = 7 − 1unknowns left, numbers right
−2x = 6collect
x = −3divide by −2

Two more, for the reflex: 5x − 45 = 0 ⟹ x = 9, and 4x + 7 = 2x − 5 ⟹ 2x = −12 ⟹ x = −6. Always substitute your answer back into the original line — it takes five seconds and catches most sign errors.

Solve it live · drag the coefficients
Solving ax + b = cx + d is finding where two straight lines cross. Set a = c and watch the answer disappear.
On the coffee → "At what volume does the new roaster stop costing us money?" is ax + b = cx + d with the old cost on one side and the new one on the other. Every break-even question in business is this shape.
Two unknowns · systems of linear equations

Two facts, two unknowns. Two ways to unpick them.

One equation with two unknowns has infinitely many answers. Add a second independent equation and the answer is usually pinned to one point. Take the system from the deck:

2x + y = 18  ·  −x + 5y = 2
🗣"two ex plus why equals eighteen; and at the same time minus ex plus five why equals two." The word doing the work is "and at the same time" — that is what makes it a system rather than two separate equations.
Method 1 · elimination
−2x + 10y = 4×2 on the second
11y = 22add the two
y = 2, x = 8back-substitute

Scale one equation so that adding them kills a variable. Fast when the coefficients are friendly.

Method 2 · substitution
x = 5y − 2extract x from the second
2(5y − 2) + y = 18put it into the first
11y = 22 ⟹ y = 2, x = 8same answer

Isolate one variable and push it into the other equation. Better when something already has a coefficient of 1.

The same answer, twice. That is the point of showing both: methods are tools, not rules. Pick the one that makes the arithmetic ugly-free. And there is a third reading — graphically, the solution is where the two lines cross, which is why "simultaneous equations" and "break-even point" turn out to be the same picture.

Section 1.4 · Leveling up II

When the unknown is squared

Why a business ever meets a square

Because revenue is price × quantity, and quantity depends on price.

Raise the price and you earn more per cup but sell fewer. Revenue is the product of the two, so if quantity falls in a straight line with price, revenue is price × (a straight line in price) — and a straight line multiplied by a straight line is a parabola. That is where the squared term comes from, and it is why revenue has a peak rather than rising forever.

On the coffee → Jane's demand estimate for the house blend: q = 900 − 60p cups a day. Then R(p) = p(900 − 60p) = 900p − 60p². Theo's instinct — "charge more, earn more" — is true only on the left half of that curve.
Second-order equations

The discriminant tells you how many answers exist before you find them.

For ax² + bx + c = 0, define the discriminant Δ = b² − 4ac. Then:

  • Δ > 0 — two solutions: x₁ = (−b + √Δ) ⁄ 2a, x₂ = (−b − √Δ) ⁄ 2a.
  • Δ = 0 — one solution: x₁ = −b ⁄ 2a. The curve touches the axis instead of crossing it.
  • Δ < 0 — no solution in ℝ. The curve never reaches the axis at all.

Δ < 0 is not a failure. It is an answer: "the thing you asked for cannot happen."

If you set profit equal to a target and get Δ < 0, the honest report to Jane is "at no price whatsoever does this product hit that target" — and that is far more useful than a number would have been.

Where does b² − 4ac come from?

Nobody invented it out of nowhere. Watch it appear.

This is the formula students most often memorise without ever being shown, and memorising it is exactly why it slips away by November. It takes four minutes to derive, and afterwards you can rebuild it if you forget it.

The problem. We can solve x² = 9 instantly — x = ±3. We cannot solve x² + 6x + 5 = 0 the same way, because x turns up twice and there is nothing to take a root of. So the whole trick is: rewrite it so that x appears only once, inside a square. That move has a name — completing the square.

With numbers first, so you can see it work:

x² + 6x + 5 = 0
x appears twice. Stuck.
(x + 3)² = x² + 6x + 9
Take half of the 6, which is 3, and square the bracket. It gives us the x² + 6x we want — plus an unwanted 9.
x² + 6x = (x + 3)² − 9
So subtract the 9 back off. Now the two x terms are one squared bracket.
(x + 3)² − 9 + 5 = 0  ⟹  (x + 3)² = 4
x appears once. Now we can take a root.
x + 3 = ±2  ⟹  x = −1 or x = −5
Check: (−1)² + 6(−1) + 5 = 0 ✓ and (−5)² + 6(−5) + 5 = 0 ✓

Now the identical moves with letters instead of numbers. Nothing new happens — it is the same five steps, and the formula falls out at the end:

ax² + bx + c = 0
the general case
x² + (b⁄a)x + c⁄a = 0
divide everything by a, so the square term is clean. This is why a ≠ 0 matters — we just divided by it.
(x + b⁄2a)² − b²⁄4a² + c⁄a = 0
half of b⁄a is b⁄2a; square the bracket and subtract the surplus, exactly as with the numbers
(x + b⁄2a)² = (b² − 4ac) ⁄ 4a²
There it is. Put the two right-hand fractions over one denominator and b² − 4ac appears on top, without being asked for.
x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) ⁄ 2a
take the square root of both sides, then move the b⁄2a across

Δ is simply the thing that ends up under the square root. That is the whole story.

And the name → "discriminant" is from the Latin discriminare, to tell apart — the same root as "discriminate". It is the number that tells the three cases apart. Naming it was a convenience, not a discovery.
So why does its sign decide everything?

Because of one fact about square roots, and nothing else.

Look at where Δ sits: under a √, immediately after a ±. Every one of the three cases is a fact about that square root:

  • Δ > 0 — the root is a real, non-zero number. The ± then adds it once and subtracts it once, giving two different answers.
  • Δ = 0 — the root is zero. Adding and subtracting zero does nothing, so the ± collapses and there is one answer.
  • Δ < 0 — you are being asked for the square root of a negative number. There isn't one. Any real number times itself is positive (a negative times a negative is positive), so nothing squared can give −11. No answer.

That last one is worth dwelling on, because students meet it and assume they have made a mistake. They have not. The mathematics is telling them something true: there is no value of x that does what you asked.

Say it as a sentence, not as a shrug"The discriminant is negative, so this target cannot be reached at any price — the cost structure would have to change first." That is a finished piece of analysis. Writing "no solution" and stopping is not, and it is worth about half the marks.
One more thing Δ is quietly telling you

It measures how far apart the two answers are.

Read the formula once more: x = −b⁄2a ± √Δ ⁄ 2a. The two roots sit symmetrically either side of −b⁄2a — which is the vertex — at a distance of √Δ ⁄ 2a each. So the gap between the two answers is

gap = √Δ ⁄ |a|
🗣"the gap equals the square root of delta, over the size of ay" — the bigger the discriminant, the further apart the answers

Check it on the example below: 3x² + 3x − 18 has Δ = 225 and a = 3, so the gap should be 15 ⁄ 3 = 5. The roots are 2 and −3, which are indeed 5 apart. ✓

That makes the three cases one continuous story rather than three rules: as Δ shrinks toward zero the two answers slide together; at zero they collide; below zero they have nothing left to do but leave the real line entirely.

Why an analyst cares → Two prices that both hit a revenue target, five francs apart, is a comfortable position — anywhere between them beats the target. Two prices ten centimes apart is a knife edge you will miss in practice. Δ is a measure of how much room you have, and that is a business fact, not a mathematical one.

Section 1.5 · Functions

What a function actually is

J
"I do not want to know what it cost us to roast forty tonnes. I want a rule that tells me what it will cost to roast any tonnage, including the one we have not tried yet. Give me the rule and I can plan. Give me one number and I can only remember."
Jane · on why a table is not enough
A first case

Advertising money in, sales out.

A small online retailer wants to know whether its marketing works. It records, for twelve months, what it spent on advertising and what it sold. Now it wants a mathematical model of the relationship between advertising spend and sales.

That relationship is a function: a rule that maps inputs to outputs. Here the inputs are the advertising expenses and the outputs are the corresponding sales figures. Once the relationship is written as a function, a question that was unanswerable — "what if we spent CHF 20 000 next month?" — becomes arithmetic.

A function is a machine: one input goes in, at most one output comes out, every single time.

The "at most one" is the whole definition and the only part people get wrong. A rule that returns two different sales figures for the same advertising spend is not a function — it is a contradiction, and a model containing one cannot be used to decide anything.

Three things fall straight out of that table, and none of them is visible in the raw cost column:

  • Producing nothing costs CHF 500. That is the fixed cost: rent and standing charges that do not care whether the roaster runs.
  • The cheapest coffee is not the smallest batch. Average cost falls from CHF 705/t at 20 tonnes to about CHF 528/t at 60 tonnes, then climbs to CHF 1 005/t at 100. There is a sweet spot, and finding it is what the whole calculus block is for.
  • The last tonne is the expensive one. Going from 60 to 61 tonnes adds CHF 776 — far more than the average. Push a machine toward its limit and each extra unit costs more than the last. That "extra cost of one more" is marginal cost, and it is the single most important idea in the fall semester (Week 4).
The formal definition, now that you have seen the point of it

Domain, range, and the two words for the two sides.

A function f : X → Y is a rule that assigns to each element x ∈ X at most one element of Y. The value assigned to x is written f(x). The subset of X for which f(x) is defined is the domain; the set of values of the form f(x) is the range.

C : A → B  ·  q ↦ C(q)  — for the roastery, A = [0; 100] and B = [500; 100 500]
🗣"see is a function from ay to bee; each quantity cue becomes see of cue." Two different arrows: → is about the whole sets, ↦ about one particular input. And [0; 100] has square brackets, so both ends are possible.
  • x is the input, or independent variable — the thing you choose. How much to produce. What to charge.
  • y = f(x) is the output, or dependent variable — the thing that follows. The cost. The revenue.

Which is which is a modelling decision, not a mathematical one, and getting it backwards is a real error: cost depends on quantity, so quantity is the input. Write q(C) and you have claimed that your cost decides your output, which is a strange business to be in.

Finding the domain

Two things break a formula, and one thing breaks a business model.

Mathematically, only a short list of operations can fail. At this level there are two: you cannot divide by zero, and (from Week 2) you cannot take a logarithm or an even root of a negative number. So finding the domain means finding the x that would make the formula misbehave, and excluding exactly those.

f(x) = x ⁄ (x − 2)when is the denominator zero?
x − 2 = 0 ⟹ x = 2the one offender
domain = ℝ \ {2}"all reals except 2"
f(x) = x ⁄ (x² − x − 2)a quadratic denominator — so use §1.4
x² − x − 2 = (x − 2)(x + 1) = 0Δ = 1 + 8 = 9
domain = ℝ \ {2, −1}two offenders this time

Three more for the reflex, and they are exactly the shape the quiz will use: 2x ⁄ (x + 3) ⟹ ℝ \ {−3} · 2x ⁄ (x² − 9x + 14) ⟹ ℝ \ {2, 7} · (x + 5) ⁄ (x² − 5x − 6) ⟹ ℝ \ {6, −1}.

The sign trap that costs the most marksThe denominator x + 3 vanishes at x = −3, not at x = +3. Every year, a large share of the class writes ℝ \ {3}. Set the denominator equal to zero and solve it — never read the sign off the formula.
Domain detective
The business domain is narrower — always

Mathematics allows q = −300 tonnes. Your roastery does not.

The cost function C(q) = 3 240 + 1.80q is mathematically defined for every real q, including negative and absurd ones. But the roastery holds 100 tonnes a day and you cannot produce a negative amount, so the domain that means anything is [0; 100].

Stating that restriction is part of the answer, not decoration. An analyst who reports "profit is maximised at 140 tonnes" for a plant that holds 100 has not made an arithmetic mistake — they have made a modelling mistake, which is worse, because the arithmetic will be right all the way to the board meeting.

Say it like this → "On the feasible range of 0 to 100 tonnes, average cost is lowest at about 60 tonnes." The clause before the comma is what makes the sentence honest.
Section 1.6 · The library

Seven shapes, seven business behaviours

Why bother naming families

Because the shape is the claim you are making about the business.

When you choose to model sales as a straight line, you have claimed that the tenth franc of advertising buys exactly as much as the thousandth. When you choose an exponential, you have claimed growth compounds. When you choose a parabola, you have claimed there is a peak. Nobody argues with your arithmetic; they argue with your shape — and they are right to.

So the library below is not vocabulary to memorise. It is a menu of claims, and knowing what each one commits you to is the difference between modelling and decorating.

The two that need a second look

Absolute value, and functions defined case by case.

The absolute-value function is defined in two pieces:

|x| = x if x ≥ 0  ·  |x| = −x if x < 0
🗣"mod ex is ex, if ex is at least zero; and minus ex, if ex is below zero." ≥ includes the boundary, < excludes it — which is why zero uses the first rule. Simpler reading: |x| is the distance from x to zero.

It behaves differently on ℝ⁺ and ℝ⁻, which is exactly why it is useful: it measures size regardless of direction. A forecast error of −200 cups and one of +200 cups are equally wrong, and |actual − forecast| says so. Average that over a year and you have the mean absolute error, which is how forecasts are graded in industry — and in the spring semester.

A case-defined function (also "piecewise") uses different rules on different parts of the domain. Business is full of them, because business is full of thresholds: volume discounts, tax brackets, overtime rates, shipping tiers, bonus schemes.

f(x) = x if 0 ≤ x < 3  ·  x − 1 if 3 ≤ x < 5  ·  4 if 5 ≤ x < 7
🗣"eff of ex is ex, when ex is from zero up to but not including three; then ex minus one, from three up to but not including five; then just four." Each ≤ includes its end and each < excludes it — that is how the pieces meet without overlapping.

That is the deck's example: its domain is 0 ≤ x ≤ 7, its range 0 ≤ y ≤ 4, and its graph jumps at x = 3 — the open circle marks the value the function does not take.

Drawing it · the Euclidean plane and the rules of a decent chart

A graph that needs the surrounding paragraph to be understood has failed.

Every point is an ordered pair (x, y): go across by x, then up by y. The graph of a function is the set of all points (x, f(x)) — the picture of every input-output pair at once, which is why a graph shows you the pattern a table cannot.

Plot the deck's six points
(3, 4) · (0, 2) · (−2, 1) · (4, 1) · (2, 0) · (−3, −2). Sign errors are the commonest cause of a wrong graph, and the second quadrant is where they show.

In Excel, plotting a function means one of exactly two chart types, and choosing wrongly is a real mistake:

  • Scatter with smoothed lines — for a continuous relationship, where the points between your data points are meaningful.
  • Scatter with straight segments — when you want to be honest that you only know the points you measured and are joining them for readability.

Never a line chart with categories on the x-axis when x is a number: Excel will space your x-values evenly whatever they are, and a chart where 60 and 61 sit as far apart as 80 and 100 is a lie told by a default setting.

Four rules, and marks depend on them all year1. An explicit title that states what is shown. 2. Both axes labelled, with units. 3. Axis minimum and maximum chosen deliberately — a truncated vertical axis exaggerates every difference on the chart. 4. The right chart type. A graph must be understandable without reading the text around it.
Section 1.7 · Exponentials and logarithms

Growth, and how to undo it

J

The first one is an exponential. The second one is a logarithm. You will be asked both every quarter for the rest of your career."
Jane · on the only two questions about growth
Exponential functions

The variable is in the exponent — and that changes everything.

The function f(x) = bx, with b > 0 and b ≠ 1, is the exponential function with base b: expb : ℝ → ℝ⁺, x ↦ bx.

Compare it with the power function xm from the library, and be sure you can tell them apart: in x² the base varies; in 2x the exponent varies. At x = 10 they are 100 and 1 024. At x = 50 they are 2 500 and about 1015. That gap is what "exponential" means in a business memo, and it is why the word gets misused: most things people call exponential are merely fast.

Two conditions, two reasons: b > 0 because (−4)1/2 has no real value; b ≠ 1 because 1x = 1 for every x — a flat line, and no growth to speak of.

Compound interest · where the exponent comes from

The compound amount of a principal P after n years at rate r.

S = P · (1 + r)n
🗣"the sum equals the principal, times, open bracket one plus arr close bracket, to the power en." The exponent is the number of years — and the fact that it IS an exponent, not a multiplier, is the whole difference between compound and simple interest.

The deck's example: CHF 10 000 at 2% a year, three years on.

S(3) = 10 000 · (1.02)³every year multiplies by 1.02
S(3) = CHF 10 612exactly 10 612.08

Where does the exponent come from? Year one gives 10 000 × 1.02. Year two multiplies that by 1.02 again. Multiplying by the same factor n times is raising to the power n. Interest earns interest — and the whole of the spring semester's financial-mathematics block is this one line, rearranged.

The number e

What if the interest compounded not yearly, but constantly?

Take 100% interest for one year and chop the year into n pieces, each paying 1/n. The amount is Sn = (1 + 1/n)n. Push n up and watch: the answer does not run away to infinity. It settles.

🗣"ee is approximately two point seven one eight…" ≃ rather than =, because the decimals never stop and never repeat. e is a specific number with a name, exactly as π is — not an unknown waiting to be solved for.

The natural exponential function — "THE exponential function" — is exp : ℝ → ℝ⁺, x ↦ ex. It is the shape of anything growing continuously rather than in steps, and it is the base every later formula in this course quietly assumes.

A concrete comparison: CHF 10 000 at 2% for three years is CHF 10 612.08 compounded annually, and 10 000 · e0.06 = CHF 10 618.37 compounded continuously. Six francs apart. The number e matters far less for the francs than for the fact that it makes every formula afterwards simpler.

Logarithms · the question asked backwards

"To what power must I raise b to get x?"

y = logb x  ⟺  by = x
🗣"why equals log, base bee, of ex — which is exactly the same as saying — bee to the power why equals ex." ⟺ is a two-way arrow. These are not two facts; they are one fact spelled two ways.

That single line is the definition, and the two forms are the same statement. Everything else about logarithms follows from reading it in whichever direction is convenient. The two fundamental equations say only that the operations undo each other:

logb(bx) = x  ·  blogb x = x
🗣"log base bee of bee to the ex, gives you back ex." Both lines say one thing: the two operations undo each other, like adding and subtracting. A logarithm is not a new kind of number — it is an exponent, read backwards.

1 of 8 · the deck's six conversions, plus two more.

Two bases get their own names because they are used constantly:

  • Common logarithm, base 10: log₁₀ x = log x. It answers "how many digits / how many orders of magnitude". Decibels, pH and the Richter scale are all log₁₀.
  • Natural logarithm, base e: loge x = ln x. It is the one that turns up whenever growth is continuous — which, in finance, is always.

A warning about notation you will meet in the wild: some textbooks and most programming languages write log for the natural logarithm. In this course, and on the exam, log means base 10 and ln means base e. Check the convention before you trust anyone else's formula.

The three rules — and the business question they answer

A logarithm turns multiplication into addition. That is the entire trick.

log(AB) = log A + log B  ·  log(A/B) = log A − log B  ·  log(An) = n · log A
🗣"log of ay bee equals log ay plus log bee." Read the third one slowly — "log of ay to the power en equals en times log ay" — because that is the one that pulls an unknown down out of the exponent, and it is the only reason "when do we get there?" is answerable at all.

The third one is the one that earns its keep, because it pulls an unknown down out of the exponent where you can solve for it. Jane's second question — when do we hit the target? — is exactly that:

1 200 · (1.06)t = 2 400subscribers growing 6% a month
(1.06)t = 2divide by 1 200
t · ln 1.06 = ln 2take ln of both sides, rule three
t = 0.6931 ⁄ 0.0583 ≈ 11.9 monthsjust under a year

Exponential: "where will we be in a year?" Logarithm: "when will we get there?" The second question is the one management actually asks.

Quick sanity check you can do without a calculator — the rule of 72: money at r% doubles in roughly 72/r years. At 6% that gives 12, and the exact answer above is 11.9. At 2% it gives 36, against an exact 35.0. Close enough to catch a typo, never close enough to put in a report.
