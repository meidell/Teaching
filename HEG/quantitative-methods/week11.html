Fall Week 11 · Quantitative Methods I · HEG Genève

Before you compute anything, ask where the numbers come from

The first ten weeks assumed the numbers: a cost function, a demand curve, a price. The second half of the semester asks how such numbers are obtained, and that question decides whether an analysis is worth anything. This week gives the vocabulary. A population is every unit you care about; a sample is the part you actually measure. A number computed on the population is a parameter; the same number computed on the sample is a statistic. Data is qualitative or quantitative, it is observed at one moment (cross-sectional) or over time (time series), and it is measured at one of four levels, which decide which summaries are allowed. Then: how data is collected (a census, a survey, an experiment, an observational study), where bias comes from, and the four ways of drawing a sample that let you say how precise your result is.

Section 11.1 · Data and its types

Business statistics turns data into information, and the first step is to know what kind of data you hold

J
"The app now records every purchase: the café, the time, the amount, the payment method, and once a month a satisfaction rating from 1 to 5. Finance wants a report. Before you open a spreadsheet, tell me what kind of number each column is. Last year someone averaged the postcodes."
Jane · Head of Insights, XY Coffee Co.
The deck's definition

Data, tools, information, decision: the goal of business statistics is to convert data into meaningful information, and then to draw conclusions and make predictions.

Data2 million till receipts
→
Toolsaverages, charts, comparisons
→
Information · DecisionSaturday sales are 30 % higher: add staff

The deck's example: a retailer holds two million till receipts (data). With averages, charts and comparisons (tools) it finds that Saturday sales are 30 % higher than weekday sales (information). It adds staff on Saturdays (decision). When it uses the same pattern to forecast next month's staffing, it is making a prediction, the second goal.

The deck's two distinctions

A variable is qualitative or quantitative; a dataset is cross-sectional or a time series.

Qualitative (categorical). Describes a quality, a category or a group: marital status, eye colour, payment method. Its subtypes are nominal (no order: blood type) and ordinal (ordered: satisfaction low ⁄ medium ⁄ high).
Quantitative (numerical). A measurable quantity you can add and average: height, age, income. Its subtypes are discrete (counted: number of children, defects per hour) and continuous (measured on a scale: weight, voltage).
Time series. One unit observed over time, in order: the daily CHF ⁄ EUR rate in 2025, Atlanta's sales 2003–2006.
Cross-sectional. Many units observed at one moment: the sales of four cities in 2003, the salaries of all HEG staff on 1 January 2026.
Quick test: can you meaningfully average or sum it? Yes → numerical. No → categorical.
🔍Why the test works. A sum only means something when the numbers measure amounts of the same thing. Two postcodes added give nothing; two amounts in francs added give an amount in francs. The test has one trap, which the deck names: people average satisfaction scores from 1 to 5. The score is ordered, but the gap from 1 to 2 need not equal the gap from 4 to 5, so the median is the safer summary. Section 11.2 makes this precise.
A number is not always quantitativePostcodes (1302), phone numbers, student IDs and jersey numbers are written with digits, but they are labels. The average postcode of a class means nothing. The variable's type depends on what you choose to record: the result of two coin tosses (HH, HT, TH, TT) is qualitative, the number of heads (0, 1, 2) is quantitative and discrete. The deck's rule: measuring is usually better, because you can always group numbers into categories later, but you cannot get the numbers back from the categories.
On the coffee → Jane's columns: the café is qualitative nominal; the time of purchase is quantitative continuous; the amount in CHF is quantitative continuous (recorded in steps of CHF 0.05, but treated as continuous); the payment method (cash ⁄ card ⁄ TWINT) is qualitative nominal; the rating 1–5 is qualitative ordinal. The amounts of one customer, month by month, are a time series; the amounts of all customers on one day are cross-sectional.

Section 11.2 · Levels of measurement

The level at which a variable is measured decides which summaries are allowed

The deck's four levels

Nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio: each level can do everything the levels below it can do, plus one thing more.

LevelWhat you can doExampleAllowed summaries
NominalSay equal or not equal. The categories have no order.ID numbers, gender, blood type, payment methodcount, percentage, mode
OrdinalPlus order: say which is larger. The gaps between categories are not equal.age range 25–34, rating 1 to 5, low ⁄ medium ⁄ highplus median, percentiles
IntervalPlus differences: the gaps mean the same everywhere, but there is no true zero.temperature in °C, a calendar yearplus mean, standard deviation
RatioPlus ratios: a true zero, so "twice as much" makes sense.salary, sales, weight, waiting timeeverything, including ratios and percentages of a total
Interval or ratio? Ask: is there a true zero, where 0 means "none of it"?
🔍The deck's test. 0 °C does not mean "no temperature", so 20 °C is not twice as warm as 10 °C: temperature in °C is interval. CHF 0 means no salary, and CHF 8 000 is really twice CHF 4 000: salary is ratio. The printed slide lists "temperature" beside ratio ⁄ interval data; the annotation says which: interval. For the exams, the two levels are treated together as "ratio ⁄ interval", the highest level, and the difference matters only when a question asks for it.
Why Jane's rating cannot be averaged with a clear conscienceThe satisfaction rating 1 to 5 is ordinal: 1 is worse than 2, but nobody knows whether the step from 1 to 2 is as large as the step from 4 to 5. The mean treats the steps as equal. Many companies average such ratings anyway; the honest summary is the median, or the share of customers who gave 4 or 5. The exams ask for the level; the level tells you what is legal.

Section 11.3 · Population and sample

A statistic is computed on the sample you have; the parameter you want belongs to the population you cannot see

J
"Marketing wants the average amount a customer spends per visit. We have 400 regular customers across the four cafés. Asking all of them takes a week. Can you ask forty and tell me the answer? And how wrong can forty be?"
Jane · Head of Insights, XY Coffee Co.
The deck's four words

Population, sample, parameter, statistic.

Population. The set of all items or individuals of interest: all likely voters, all parts produced today, all sales receipts for November, XY Coffee's 400 regular customers.
Sample. A subset of the population selected for analysis: 1 000 voters interviewed, a few parts for destructive testing, every 100th receipt audited, 40 of the 400 customers.
Parameter. A summary measure of the population: the true average spend of all 400, written μ (mu). It is fixed, but usually unknown.
Statistic. The same summary computed on the sample: the average spend of the 40, written x̄ (x-bar). It is known, but it changes from sample to sample.
Parameter ↔ Population  ·  Statistic ↔ Sample  ·  Greek letters (μ, σ, p) for the population, Latin letters (x̄, s, p̂) for the sample
🔍The deck's memory aid: the first letters match. P-arameter goes with P-opulation, S-tatistic with S-ample. The letters matter in every formula from now on: when you see μ, the sentence is about the whole population; when you see x̄, it is about the sample on your desk. Inference (section 11.5) is the step from the Latin letter you know to the Greek letter you want.
Descriptive statistics describes the data you have: "the 120 students in this course scored 4.6 on average."
Inferential statistics draws a conclusion about a larger group: "based on 120 randomly chosen HEG students, the average of all HEG students is probably between 4.4 and 4.8." The deck's remark: the size of the dataset is not the difference. The difference is whether you only describe what you observed, or generalise from it, with uncertainty.

Section 11.4 · Collecting data

Four ways to collect data, six steps to a survey, and the eight places where bias gets in

The deck's four methods

The objective is to collect accurate and reliable data. There is no one best method.

MethodWhat it isCostThe deck's example
CensusA survey of the whole population.Very expensive and slow when the population is large; "complete view" does not mean "no error".The Swiss population census.
Sample surveyA fraction of the population is asked.Cheaper, less accurate; bias can be an issue.A poll of 1 000 voters.
ExperimentA controlled study: the researcher decides who gets which treatment.Very costly, but the only method that can show cause and effect.An A ⁄ B test where we decide who sees price A or price B.
Observational studyLike an experiment, but nobody is assigned: the groups chose themselves.Cheap; correlation is not causation.Comparing shops that chose to open on Sundays with shops that did not.

The deck's table of survey methods adds four more lines: a phone survey is cheap and timely but has a poor reputation and closed questions (today many people do not answer unknown numbers, and the ones who do may differ from the ones who do not); a written or online survey is cheap and allows open questions but gets a low response rate (often only the very happy and the very unhappy reply, like online reviews); direct observation shows behaviour, not opinions, and two observers may count differently; a personal interview can ask "why?" but the interviewer's tone changes the answers.

The deck's steps for a survey

Define the issue, define the population, develop the questions, pre-test, choose the sample size and method, then select the sample and ask.

The deck's example: a cafeteria survey at HEG · six moves
Step through all six moves.
Two bad questions from the deck"Do you agree with most other reasonably minded people that the city should spend more money on neighborhood parks?" is leading: it pushes people to agree. "What is your opinion on Australian wines on a scale from 1 to 10?" is unclear: what do 1 and 10 mean, and which wines? Closed questions with clear answer options are easier to analyse, and a pre-test with ten people catches a confusing question before you collect 500 useless answers.
The deck's bias wheel

Eight sources of bias, each a different way for the data to misrepresent the population.

BiasWhat goes wrongAn XY Coffee example
SelectionThe sample does not represent the population.A survey only through the app misses the customers who pay cash.
Non-responseThe people who do not answer differ from those who do.Of 400 emails, 60 reply, mostly the regulars who like the place.
MeasurementThe instrument or the question distorts the answer."Don't you agree our new blend is better?" · a till that rounds every amount down.
Interviewer ⁄ ObserverThe person collecting the data influences it by tone, expectation or presence.The barista asks the rating out loud, in front of the queue.
Data accuracyErrors when typing or copying the data.1 000 entered instead of 100 for one day's sales.
Internal validityCan we trust the cause-and-effect conclusion inside the study?Sales rose after the new sign went up, and also after the weather improved.
External validityDo the results hold outside the study, for other people, places and times?What worked at the station café in December may not hold at the campus café in July.
The deck's lesson → The Literary Digest had 2.4 million answers and was wrong; Gallup had a few thousand, drawn properly, and was right. A huge sample does not fix bias.

Section 11.5 · Sampling methods

Only a random sample lets you say how precise your result is, and there are four ways to draw one

The deck's two families

Nonstatistical sampling: the researcher chooses. Statistical sampling: chance chooses, with known probabilities.

Convenience sampling. Whoever is easy to reach: the students in this classroom, asked about the spending of all Swiss young people. Easy, not representative.
Judgement sampling. The researcher picks "typical" cases: a manager chooses five typical shops to test a product.
Ratio (quota) sampling. Quotas that match the population, 50 % women and 50 % men, but who fills each quota is still chosen by convenience.
Statistical sampling. Each item of the population has a known chance of being chosen: each of the 2 000 customers in the database has a 5 % chance. Only then can you compute how precise the result is, the margin of error.
Random methodHowWhenWatch out
1 · Simple randomEvery possible sample of size n has the same chance. Number the population, add a column of =RAND(), sort, take the first n (without replacement); =RANDBETWEEN repeated n times samples with replacement.A list of the whole population exists and it is homogeneous enough.By chance a small group (exchange students) can be missing.
2 · StratifiedDivide the population into strata (gender, income level, programme) that are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive; draw a simple random sample inside each; combine them in proportion.The groups differ from each other and every group must be in the sample.You need to know each unit's stratum beforehand.
3 · SystematicOrder the list, compute the step k = N ⁄ n, draw a random start between 1 and k, then take every k-th item.A long ordered list, a production line.Periodicity: every 7th day of daily sales is always the same weekday.
4 · ClusterThe population is already in natural groups (airports, buildings, classes). Draw some whole clusters at random and measure everyone in them.Travelling to every unit is impossible: 100 airports out of 5 200.Cheaper, usually less precise; an ideal cluster is a mini-population, heterogeneous inside.
Stratified → a sample from every group (groups similar inside)  ·  Cluster → some whole groups (each group mixed inside)
🔍The pair that is confused in every exam. Both methods cut the population into groups. Stratified sampling takes a few units from each group, so that every group is represented. Cluster sampling takes all the units of a few groups, because visiting a few groups is cheap. Ask: do I sample inside every group (stratified) or do I pick whole groups (cluster)?
Careful with the letters in systematic samplingThe deck's example: N = 30 people, n = 3, so the step is k = N ⁄ n = 30 ⁄ 3 = 10. The random number, here 4, is the starting point, chosen between 1 and k; the sample is 4, 14, 24. The printed slide calls the random number "k"; it is the random start, and k is the step.

The deck's last slides: from the statistic to the parameter

Inference draws a conclusion about the population from the sample, with a margin of error.

The deck's example: in a random sample of 1 000 Geneva households, 400 use mobile banking. The sample statistic is p̂ = 400 ⁄ 1 000 = 40 %, known. We infer that in the whole population about 40 % ± 3 % of households use it. The ± is the margin of error:

margin of error ≈ 1.96 × √( p̂ (1 − p̂) ⁄ n ) = 1.96 × √(0.40 × 0.60 ⁄ 1 000) ≈ 0.030
🔍Read the formula from the inside out. p̂(1 − p̂) is largest when p̂ = 0.5, so uncertain questions need larger samples. Dividing by n and taking the root means the margin shrinks with the square root of n: four times the sample halves the margin. The 1.96 gives 95 % confidence, the usual convention. The true proportion p stays unknown; the sample gives a likely range for it. This formula returns in the spring semester with its full derivation; this week you only need to compute it.
