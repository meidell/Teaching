Fall Week 4 · Quantitative Methods I · HEG Genève

What does one more cost us?

Week 1 found the cost of the 61st tonne by subtracting two rows of a table. That only works when somebody hands you the table. Week 3 read a rate off a straight line, and the rate was the same everywhere. But the roastery's cost is a curve: every extra thousand doses costs more than the one before, and "the rate" depends on where you ask. The derivative is how you answer "how fast is this changing, right here?" from the formula, at any point, exactly — and that number is what almost every business decision turns on.

This week builds it in three moves. A secant through two points gives an average rate. Slide the second point toward the first and the secant becomes a tangent: the rate at one point. Then a short table of derivatives and three rules let you find that rate without ever taking a limit again. The second half of the deck is a parenthesis — solving equations where the unknown sits in an exponent or inside a log, and differentiating those functions — because next week's rules and half the homework need it.

Section 4.1 · Sensitivity

From the secant to the tangent — the rate at one point

The question, as the deck asks it

How to estimate sensitivity?

The sensitivity of business metrics refers to how changes in certain variables impact key metrics or indicators. The deck's own four examples:

  • how changes in employee satisfaction levels or compensation packages impact turnover rates;
  • how changes in the number of job postings on different platforms affect the number of qualified applicants;
  • how changes in advertising expenditure affect sales revenue;
  • how to determine the production level that maximizes its profit.
🔍Every one of those is the same shape of question: if I move this by a little, how much does that move? In Week 3 the answer was a slope, and a line has one slope. But none of these relationships is a line — more advertising brings in less and less extra revenue, more output costs more and more per unit — so the answer depends on where you are. That is the whole reason this week exists: a slope that changes from point to point needs a new definition, and the new definition is the derivative.

Last week: how fast does one thing change when I change another — along a line? This week: the same question, along a curve, at exactly one point.

The slope of a function at a point

The slope of the function is the slope of the tangent line. How can we construct the tangent line?

The deck's answer, in two steps. First, the secant: the slope of f(x) at x₁ is "approximated" by the slope of the line going through the two points (x₁, f(x₁)) and (x₂, f(x₂)):

slope = ( f(x₂) − f(x₁) ) ⁄ ( x₂ − x₁ )
🔍This is Week 3's slope formula, unchanged — rise over run between two points. What is new is the word "approximated": on a curve the two-point slope depends on which second point you pick, so it is not the slope, it is an average rate over the stretch between the two points. At the roastery, the secant from 60 to 100 thousand doses says "CHF 1 000 per thousand on average over that stretch" — true, and not what Theo asked.

Second, let the second point slide toward the first:

slope = limx₂ → x₁ ( f(x₂) − f(x₁) ) ⁄ ( x₂ − x₁ )
🔍Read "lim" as "the value the fraction settles on as the gap closes". You cannot put x₂ = x₁ in directly — that is 0 ⁄ 0, which is not a number — but you can bring x₂ as close as you like and watch what the slope does. If it settles on a number, that number is the slope at the point, and the secant has become the tangent. The widget below does exactly this, with the roastery's cost curve, and you can watch the number stop moving.

Section 4.2 · The derivative

A limit, a slope, and a function — and the table you will use all year

Definition — for the sake of completeness

The derivative of a function f is the function denoted f′ and defined by

f′(x) = limh → 0 ( f(x + h) − f(x) ) ⁄ h

provided that the limit exists. If f′(a) can be found, f is said to be differentiable at a, and f′(a) is called the derivative of f at a. The process of finding the derivative is called differentiation.

🔍It is section 4.1's limit with the letters renamed: x₁ is now x, and the second point is x + h, so the run is just h. The top is the change in the output; the bottom is the change in the input; h → 0 closes the gap. Three things to hold on to. First, f′ is a function — it hands you the slope at every x, not just one. Second, "provided that the limit exists" is a real condition: a curve with a corner or a jump has no tangent there, and no derivative. Third, the deck says "for the sake of completeness" because you will compute derivatives from a table, not from this limit — but you should see the limit work once, below, so the table is not magic.

Notation — five ways of writing the same thing: f′(x), (df ⁄ dx)(x), df(x) ⁄ dx, (d ⁄ dx) f(x), y′.

🔍The prime says "the rate of this"; f′ is the rate of f. d ⁄ dx says "the rate of … with respect to x" — it names the input, which matters when a cost depends on both output and time and you must say which one you are moving. The d is not a number and df ⁄ dx is not a fraction, though it is written like one and behaves like one often enough that the notation survived three centuries. y′ is the shorthand for when the function has been called y. Pick whichever the question uses, and never mix f′ with the wrong letter — f′(q) is a rate per unit of q, and nothing else.

Because the derivative gives the slope of the tangent line, f′(a) is the slope of the line tangent to the graph of y = f(x) at (a, f(a)).

Try it · from the definition, once

f(x) = x². Take the limit by hand, one move at a time.

This is the derivation the deck leaves to the room on slides 11 and 12. Do it once properly and the power rule in the table stops being a rule you were told and becomes one you have seen.

(f(x + h) − f(x)) ⁄ h for f(x) = x² · four moves
Step through all four.
Why you cannot just put h = 0 inBefore the algebra, the fraction is (0) ⁄ (0) at h = 0 — not a number. The algebra's job is to cancel the h in the denominator, so that what is left (2x + h) is a perfectly ordinary expression you can put h = 0 into. Every derivation from the definition has this shape: expand, cancel, then let h go. If you take the limit before cancelling, you get 0 ⁄ 0 and nothing else.

Section 4.3 · Tangent lines and the rules

The slope at a point, the rules for sums, and the line itself

Finding the slope of a tangent line

Find the slope of the tangent line to the curve y = f(x) = x² at the point (1, 1).

f′(x) = 2x  — from the table, or from the derivation in section 4.2
mtan = f′(1) = 2
🔍Two steps, always in this order: differentiate first, then substitute the point. Substituting first gives f(1) = 1, a number, whose derivative is 0 — the wrong answer and a common one. The deck's second version asks for the slope at a general point (x, f(x)) and the answer is just mtan = f′(x) = 2x: the derivative is the slope at every point at once, and "find the slope at (1, 1)" is "read f′ at x = 1".

On the coffee → At 450 cups the tangent is horizontal: R′(450) = 9 − 9 = 0. That is Week 3's vertex, −b ⁄ 2a = 450, found a second way — a parabola peaks exactly where its derivative is zero, and in Week 8 that sentence becomes the whole method for maximising anything. Left of 450 the slope is positive (one more cup still adds revenue); right of it, negative. The derivative does not just give the peak; it tells you which side of it you are on.
Rules for differentiation

Three rules, and together they differentiate every polynomial.

1. Derivative of a constant  (d ⁄ dx)(c) = 0
2. Constant factor rule  (d ⁄ dx)(c · f(x)) = c · f′(x)
3. Sum or Difference rule  (d ⁄ dx)(f(x) ± g(x)) = f′(x) ± g′(x)
🔍A constant has no rate — the rent does not react, and neither does √5 or ln 2, however exotic it looks. A constant factor rides along: five times a function changes five times as fast. A sum changes at the sum of the rates, so you may differentiate term by term. What the three rules do not say is anything about a product or a quotient of two functions of x — x · ex, x ⁄ ex — and that is next week. This week, if two x's are multiplied together, expand first (homework 4.3 l) or divide through (4.3 m, n) until it is a sum of powers.

The deck's first example, f(x) = 6x³ − 2x² + 7x − 8, one move at a time:

Differentiate 6x³ − 2x² + 7x − 8 · four moves
Step through all four.

And the second, where the rules meet the table's fractional and negative exponents — f(x) = x⁴ ⁄ 4 − 5 ⁄ x1⁄3:

(d ⁄ dx)( x⁴ ⁄ 4 ) − (d ⁄ dx)( 5 ⁄ x1⁄3 ) = (1 ⁄ 4)(4x³) − 5·( −(1 ⁄ 3) x−4⁄3 )
= x³ + (5 ⁄ 3) x−4⁄3
🔍Rewrite before you differentiate: 5 ⁄ x1⁄3 is 5 x−1⁄3, a constant times a power, and the power rule gives −(1 ⁄ 3) x−4⁄3. Two signs meet — the minus of the subtraction and the minus of the exponent — and they make a plus. The ⁄4 in the first term is a constant factor and simply waits: ¼ × 4x³ = x³. Homework 4.3 is fourteen of these, and the rewrite is the step most of them turn on.
Finding an equation of a tangent line

Strategy — given f(x), first we find f′(x), then evaluate it at the point, then write the line in point-slope form.

The deck's example: f(x) = 2x² + 2x + 3, tangent at (1, 7).

f′(x) = 4x + 2,  so the slope at (1, 7) is f′(1) = 6.
y − 7 = 6(x − 1),  that is  y = 6x + 1
🔍The derivative supplies one number — the slope — and then it is Week 3: a point and a slope make a line, in point-slope form. Check that the point is on the curve first: f(1) = 2 + 2 + 3 = 7 ✓. If a question gives you only the x, compute f(x) yourself to get the point. Homework 4.2 is this with y = 4 ⁄ (x + 1) at (3, 1): the derivative is −4 ⁄ (x + 1)² — the reciprocal row of the table, with x + 1 in place of x, because shifting a graph sideways changes no slope — so the slope is −¼ and the line is y = −x ⁄ 4 + 7 ⁄ 4.

Section 4.4 · Rates of change

Marginal cost, marginal revenue — and relative and percentage rates

Three definitions

The rate, the relative rate, and the percentage rate of change of f(x).

The rate of change of f(x) is  f′(x)
The relative rate of change of f(x) is  f′(x) ⁄ f(x)
The percentage rate of change of f(x) is  ( f′(x) ⁄ f(x) ) · 100
🔍The rate is in the units of f per unit of x: francs per thousand doses. The relative rate divides by the current size, so it is a pure number per unit of x — "the function is growing at 3.3% of itself per thousand doses" — and the percentage rate is that number times 100. Why three? Because "profit is rising by CHF 200 per thousand doses" means something different at a profit of 6 000 (3.3%, healthy) and at a profit of 600 000 (0.03%, flat). The relative rate is what lets you compare a small business with a large one, and it is what a growth rate is. Homework 4.6 asks for all three of the same function, in order.
Marginal cost — the derivative is the business idea

What does the next thousand doses cost? C′(q), read at the current output.

The roastery: C(q) = 10 000 + 200q + 5q². By the rules of section 4.3, C′(q) = 200 + 10q, and at today's 60 thousand doses:

C′(60) = 200 + 600 = 800  francs per thousand doses — the marginal cost at 60
🔍Compare it with two other numbers at the same output. The average cost, C(60) ⁄ 60 = 40 000 ⁄ 60 = 666.67, is what each thousand doses has cost on average so far. The subtraction, C(61) − C(60) = 805, is Week 1's method and is off by 5 — the curve bending inside the one-unit gap. Marginal cost is above average cost here, which means the next unit drags the average up: Week 1 noticed this in the table (776 against 528) and could not explain it. Now it is a sentence: the average rises whenever the marginal is above it. Week 5 proves that with the quotient rule.

Section 4.5 · Parenthesis · Exponential and logarithmic equations

Solving for an exponent, and for what sits inside a log

Board summary — everything in this section comes from these lines

A log is a question: "b to what power gives x?"

Definition. logb x = y  ⟺  by = x  (x > 0).  log₂ 8 = 3 because 2³ = 8.  ln is base e ≈ 2.718.
Laws. log(MN) = log M + log N,  log(M ⁄ N) = log M − log N,  log Mk = k log M
Change of base. logb u = ln u ⁄ ln b
Consequences. logb 1 = 0 (b⁰ = 1); logb b = 1 (b¹ = b), so ln e = 1. Domain: x must be positive — no power of 2 gives −8 or 0, so log₂(−8) does not exist.
🔍Read the definition both ways. Left to right: log₂ 8 = 3 since 2³ = 8. Right to left: if 4x−1 = 7 ⁄ 3 then x − 1 = log₄(7 ⁄ 3) — and that step is the whole of the next example. Each law is an exponent rule read through the definition: with p = log_b M and q = log_b N, MN = bp bq = bp+q, so log_b(MN) = p + q. Check: log₂(8 · 4) = log₂ 32 = 5 = 3 + 2. Layman: ln x counts, roughly, how many times you multiply by 2.718 to reach x. Multiplying always adds the same amount to the count — so logs turn multiplication into addition and powers into multiplication. And the slope 1 ⁄ x says each extra unit of x matters less the further out you are. Bases: ln is base e; calculators and Excel's LOG are base 10; Python's math.log and R's log are ln. Say which you mean.

A logarithmic equation involves the logarithm of an expression containing an unknown. An exponential equation has the unknown appearing in an exponent.

Try it · an exponential equation, one move at a time

Solve 5 + 3 · 4x−1 = 12.

Plan before solving. The unknown sits in an exponent. There is no log law for a sum, so taking a log of 5 + 3 · 4x−1 directly gets nowhere. Isolate the power first, with ordinary algebra; then read off the exponent with the definition; then change the base so a calculator can evaluate it.

5 + 3 · 4^(x−1) = 12 · five moves
Step through all five.
🔍Alternative for the middle step: take ln of both sides and use the power law, (x − 1) ln 4 = ln(7 ⁄ 3), then divide by ln 4. Same answer, no log₄ needed — and this is the route to use when the base is awkward. Check: 40.6112 = e0.6112 × 1.3863 = e0.8473 = 2.333 = 7 ⁄ 3, and 5 + 3 × 2.333 = 12 ✓.
The deck's finance case — how many periods?

In finance, the capitalisation formula reads Cn = C₀ · (1 + i)ⁿ.

where i is the interest rate for one period, n the number of time periods, C₀ the initial value and Cn the value after n periods. Problem: find the number of time periods, if the rate, the initial and final values are known.

C₀ · (1 + i)n = Cn  →  (1 + i)n = Cn ⁄ C₀  — divide by C₀ so the power stands alone
ln( (1 + i)n ) = ln( Cn ⁄ C₀ )  — apply ln to both sides; the equation stays true
n · ln(1 + i) = ln( Cn ⁄ C₀ )  — power law: n is now a factor, not an exponent
n = ln( Cn ⁄ C₀ ) ⁄ ln(1 + i)  — divide by the number ln(1 + i)
🔍Same situation as the previous example: n is in the exponent, so isolate the power, take ln, bring n down, divide. ln is chosen because it is the function that turns an exponent into a multiplier — any log would do, and ln is the one on the calculator. Layman: "how many years of compounding to get from C₀ to Cn?" — a log counts how many times the factor (1 + i) was applied. In Excel: =LN(1.5)/LN(1.04), or =NPER(4%,0,-10000,15000).
n = ln(Cₙ ⁄ C₀) ⁄ ln(1 + i) · open all four
0 of 4 opened — open all four to complete this half of the section.
On the coffee → XY Coffee puts CHF 50 000 aside at 4% for the next roaster, which will cost CHF 75 000. n = ln 1.5 ⁄ ln 1.04 = 0.4055 ⁄ 0.0392 ≈ 10.3 years. The "rule of 72" — divide 72 by the rate in percent — gives a doubling time of about 18 years at 4% and 10.3 at 7%; it is this formula, rounded, and it is worth having in your head for a meeting. The exact version is on your calculator in two keystrokes.

(1) Collect all logs on one side; (2) merge them into a single log with the product law; (3) remove the log with the definition; (4) solve the quadratic; (5) check each root in the original equation — a log of a negative number does not exist, so one root will have to be thrown away.

This is the same discipline that rejected the negative price in Week 3's coffee market and the phantom root in homework 3.20 — and it decides quiz questions 5 and 6 and homework 4.9, 4.11, 4.12 d) and 4.15 a).

Section 4.6 · Differentiating logs and exponentials

Rewrite first, then u′ ⁄ u — and always ask where the x is

Rewriting logarithmic functions before differentiating

Differentiate f(x) = ln( (2x + 5)³ ).

Plan. Two rules do the whole job: the power law ln(Mk) = k ln M and the derivative rule (ln u)′ = u′ ⁄ u — derivative of the inside over the inside. Rewrite first: pull the exponent 3 out front so you differentiate ln(2x + 5), not ln of a cube.

f(x) = 3 · ln(2x + 5)  — power law, the exponent comes out as a factor
f′(x) = 3 · ( 1 ⁄ (2x + 5) ) · 2 = 6 ⁄ (2x + 5)  — u = 2x + 5, u′ = 2; the 3 rides along
🔍Without the rewrite you still get there: u = (2x + 5)³, u′ = 3(2x + 5)² · 2, and u′ ⁄ u = 6(2x + 5)² ⁄ (2x + 5)³ = 6 ⁄ (2x + 5). Same answer, three times the algebra — the rewrite is a convenience, not a different rule. Domain: 2x + 5 > 0, i.e. x > −2.5; a derivative only exists where the function does. And the "· 2" is the chain rule in its smallest form — next week makes it general.
Try it · a log of a product of powers

Differentiate f(x) = ln( (x + 1)² · (x + 2)³ · (x + 3)⁴ ).

Plan. Do not touch the product rule: first split with the product law ln(ABC) = ln A + ln B + ln C, then pull each exponent out with the power law. You are left with three simple terms, each differentiated as constant × u′ ⁄ u with u′ = 1.

Procedure to differentiate logb u, and the two exponential examples

Convert logb u to ln(u) ⁄ ln(b), and then differentiate.

Where the strategy comes from. Let y = logb u. By definition by = u. Take ln of both sides: ln(by) = ln u. Power law: y ln b = ln u. Divide: y = ln u ⁄ ln b. Check: log₂ 8 = 3 and ln 8 ⁄ ln 2 = 2.0794 ⁄ 0.6931 = 3.000.

f(x) = log₂ x:  f′(x) = (d ⁄ dx)( ln(x) ⁄ ln(2) ) = (1 ⁄ ln 2) · (d ⁄ dx)(ln x) = 1 ⁄ (ln(2) · x)
🔍ln 2 ≈ 0.693 is a constant, so ln x ⁄ ln 2 = (1 ⁄ ln 2) · ln x and the constant-factor rule applies: only ln x is differentiated, giving 1 ⁄ x. Same move as 5x³. Why convert at all: the only log with a differentiation rule is ln, and ln b is a number, so log_b u is just ln u times the constant 1 ⁄ ln b. General: (log_b x)′ = 1 ⁄ (x ln b); for b = e it collapses to 1 ⁄ x. Check at x = 4: 1 ⁄ (4 × 0.693) = 0.361; the secant (log₂ 4.01 − 2) ⁄ 0.01 = 0.360 ✓ — the widget below does this for you.

And the two exponential examples:

f(x) = 3 ex  →  f′(x) = 3 ex  — constant-factor rule only; ex is its own derivative
f(x) = x ⁄ ex  →  f′(x) = (1 · ex − x · ex) ⁄ e2x = (1 − x) ⁄ ex
🔍The second is a quotient, and the quotient rule is Week 5 — the deck shows the result so you have seen the shape. The alternative is to rewrite x ⁄ ex = x e−x and use the product rule, also Week 5: 1 · e−x + x · (−e−x) = e−x(1 − x), the same thing. What the result says: f′ = 0 at x = 1, so x ⁄ ex rises, peaks at x = 1 (value 1 ⁄ e ≈ 0.37), then decays to 0 — a "rise then fade" shape, which is what a product launch looks like. You will meet it again.

Try it · the deck's last example

f(x) = e² + ex + xe + 2√x + ln(2). Before differentiating: where is x in each term?

Three possibilities, and each has its own rule. No x at all → a constant, however exotic it looks → derivative 0. x in the base, constant exponent → power rule. Constant base, x in the exponent → exponential rule, (au)′ = au ln a · u′. Sort the six terms below.

Constant · Power rule · Exponential rule
f′(x) = ex + e · xe−1 + e√x · ln 2 · ln(2) · ( 1 ⁄ (2√x) ) = ex + e · xe−1 + ( 2√x · ln(2) ) ⁄ ( 2√x )
🔍Term by term: (e²)′ = 0 (e² ≈ 7.39 is a number); (ex)′ = ex; (xe)′ = e xe−1 (power rule — the exponent e ≈ 2.718 is a constant, so it is just x to a power); (2√x)′: rewrite 2√x = e√x ln 2 — the change-of-base trick in the other direction — with inside u = √x ln 2 and u′ = ln 2 ⁄ (2√x), so the derivative is 2√x · ln 2 ⁄ (2√x); (ln 2)′ = 0. Two of the five terms were constants wearing a disguise. Asking "where is x?" before touching a term is the habit that gets the mixed exam question right.
