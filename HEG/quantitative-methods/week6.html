Section 6.1 · From text to equations · the advertising dilemma

A paragraph of prose is two equations waiting to be written

T
"The Board wants proof that we can hit our target of 1,200 bag sales for the launch campaign while staying within our strict 110,000 CHF promotional budget. If we can't show a clear, optimized path to profitability, they will table the launch."
Theo · junior brand manager, Luxury Coffee Co. — the Week 6 review case
The case's data, as printed

Two channels, one budget, one target.

  • Social Media Campaigns: each comprehensive social media ad package costs exactly 4,000 CHF to produce and run. Historical conversion data shows that each package drives approximately 800 website visits to the online boutique, with a conversion rate of 5% making a purchase.
  • High-End Coffee Shops Events: each package costs 5,000 CHF to set up and operate. These exclusive events generate 500 physical visitors per package, but boast a much higher conversion rate of 12% due to tasting experiences.

Theo had been allocated a total marketing budget of 110,000 CHF and was mandated by the Board to secure exactly 1,200 box conversions during the promotional window.

(1) Find the number of social media campaign packages to run, and the number of events to host to hit the budget and conversion targets precisely.

Try it · translate, one sentence at a time

From the paragraph to the system — five moves.

Plan. Before any algebra: what does the Board choose? Those are the unknowns. Then every sentence that contains a number is either a rate (CHF per package, boxes per package) or a total to hit. A rate times an unknown, summed and set equal to a total, is an equation.

Question (1) · from prose to (x, y)
Step through all five.
4 000x + 5 000y = 110 000   ·   40x + 60y = 1 200
🔍Each equation is one sentence of the case, and each side carries a unit. The first is in francs: francs per package times packages, plus francs per event times events, equals the francs the Board allows. The second is in boxes. A check worth making every time: if the two sides of an equation are not in the same unit, the equation is wrong before any number is put in. The 800 visits and the 5% never appear on their own — they meet first, as 40 boxes per package, and only that product enters the system.
Try it · question (2) — cut the budget

Slide the budget. Watch where the two lines cross — and when the crossing leaves the map.

The conversion target stays at 1 200 boxes: the navy line 40x + 60y = 1 200 never moves. The red line is the budget, 4 000x + 5 000y = B. The plan is where they cross. A plan only exists in the shaded corner, where neither number is negative.

budget B · the plan (x, y) · does it exist?
Try at least four budgets, including 110 000 and 90 000, to complete this half.
On the coffee → "Impossible" has a number behind it. An event sells 60 boxes for 5 000 francs — CHF 83.33 a box; a social package sells 40 for 4 000 — CHF 100 a box. So 1 200 boxes cost at least 1 200 × 83.33 = CHF 100 000 (all events, no social) and at most CHF 120 000 (all social). Any budget between the two is a plan; any budget outside is not. At 90 000 the algebra still returns an answer, (x, y) = (−15, 30): thirty events — 150 000 francs of them — paid for by "un-running" fifteen social packages. The mathematics is fine. The business is not.
A negative answer is not an arithmetic errorWhen a system returns x = −15, the temptation is to look for the mistake. If the check works — 4(−15) + 5(30) = 90 and 4(−15) + 6(30) = 120 — there is no mistake: the model is telling you that no real plan satisfies both sentences. That is what question (2) asks you to show. Write the solution, then the sentence: "a number of campaigns cannot be negative, so the target is out of reach at this budget."

🔍The quadratic formula, with the discriminant Δ = b² − 4ac under the root. Compute Δ first: it says how many answers there are before you look for them — two if positive, one if zero, none if negative (a square root of a negative number is not a real number). For the case's profit, a = −0.4, b = 80, c = −1 500: Δ = 6 400 − 2 400 = 4 000, √Δ = 63.25, so two break-evens, (−80 ± 63.25) ⁄ (−0.8) = 20.94 and 179.06. The ± is the two answers; the 2a underneath divides both. Week 1 derived it by completing the square.
Try it · the sweet spot

New Geneva Roast: demand p = 100 − 0.4q, fixed cost 1 500 CHF a day, 20 CHF a box. Slide the daily output.

Revenue R(q) = (100 − 0.4q)q (navy), cost C(q) = 1 500 + 20q (red), and profit P = R − C = −0.4q² + 80q − 1 500 (green). Questions (3) to (10) of the case are all on this one picture.

price · revenue · cost · profit · average cost — at q boxes a day
Try at least five outputs, including 100, to complete this half.
On the coffee → Profit is a downward parabola, so the three numbers the Board wants all come from Week 3. Break-even where P = 0: about 21 and 179 boxes a day. The peak at the vertex, q = −b ⁄ 2a = −80 ⁄ (−0.8) = 100, where profit is CHF 2 500 a day and the price that sells exactly 100 boxes is 100 − 40 = CHF 60. Why two break-evens? Below 21 boxes the fixed 1 500 is not covered. Above 179, selling that many boxes needs a price so low that it falls under the cost of a box: at 179.06 boxes the price is CHF 28.38 and the average cost 20 + 1 500 ⁄ 179.06 is CHF 28.38 too. Past that point every box sold at the price needed to sell it loses money.
Try it · x in the exponent, one move at a time

The case's sales projection is S(t) = 1 000e0.04t. When do cumulative sales reach 2 000 units?

Plan. The unknown is in an exponent. Get the exponential alone on one side, then take the natural logarithm of both sides — ln undoes e.

From 1 000e0.04t = 2 000 to t · four moves
Step through all four.
Isolate first, then take logsTaking ln of 1 000e0.04t directly is allowed — ln 1 000 + 0.04t, by the product law — but it is one more law to get right. Taking ln of a sum is not allowed at all: ln(500 + et) is not ln 500 + t. Divide by the 1 000 first, and the log meets nothing but the exponential.

Section 6.3 · Computing derivatives · the espresso, the growth, the ad budget

Any function, including complicated ones — name its shape first

The rules, by the shape that calls for them

Every derivative in the case is one of these — or several, nested.

The function is…RuleIn the case
a sum of powers(xn)′ = n xn−1, term by termQ12: −0.01q³ + 1.05q² + 90q − 500 → −0.03q² + 2.1q + 90
a product(fg)′ = f′g + fg′Q3: R = p(q) · q → R′ = p + q · p′ = 100 − 0.8q
a quotient(f ⁄ g)′ = (f′g − fg′) ⁄ g²Q9: C̄ = C(q) ⁄ q → −1 500 ⁄ q², always negative
e to a function(eu)′ = eu · u′Q14: 1 000e0.04t → 40e0.04t
ln of a function(ln u)′ = u′ ⁄ unot in the case — Week 4 · §4.6
a function of a function(f(g(x)))′ = f′(g(x)) · g′(x)Q16: R(V(a)) → R′(V) · V′(a)
P′(q) = 0  and  P″(q) < 0  ⇒  a local maximum at q
🔍Two derivatives, two questions. P′ = 0 finds the outputs where profit stops rising or stops falling — the flat spots, called critical points. P″ says which way the curve bends there: negative means it bends downward, so the flat spot is a top. The case states the test in question (13). In Week 5 it was "rising faster or rising slower"; here it decides between a peak and a valley. Week 8 makes it the method for every optimisation problem.
Try it · the espresso

Price-taker at 115 CHF a bag, cost C(q) = 0.01q³ − 1.05q² + 25q + 500. Find the flat spot, then ask which way it bends.

Profit is P(q) = 115q − C(q) = −0.01q³ + 1.05q² + 90q − 500. The red line is its tangent at the chosen output; its slope is P′(q). Slide until the tangent is flat.

P(q) · P′(q) · P″(q) — the slope and the bend
Try at least five outputs, including 100, to complete this half.
On the espresso → P′(q) = −0.03q² + 2.1q + 90 = 0 is a quadratic — so a derivative question has become an equation question, which is how most of them end. Its roots are 100 and −30; only the positive one is an output. P″(100) = −0.06 · 100 + 2.1 = −3.9 < 0: a maximum, at CHF 9 000 a day. Notice what happens there: the marginal cost C′(100) = 300 − 210 + 25 = 115 equals the market price. For a price-taker, every extra bag brings in exactly 115 francs, so the best output is the one where the next bag also costs 115.
Try it · question (16), two ways

V(a) = 200√a visits for a thousand francs of ads, R(V) = 50V + 0.1V² francs from V visits. What does the next thousand bring in?

Plan. Revenue depends on ads only through visits — a function of a function. Either substitute first and differentiate the result (the printed answer's route), or multiply the two rates without substituting (the chain rule's). Both must give the same number.

From R(V(a)) to R′(25) · five moves
Step through all five.
The inner derivative is always thereQuestion (14) is the shortest derivative in the case and the easiest to get wrong: (1 000e0.04t)′ = 1 000 · e0.04t · 0.04 = 40e0.04t. Without the 0.04 the answer is 25 times too big — the Board would hear that sales are growing by 1 492 units a week at week 10 instead of 60. The exponential copies itself; the chain rule multiplies by the exponent's rate.
