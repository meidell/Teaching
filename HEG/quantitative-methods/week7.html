Fall Week 7 · Quantitative Methods I · HEG Genève

The derivative as a forecast — and as a way to solve what algebra cannot.

Weeks 4 and 5 taught you to compute a derivative. This week uses it for three jobs that come up in business every day. Forecasting a small change: if you know where you are and how fast things are moving, the tangent line tells you roughly where you will be — next month's revenue, a bond's price after a rate move. Measuring sensitivity in percent: the elasticity of demand says how many percent of sales a 1 % price rise costs, which decides whether a price rise brings in more or less money. And solving equations no formula can solve: a break-even with a cubic cost, an equilibrium with a cubic supply. Newton's method uses the tangent line over and over until it lands on the answer.

1Linear approximationthe tangent line as the best straight-line forecast near a point
2Sensitivitya bond's price against the interest rate — and how good the straight line is
3Elasticitythe same approximation in percent: η = Q′(p) · p ⁄ q
4Newton's methodfollow the tangent to the axis, again and again, to solve f(x) = 0

The session is three hours in two parts. Part 1 (~1 h 15) is the lesson: your instructor presents the deck and you follow the same numbered sections here — every slide names its section. Part 2 is eight exercises worked together, given on the slides and as forms below; solutions open as your instructor reveals them.

Section 7.1 · Linear approximation

Where you are, how fast you are moving — and roughly where you will be

J
"Finance wants next quarter's app revenue by Friday. I don't have a model of the whole year, and I don't need one. I know what we made this month and how fast it is growing. Give me the straight-line forecast — and tell me how far off it can be."
Jane · Head of Insights, XY Coffee Co.
The deck's opening example

A startup sells a software product on subscription. Monthly revenue is $10,000, and the customer base grows at 5 % a month. What is a projection of revenue for the next three months?

The deck answers with the tangent line at today, R(t) ≃ R(0) + R′(0) · t. Step through it.

From "5 % a month" to a three-month forecast · four moves
Step through all four.
The printed slide writes 10 000 + 0.05 · tThe rate of change is not 0.05: 5 % of $10 000 is $500 a month, so R′(0) = 500 and the forecast is 10 000 + 500t. Read literally, "+ 0.05 · t" adds five cents a month. A rate quoted in percent has to be multiplied by the level before it is a rate in dollars.
Linear approximation — the deck's definition

The best line near x0 is the tangent: same value, same slope.

1. g takes the same value as f at x0: g(x0) = f(x0).
2. The derivative of g takes the same value as the derivative of f at x0: g′(x0) = f′(x0).
f(x) ∼ g(x) = f(x0) + f′(x0) · (x − x0)
🔍Three pieces: where you are, how fast you are moving, how far you go. f(x0) is today's value. f′(x0) is today's rate — change per unit. (x − x0) is the distance to where you want a forecast. Rate times distance is the change; add it to today's value. The ∼ (or ≈) means "approximately", and the approximation is only good while x stays close to x0 — the further you go, the more the curve bends away from its tangent. This is Week 4's tangent line, given a new job: not "what is the slope here?" but "where does the slope take me?"
On the coffee → The deck's own example, f(x) = x² at x0 = 3: f(3) = 9, f′(3) = 6, so f(x) ∼ 9 + 6(x − 3) = 6x − 9. At 3.1 the line says 9.6; the truth is 9.61. At 4 the line says 15; the truth is 16. Close by, almost exact; further away, off by the curve's bend. At the roastery, C(60) = 40 000 and C′(60) = 800: 62 thousand doses cost about 40 000 + 2 × 800 = 41 600. The exact figure is 41 620 — the missing 20 is the 5q² bending upward.

Section 7.2 · Sensitivity

A bond's price against the interest rate — and how far to trust the straight line

The deck's example

A zero-coupon bond pays CHF 1 000 in one year. What is it worth today, at various interest rates?

Today's price is the 1 000 discounted by one year: P(r) = 1 000 ⁄ (1 + r). When rates rise, the bond is worth less — an investor can get the same 1 000 next year by putting less money in the bank.

Try it · the tangent at 4 %, one move at a time

Let r0 = 4 %. If the market rate moves to r, what is the bond worth?

P(r) ∼ P(4 %) + P′(4 %) · (r − 4 %) · four moves
Step through all four.
ΔP ≈ P′(r0) · Δr  =  −924.56 × 0.01 ≈ −9.25 per percentage point
🔍Δ (delta) means "the change in". ΔP is how much the price moves, Δr how much the rate moves. The linear approximation rewritten as a change: the price moves by the rate's move times the derivative. A derivative in francs per unit of r is awkward, because a unit of r is 100 %. Traders quote it per percentage point (Δr = 0.01) or per basis point (0.0001): this bond loses about CHF 9.25 per point and 92 centimes per basis point. That number is the bond's sensitivity, and a treasurer reads it before anything else.
Try it · move the rate

Slide the market rate. Compare the bond's true price with the straight-line estimate.

exact P(r) · tangent at 4 % · the gap
Try at least five rates, including 4 % and 10 %, to complete this half.
On the coffee → The line is always a little below the curve, on both sides of 4 %. That is not luck: P″(r) = 2 000 ⁄ (1 + r)³ > 0, so the curve bends upward — Week 5's second derivative — and a curve that bends up always lies above its tangents. For XY Coffee's treasurer the reading is: the straight-line estimate is cautious. If rates jump, the bond loses a little less than the line says; if they fall, it gains a little more. Bond traders call the bend convexity, and it is worth money.

Section 7.3 · Elasticity

The same approximation, in percent — and what it says about a price rise

J
"Purchasing says the café can put the flat white up by 5 %. Sales says we will lose customers. They are both right; the question is how many, as a share. If we lose fewer than 5 % of cups, takings go up. If we lose more, they go down. Give me that one number."
Jane · Head of Insights, XY Coffee Co.
The deck's example

For 0 < p < 5 000, the daily demand for sugar at a sale price p (CHF per ton) is Q(p) = 50 000 ⁄ p − 10.

Around p0 = 1 000 CHF per ton the demand is q0 = 40 tons, and Q′(p) = −50 000 ⁄ p² gives Q′(1 000) = −0.05. The linear approximation in plain units is Δq = −0.05 · Δp: each franc on the price of a ton costs 0.05 tons of sales a day. True — and hard to use, because "0.05 tons per franc" means nothing until you know the size of the market and the price. Elasticity rewrites it in percent.

Try it · from Δq to η, one move at a time

Turn "tons per franc" into "percent per percent".

Δq = Q′(p₀) · Δp → Δq ⁄ q₀ = η · Δp ⁄ p₀ · four moves
Step through all four.
η = Q′(p0) · p0 ⁄ q0   ·   Δq ⁄ q0 ≈ η · Δp ⁄ p0
🔍η (eta) is the percent change in quantity per percent change in price. The derivative Q′ gives the change in tons per franc; multiplying by p₀ ⁄ q₀ converts both sides into shares of where you started. Demand slopes down, so η is negative: −1.25 reads "a 1 % price rise loses about 1.25 % of sales". The size, |η|, sorts every product into three: elastic if |η| > 1 (sales react more than the price), inelastic if |η| < 1 (they react less), unit elasticity if |η| = 1. That classification is the whole business content.
Try it · how far does the percent rule hold?

Change the price of sugar by a percentage. Compare the real fall in demand with η × the price change.

real Δq ⁄ q₀ · approximate η · Δp ⁄ p₀ · revenue
Try at least five changes, including +1 % and +50 %, to complete this half.
On the coffee → What elasticity does to revenue. Revenue is p × q, so in percent it moves by roughly Δp ⁄ p + Δq ⁄ q = (1 + η) × Δp ⁄ p. With η = −1.25, a 1 % price rise loses about 0.25 % of revenue: elastic demand punishes a price rise. With Week 3's café, q = 900 − 100p, the elasticity is η = −100p ⁄ q: −0.8 at CHF 4 (inelastic — a rise brings in more), −1.25 at CHF 5 (elastic — a rise brings in less), and exactly −1 at CHF 4.50 — which is where Week 3 found the revenue peak, 450 cups and CHF 2 025. Revenue is highest exactly where demand has unit elasticity.
Don't forget that this approximation is not valid for "large" variationsThe deck's warning, and the table is the proof: a 1 % rise costs 1.24 % of sales against the rule's 1.25 %, but a 50 % rise costs 41.67 %, not 62.5 %. η is a derivative — a rate at one point. Use it for price moves of a few percent; for big moves, compute the demand at the new price.

Section 7.4 · Newton's method

An equation with no formula — follow the tangent to the axis, again and again

The deck's example · break-even analysis with a nonlinear cost function

C(q) = 1 000 + 5q² − 2q³, R(q) = 10q. Find the break-even: 1 000 + 5q² − 2q³ = 10q.

Week 3 solved break-evens with a line or a parabola — algebra and the quadratic formula. This one is a cubic. There is a formula for cubics, but nobody uses it; and for equations mixing powers, exponentials and logarithms there is none at all. How do we find the solution to this equation and the corresponding break-even quantity? The deck's answer is Newton's method, and the printed slide leaves the solution to you: from q₀ = 8 it lands on q ≈ 8.63 in three steps (worked in the speaker notes and in exercise 6's style).

Read the cost function before solving it C′(q) = 10q − 6q² is negative for every q above 1.67: this cost falls as output rises, and is below zero past about q = 8.8. Real costs do not do that, so the slide is an exercise in solving, not a costing. The homework's 7.3 has the same feature (c = 250 + 2q − 0.1q³). Exercise 6 below uses a cost that rises with output and has two break-evens.
Newton method — the deck's definition

Goal: find A such that f(A) = 0, for any function f.

Result. If f is continuous on [a, b] and f(a) and f(b) have opposite signs, then f(x) = 0 has at least one real root between a and b.
Definition. Let x0 in [a, b] be given. Then compute, for n = 0, 1, 2, 3, …
xn+1 = xn − f(xn) ⁄ f′(xn)
🔍It is section 7.1, aimed at zero. At your current guess xn, replace the curve by its tangent, f(xn) + f′(xn)(x − xn). A straight line is easy to solve: set it to 0 and you get exactly the formula. That crossing is the next guess. Each step asks "if the curve were straight from here, where would it hit zero?" Near the root the curve is almost straight, which is why the method converges fast — the number of correct decimals roughly doubles at every step. The subscript n just counts the steps.
On the coffee → Where to start (the "good" question). Find two values with opposite signs — the root is between them — and start at the end where f is closer to zero. The deck: x⁴ − 4x + 1 has f(0) = 1 and f(1) = −2, so start at 0: x₁ = 0.25, x₂ = 0.25099, x₃ ≃ 0.25099. When to stop: when two successive guesses agree to the accuracy you need (homework 7.2 asks for less than 0.0001). When it fails: where f′(xn) = 0 the tangent is flat and never meets the axis — and near such a point the next guess can be thrown far away.
