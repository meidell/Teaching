Fall Week 3 · Quantitative Methods I · HEG Genève

Two shapes run most of a business.

The straight line and the parabola. A cost that rises by the same amount per unit is a line. A revenue that climbs, peaks and falls back as the price goes up is a parabola. Almost every number a manager asks for this term — where do we break even, what price clears the market, what output makes the most money — is a question about where two of those shapes meet, or where one of them turns.

This week makes both shapes precise. Slope is a rate of change with units attached — francs per cup, dollars per year. A line can be written three ways, and each way answers a different question. A parabola has a vertex, and the vertex is a decision. And when two relationships hold at once, you have a system of equations — solved by adding, by substituting, or by drawing — which is what break-even and market equilibrium both are underneath.

Section 3.1 · Slope

Slope is a rate of change — with units

J
"The café manager tells me every franc we add to the price costs us a hundred cups a day. That is the most useful sentence anyone has said to me this month, and it is a slope. Not a formula — a sentence with a number and two units in it. Everything we do this week is about getting that sentence out of a pair of measurements, and putting it back into a decision."
Jane · Head of Insights, XY Coffee Co.
The situation

Two measurements, one line, and the number in between.

XY Coffee's café in Carouge tried two prices for its house espresso. At CHF 3.00 it sold 600 cups a day; at CHF 5.00, 400 cups. Two points: (3, 600) and (5, 400), price first, cups second.

The question the manager actually asked is not "what is the equation?" — it is what does one more franc cost us? That number is the slope:

m = (y₂ − y₁) ⁄ (x₂ − x₁) = (400 − 600) ⁄ (5 − 3) = −200 ⁄ 2 = −100  cups per franc
🔍Read it as a fraction of two changes: the change in cups, over the change in price that caused it. The top is vertical change (what happened), the bottom is horizontal change (what you did). And the sign is not decoration: negative means the two move in opposite directions — price up, cups down. A slope with the wrong sign is a demand curve that says people buy more when it costs more, and no examiner will let that pass.

The units are the part most people drop and the part the business needs. −100 on its own is a number; −100 cups per franc is a fact the manager can act on. Every slope in this course has units, and they are always (units of y) per (unit of x).

Last week: how do I put two relationships together? This week: how fast does one thing change when I change another — and where do two such relationships meet?

That is what "linear" means: the rate of change is the same everywhere. Choose a pair of prices; the triangle shows the rise and the run, and the slope is computed in front of you.

But the number the manager uses every day is the slope, because it answers the question a price meeting asks: if we go from 4.00 to 4.50, how many cups do we lose? Fifty. No graph needed.
The four kinds of slope

Sign, zero, and undefined — each one is a business situation.

Negative — y falls as x rises. The line drops from left to right. Demand: price up, cups down.
Positive — y rises as x rises. The line climbs from left to right. Supply, total cost, revenue at a fixed price.
Zero — y does not move at all. A horizontal line. A fixed cost: the rent is the rent whatever you roast.
Undefined — x does not move at all. A vertical line. A capacity limit: 2 000 kg a month, at any price.
🔍Zero and undefined are not the same thing, and confusing them is the commonest error on this topic. Zero slope is a real number: the rise is 0, so 0 ⁄ run = 0, a perfectly good horizontal line. Undefined is what you get when the run is 0 — rise ⁄ 0 — which is a division by zero and not a number at all. A vertical line has a graph and an equation (x = 3), but no slope, and — as Week 2 said — it is not a function.

Section 3.2 · Equations of a line

Three ways to write the same line — and what each is for

Point-slope form

You know one point and the rate. That is enough.

A point-slope form of the equation of the line through (x₁, y₁) with slope m is

(y − y₁) ⁄ (x − x₁) = m,  that is  y − y₁ = m(x − x₁)
🔍Read the left-hand version first, because it is the definition of slope from section 3.1: the rise from your known point to any other point (x, y) on the line, over the run, must equal m. That is all point-slope form says — every point on the line makes the same triangle. The right-hand version is the same statement with the denominator cleared, and it is the one you write with, because it has no fraction to divide by zero.

The strategy, exactly as the deck puts it: first find the slope from the given points; then substitute the slope and one of the points into the point-slope form. Worked on the deck's own example — the line through (−3, 8) and (4, −2):

m = (−2 − 8) ⁄ (4 − (−3)) = −10 ⁄ 7
y − 8 = −(10 ⁄ 7)·(x + 3)  — using the point (−3, 8)
🔍Two things to watch. The double negative in the denominator: 4 − (−3) = 7, not 1. And x − x₁ with x₁ = −3 is x + 3, which is why the bracket has a plus in it although the point had a minus. Either point would do — using (4, −2) gives y + 2 = −(10 ⁄ 7)(x − 4), a different-looking equation for the same line. Both are right, and both simplify to the same slope-intercept form.
Slope-intercept form

The rate, and where you start.

The slope-intercept form of the equation of the line with slope m and y-intercept b is

y = mx + b
🔍This is the form a business reads most easily, because its two numbers are two facts. b is where you start — the value of y when x is zero: the fixed cost, the opening balance, the price at which nobody buys. m is how fast you move — per unit of x. Total cost = fixed + variable × quantity is this form with a business's names on the letters: C = 900 + 1.00·q at the café. Nobody had to teach you that one.

The strategy: rewrite the equation so it has the form y = m·x + b. The slope is the coefficient in front of x and the y-intercept is the constant term. Worked on the deck's example, 2y − 3 = 2x + 5(3 − 2x):

2y = 3 + 2x + 5(3 − 2x) = 3 + 2x + 15 − 10x = −8x + 18
y = −4x + 9  — slope −4, y-intercept 9
🔍The only real work is expanding the bracket and collecting; the only real trap is dividing every term by 2 at the end, not just the first one. If your intercept came out as 18, that is the term you forgot.
General linear form

Everything on one side, nothing on the other.

The general linear form of the equation of a line is

ax + by + c = 0
🔍The form that looks least useful and is actually the most general: it is the only one of the three that can write a vertical line (x − 3 = 0, with b = 0), and it has no fractions, which is why textbooks like it for the final answer. Its slope is not visible — you have to solve for y to see it, which is the "reversed" conversion below. The letters a, b, c here are not the a, b, c of a quadratic; that clash of names is unfortunate and permanent.

Converting, both ways, on the deck's examples. From slope-intercept to general, clear the fractions: y = −(2 ⁄ 3)x + 4 → multiply by 3 → 3y = −2x + 12 → 2x + 3y − 12 = 0. From general to slope-intercept, solve for y — step through it:

3x + 4y − 2 = 0 → y = ? · one move at a time

Section 3.3 · Parallel, perpendicular, and linear functions

Same slope, opposite slope, and a function whose graph is a line

The definitions

Parallel means the same rate. Perpendicular means the rate turned round and flipped.

Two lines are parallel if and only if they have the same slope: m₁ = m₂, or are both vertical.
Two lines with slopes m₁ and m₂ are perpendicular if and only if m₁ = −1 ⁄ m₂. Moreover any horizontal line and any vertical line are perpendicular to each other.
🔍Parallel needs no explanation: same rate, they never meet. Perpendicular is the one to read carefully — the slopes are negative reciprocals, which means two things happen at once: the fraction turns upside down and the sign flips. Slope 3 becomes −1 ⁄ 3; slope −2 ⁄ 5 becomes +5 ⁄ 2. Equivalently, m₁ · m₂ = −1 — which is the quick check: multiply the two slopes, and if you get −1 they are perpendicular. The last sentence exists because a vertical line has no slope to multiply, so the rule needs a special case for it.

Worked as the deck does — two lines through (3, −2), one parallel and one perpendicular to y = 3x + 1:

Parallel, slope 3: y − (−2) = 3(x − 3) → y + 2 = 3x − 9 → y = 3x − 11
Perpendicular, slope −1 ⁄ 3: y + 2 = −(1 ⁄ 3)(x − 3) → y + 2 = −(1 ⁄ 3)x + 1 → y = −(1 ⁄ 3)x − 1
🔍Both are point-slope form with the same point and a different m — the point does not change, only the rate. The trap in the second line is −(1 ⁄ 3) × (−3) = +1: two negatives, so the constant comes out positive before the −2 is moved across. Most wrong answers here have −3 where the +1 should be.

Linear functions

A function whose graph is a line — with one condition.

A function f is a linear function if and only if f(x) can be written as

f : ℝ → ℝ,  x ↦ a·x + b,  where a and b are constants and a ≠ 0
🔍This is slope-intercept form with the word function attached, and one condition added. a ≠ 0 rules out the horizontal line: f(x) = 7 is a perfectly good function, but it is called a constant function, not a linear one. And notice what is not allowed at all — a vertical line — because it is not a function. So "linear function" means a line that is neither flat nor upright.

The deck's example: f(−2) = 6 and f(1) = −3. Two values of a linear function are two points on its line, so it is section 3.2 in a different notation:

m = (−3 − 6) ⁄ (1 − (−2)) = −9 ⁄ 3 = −3
y − 6 = −3(x − (−2)) = −3x − 6 → y = −3x → f(x) = −3x
🔍"f(−2) = 6" is the point (−2, 6) — the input is x, the output is y. Once you see that, the whole example is two points and the strategy from section 3.2. The intercept came out as zero, so the line goes through the origin; check it with the other point: f(1) = −3 ✓.
On the coffee → The linear functions XY Coffee runs on this week: total cost C(q) = 900 + q (CHF 900 of rent and staff a day, CHF 1.00 of beans and milk a cup), revenue at the current price R(q) = 4q, the roaster's book value V(t) = 48 000 − 5 000t, and the café's demand q(p) = 900 − 100p. Four lines, four different pairs of units, one shape. Straight-line depreciation is worth a sentence of its own: a CHF 48 000 roaster worth CHF 8 000 after eight years loses (48 000 − 8 000) ⁄ 8 = 5 000 a year — a slope, negative, in francs per year — and its book value in year five is CHF 23 000. Section 3.4 · Quadratic functions

Parabolas — the vertex is the decision

The definition, and the three things a parabola gives you for free

A quadratic function has a peak or a trough, and you can find it without a graph.

A function f is a quadratic function if and only if f(x) can be written as f(x) = a·x² + b·x + c, where a, b and c are constants and a ≠ 0. Its graph is a parabola, and:

If a > 0, the parabola opens upward. If a < 0, it opens downward.
The vertex (summit) is the point ( −b ⁄ 2a , f(−b ⁄ 2a) ).
The y-intercept is c.
🔍Three facts, in the order you use them. The sign of a tells you the shape — and therefore whether the vertex is the highest point (a < 0, a maximum) or the lowest (a > 0, a minimum). −b ⁄ 2a tells you where — and it is not a new formula: it is the midpoint of the two roots from the quadratic formula, (−b ± √Δ) ⁄ 2a, with the ± averaged away. Then f of that tells you how high. The y-intercept is c for the same reason it was in Week 2: put x = 0 in and every other term dies.

The deck's two examples, worked:

f(x) = −x² − 4x + 12:  a = −1 so it opens downward; vertex at −(−4) ⁄ (2·(−1)) = −2, height f(−2) = −4 + 8 + 12 = 16; roots from −(x + 6)(x − 2) = 0 → −6 and 2; y-intercept 12.
f(x) = x² − 6x + 7:  opens upward; vertex at −6 ⁄ (2·1) = 3, height f(3) = 9 − 18 + 7 = −2; roots 3 ± √2 (from the quadratic formula, Δ = 8); y-intercept 7.
🔍Notice the vertex sits exactly halfway between the roots both times: −2 is halfway between −6 and 2, and 3 is halfway between 3 − √2 and 3 + √2. That is not a coincidence; a parabola is symmetric about the vertical line through its vertex. So if you have the roots you have the vertex for free, and if you have the vertex and one root you have the other.

The case the deck builds toward — maximum revenue

Revenue is price times quantity, and when the price moves the quantity, revenue is a parabola.

The deck's example: demand p = 1 000 − 2q. Revenue is

r = p·q = (1 000 − 2q)·q = 1 000q − 2q²
🔍A quadratic with a = −2, b = 1 000, c = 0. a is negative, so it opens downward and the vertex is a maximum — which is exactly what you want when the question says "maximise". The vertex is at q = −b ⁄ 2a = −1 000 ⁄ (−4) = 250, and the revenue there is 1 000(250) − 2(250)² = 250 000 − 125 000 = 125 000. c = 0 says revenue is zero when you sell nothing, which is the y-intercept doing something obvious for once.

Now XY Coffee's own version. The café's demand from section 3.1 was q = 900 − 100p; read the other way round, p = 9 − 0.01q, so revenue is R(q) = 9q − 0.01q². Slide the output and watch it peak.

Section 3.5 · Systems of equations

Two facts, two unknowns

J
"Marketing has a budget and a target. The budget is one sentence — 100 000 dollars across TV and social. The target is another — 600 conversions. Two sentences, two unknowns: how many TV ads, how many social ads. Neither sentence answers the question on its own. Together they answer it exactly. That is what a system of equations is: two facts that are both true at once."
Jane · Head of Insights, XY Coffee Co.
The deck's second case

Two channels, a total budget, a conversion target.

A company advertises on TV and on social media. A TV ad costs $5 000 a week and generates 500 visits at a 5% conversion rate — 25 purchases. A social media ad costs $3 000 and generates 300 visits at 8% — 24 purchases. The company will spend $100 000 and wants 600 conversions. How many ads of each kind?

Let x be the number of TV ads and y the number of social media ads:

5 000x + 3 000y = 100 000  — the budget
25x + 24y = 600  — the target  (with 25 = 500 · 0.05 and 24 = 300 · 0.08)
🔍Each line is one sentence from the brief, translated. The budget line has money on both sides: dollars per ad times ads, plus dollars per ad times ads, equals dollars. The target line has purchases on both sides. Check the units of every term before you solve anything — a system with a money term added to a purchases term is a translation error, and the algebra will happily solve it and hand you nonsense.

The solution, which the deck gives and section 3.5 shows you how to reach: x = 40 ⁄ 3 ≈ 13 TV ads and y = 100 ⁄ 9 ≈ 11 social media ads. The deck's note applies: this assumes a simplified linear relationship between ad spending, website visits and conversions.

Three kinds of system, two methods

Two lines either cross once, never, or are the same line.

One intersection point (x₁, y₁) — the lines have different slopes. One solution. The normal case.
No intersection point — parallel lines, same slope, different intercepts. No solution.
Infinitely many intersection points — the two equations describe the same line. Every point on it is a solution.
🔍The picture is section 3.3 again: two lines with different slopes must cross, exactly once; two lines with the same slope are parallel and cross either never or everywhere. So you can tell which case you are in from the slopes alone, before solving anything. And "no solution" is a real answer in business: a budget of $100 000 and a target of 600 conversions might simply be incompatible, and the algebra is how you find that out before the campaign runs.

Two methods to solve simultaneous equations: a) elimination by addition and b) elimination by substitution. Same system, both ways — the deck's 3x − 4y = 13, 2x + 3y = 3. Step through the first:

Elimination by addition · one move at a time

By substitution, from the same first equation: extract x, x = 13 ⁄ 3 + (4 ⁄ 3)y, and insert it into the second: 3y + 2·(13 ⁄ 3 + (4 ⁄ 3)y) = 3 → 3y + 26 ⁄ 3 + (8 ⁄ 3)y = 3 → (17 ⁄ 3)y = −17 ⁄ 3 → y = −1, then x = 3. Hopefully this gives the same solution! — it does, and checking that it does is how you know both are right.

🔍Addition when the coefficients are easy to match; substitution when one equation already has a variable alone. The result is the same. Substitution is also the only method that works when one equation is not linear — which is why it comes back below for nonlinear systems, and why the deck teaches both.

Nonlinear systems

A line meets a parabola: up to two answers, found by substitution.

A system of equations with at least one nonlinear equation is called a nonlinear system. It is often solved by substitution: solve the linear equation for one variable and substitute for that variable into the other equation. The deck's example:

x² − 2x + y − 7 = 0  and  3x − y + 1 = 0
From the second, y = 3x + 1. Into the first: x² − 2x + (3x + 1) − 7 = 0 → x² + x − 6 = 0 → (x + 3)(x − 2) = 0
So x = −3, y = −8;  or  x = 2, y = 7. The solution is either (−3, −8) or (2, 7).
🔍Always substitute from the linear equation into the nonlinear one, never the other way — the linear one is the one you can solve for a variable cleanly. What comes out is a quadratic in one variable, and a quadratic has up to two roots, so a line can meet a parabola twice, once, or not at all — the discriminant from Week 1 decides which. Each x needs its own y, found from the linear equation; forgetting to go back for the y is the standard way to lose the last mark.
On the coffee → A nonlinear system is what break-even looks like when cost bends. Week 2's roastery had C(q) = 10 000 + 200q + 5q² against R(q) = 800q: a parabola and a line, meeting at 20 and 100 — two break-evens, found by exactly this substitution. Homework 3.12, 3.13, 3.17, 3.19 and 3.20 are all this move, and the market-equilibrium example in section 3.6 is too.

Section 3.6 · Applications

Where supply meets demand, and where revenue meets cost

Market equilibrium

The point of equilibrium is where the demand and supply curves intersect.

Demand slopes down: the higher the price, the less people buy. Supply slopes up: the higher the price, the more producers bring to market. Where the two cross, the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer — the equilibrium price and the equilibrium quantity. Finding it is a system of two equations, exactly as in section 3.5.

🔍Above the equilibrium price there is a surplus — sellers offer more than buyers take, stock piles up, and the price is pushed down. Below it there is a shortage — buyers want more than is offered, and the price is pushed up. That is why the crossing is called an equilibrium: it is the price the market drifts back to. Nothing in the algebra says so; the economics does, and you should be able to say it.

The deck's example, worked. Annual supply of coffee doses S(p) = 30·p (kg per year, p in francs per 100 g) and demand D(p) = 10 000 000 ⁄ (p + 50), both on [0, 1 500]. Supply and demand are equal if:

30p = 10 000 000 ⁄ (p + 50) → 30p(p + 50) = 10 000 000 → 30p² + 1 500p − 10 000 000 = 0
p = ( −1 500 ± √(1 500² − 4·30·(−10 000 000)) ) ⁄ (2·30) → p₁ ≈ 552.89, p₂ ≈ −602.89
The second is outside [0, 1 500] and is dismissed. Supply 30 × 552.89 ≈ 16 587 kg; demand 10 000 000 ⁄ 602.89 ≈ 16 587 kg. ✓
🔍A nonlinear system — one line, one curve — solved by setting the two equal, clearing the fraction, and landing on a quadratic. Two roots come out and one is rejected, and the rejection is not optional: a negative price is not a price, and the question told you the interval. The check at the end is the whole point of doing it: supply at 552.89 and demand at 552.89 are the same number, which is what "equilibrium" means.

And the deck's last example — S(p) = 5 000 000·√p, D(p) = 5 000 000 ⁄ (p + 1) − 500 000 — reduces to a third-order equation. There is a formula for those and nobody uses it; the deck's honest instruction is use the solver in Excel, which gives p ≈ 0.386. Knowing when a problem has left the territory of hand algebra is itself a skill.

Break-even

Profit is zero where revenue equals cost.

Profit (or loss) = total revenue (TR) − total cost (TC)
Total cost = variable cost + fixed cost:  yTC = yVC + yFC
The break-even point is where TR = TC (profit is zero).
🔍Two lines: revenue through the origin with slope = price, and cost starting at the fixed cost with slope = variable cost per unit. They cross once — provided the price is above the variable cost, so the revenue line is steeper. If it is not, the lines never cross on the right of the axis and the business loses money at every volume; that is a "no solution" case with a very clear meaning.

The deck's third case. A manufacturer sells at $8 a unit, fixed cost $5 000, variable cost 22 ⁄ 9 dollars a unit:

yTR = 8q,  yTC = (22 ⁄ 9)q + 5 000.  Break-even: 8q = (22 ⁄ 9)q + 5 000 → (50 ⁄ 9)q = 5 000 → q = 900, revenue 8 × 900 = 7 200.
Profit at 1 800 units: 8(1 800) − ((22 ⁄ 9)(1 800) + 5 000) = 14 400 − 9 400 = 5 000.
Loss at 450 units: 8(450) − ((22 ⁄ 9)(450) + 5 000) = 3 600 − 6 100 = −2 500.
Output for a profit of 10 000: 10 000 = 8q − (22 ⁄ 9)q − 5 000 → 15 000 = (50 ⁄ 9)q → q = 2 700.
🔍The number 8 − 22 ⁄ 9 = 50 ⁄ 9 ≈ 5.56 appears in three of the four lines and has a name: the contribution per unit, what each sale leaves after its own variable cost. Break-even is fixed cost divided by contribution, 5 000 ⁄ (50 ⁄ 9) = 900; a target profit is (fixed cost + target) divided by contribution, 15 000 ⁄ (50 ⁄ 9) = 2 700. Gallo's HBR article is about exactly this number, and it is the one a manager keeps in their head.

Equilibrium and break-even are the same algebra and different questionsBoth set two functions equal and solve. But one is about a market — what price clears it — and the other about a firm — what volume covers its costs. A student who solves for "the price" in a break-even question, or "the quantity" in an equilibrium question and then forgets to report the price, has done the algebra and missed the question.
