Fall Week 5 · Quantitative Methods I · HEG Genève

Revenue is price times quantity — and both of them move.

Week 4 gave you the derivative and three rules: constants vanish, factors ride along, sums split. That is enough for a cost made of powers of q. It is not enough for almost anything else a business measures. Revenue is a price times a quantity, and when you sell more the price has to fall — two things moving at once. Average cost is cost divided by output. And the revenue an advert brings in depends on the customers it brings in, which depend on the advert — a function of a function. Week 4 could only handle these by expanding or dividing through first, and most of the time that is not possible.

This week adds the three rules that remove that limit — product, quotient and chain — and puts each one straight to work: marginal cost from an average-cost formula, the marginal propensity to consume, the revenue an extra advert brings in. Then the deck goes back to exponentials and logarithms with the new rules in hand (and differentiates xx), and ends with the derivative of the derivative: whether a cost is rising faster or slower, and whether a product launch is still accelerating.

Section 5.1 · Marginal quantities

An advert brings customers, customers bring revenue — and marginal cost from an average

J
"Marketing wants a bigger ad budget for the app. Their argument is a chain: more ads, more customers; more customers, more revenue. Both links are real and both are curves — the hundredth ad brings fewer customers than the first. I don't want the chain described. I want one number: what does the next ad bring in, in francs, at the budget we have now."
Jane · Head of Insights, XY Coffee Co.
The deck's opening example

When the number of customers is linked to the number of ads as N(x) = 100√x, and the revenue depends on the number of customers as R(n) = 70n + n² ⁄ 20 …

… then the revenue depends on the number of ads as

R(N(x)) = 70(100√x) + (100√x)² ⁄ 20 = 7 000√x + 500x

How can we compute the sensitivity of the revenue with respect to changes in the number of ads?

🔍Read it as two machines in a row. The first turns ads into customers: N(x) = 100√x, so 100 ads bring 1 000 customers and 400 ads bring only 2 000 — the root means each extra ad brings fewer people than the last. The second turns customers into francs: R(n) = 70n + n² ⁄ 20, seventy francs a customer plus a bonus that grows with the crowd. Feeding the first machine's output into the second is composition — Week 2's R ∘ N — and the question "what does one more ad bring in?" is the derivative of the composite. The deck asks it here and answers it in section 5.4 with the chain rule. Here, this one happens to simplify to 7 000√x + 500x, so Week 4's table already answers it: 3 500 ⁄ √x + 500, CHF 850 at 100 ads. Most compositions do not simplify, and that is why the chain rule exists.

On the coffee → At 100 ads the next ad brings about 5 customers (N′(100) = 50 ⁄ √100), and at 1 000 customers each extra customer is worth about CHF 170 (R′(1 000) = 70 + 1 000 ⁄ 10). Five customers at 170 francs: CHF 850 for the next ad. That is the number Jane asked for, and it came from multiplying the two links' rates — no simplification needed. If an ad costs CHF 400, the budget should grow; past about 400 ads (where the next one brings in CHF 675) it still pays, but less each time. Notice what moves in the two links as x grows: customers per ad falls, francs per customer rises. The product of a falling number and a rising one is what the chain rule computes.
Applications of rate of change to economics — the deck's five definitions

Total cost, marginal cost, total revenue, marginal revenue, average cost.

1. The total cost function is c = C(q).
2. The marginal cost is defined as dc ⁄ dq = C′(q).
3. The total revenue function is r = R(q).
4. The marginal revenue is defined as dr ⁄ dq = R′(q).
5. The average cost is defined as C̄ = C(q) ⁄ q.
🔍Four of the five are Week 4's. The fifth is new and it is the reason for this week: the average cost is a quotient — cost divided by output — so its rate of change needs a rule Week 4 did not have. The bar over the C is how economists write "average" (read it "C-bar"); it is not a derivative and not a complement. At the roastery, with C(q) = 10 000 + 200q + 5q²: at 60 thousand doses the total is 40 000, the marginal is 800 and the average is 666.67. Three different numbers, three different questions — and section 5.2 shows how the second decides whether the third is rising or falling.

Section 5.2 · The product rule and the quotient rule

When two things move at once — revenue p · q, and average cost C ⁄ q

Product rule

The derivative of a product is not the product of the derivatives.

(d ⁄ dx)( f(x) · g(x) ) = f′(x) · g(x) + f(x) · g′(x)
🔍In words: the first one moves while the second stands still, plus the second one moves while the first stands still. Two terms, because two things are changing. The tempting wrong version, f′ · g′, fails on the simplest case there is: x · x = x² has derivative 2x, and 1 · 1 = 1 is not 2x. The rule gives 1 · x + x · 1 = 2x ✓. Check every rule you are unsure of on a case you already know.
Try it · why the rule has two terms

Revenue is a rectangle: price up the side, cups along the bottom. Sell a few more cups and watch which pieces change.

At the café, Week 3's demand gives a price of p(q) = 9 − 0.01q francs when q cups a day are sold, so revenue is R(q) = p(q) · q — a product of two things that both depend on q. Start at 200 cups and CHF 7. Choose how many more cups to sell.

ΔR = p · Δq (the gain) + q · Δp (the loss) + Δq · Δp (the corner) · open all four
0 of 4 opened — open all four to complete this half of the section.
On the coffee → Selling more cups does two things to revenue, and the product rule names both. The green strip is the extra cups sold at today's price — p · Δq, the first term. The red strip is the price cut those cups needed, paid on every cup already being sold — q · Δp, the second term, negative. The tiny corner is the price cut on the new cups themselves, and it vanishes as Δq shrinks. Per extra cup: R′ = p + q · p′ = 7 + 200 × (−0.01) = 7 − 2 = 5 — Week 4's R′(200) = 5, now with its meaning on show: the price of the next cup, minus the discount it forces on the 200 others. That second term is what every manager who says "we'll make it up on volume" forgets.
Try it · the deck's product example

Let F(x) = (x² + 3 · x) · (4 · x + 5). Compute F′(x).

f = x² + 3x,  f′ = 2x + 3  ·  g = 4x + 5,  g′ = 4  — name the four pieces first
F′(x) = (2x + 3)(4x + 5) + (x² + 3x) · 4  — first moves, second still; plus first still, second moves
= 8x² + 22x + 15 + 4x² + 12x = 12 · x² + 34 · x + 15  — the deck's answer
🔍Writing out f, f′, g, g′ before anything else is the habit that makes the product rule mechanical: four short lines, then one assembly. Check it the Week 4 way: expand first, F = 4x³ + 17x² + 15x, so F′ = 12x² + 34x + 15 ✓. For two polynomials, expanding works and either route is fine. For x² · ex or (x² + 1)(ex + 1) — quiz question 3 — nothing expands, and the product rule is the only route.
Quotient rule

Bottom times the derivative of the top, minus top times the derivative of the bottom, over the bottom squared.

(d ⁄ dx)( f(x) ⁄ g(x) ) = ( f′(x) · g(x) − f(x) · g′(x) ) ⁄ g²(x)
🔍The same two terms as the product rule, with a minus between them and the bottom squared underneath. The minus is the one thing to remember about order: a top that grows makes the fraction grow (+), a bottom that grows makes it shrink (−). g²(x) means (g(x))², the bottom squared — not g of g. Where does it come from? Write f ⁄ g = f · g−1 and use the product rule with section 5.4's power rule: f′ g−1 + f · (−g−2 g′) = (f′g − fg′) ⁄ g². So the quotient rule is the product rule in disguise, and if you forget it you can rebuild it.

The deck's example, F(x) = (4x² + 3) ⁄ (2x − 1):

F′(x) = ( 8x(2x − 1) − (4x² + 3)2 ) ⁄ (2 · x − 1)²  — the deck's line
= ( 16x² − 8x − 8x² − 6 ) ⁄ (2x − 1)² = ( 8x² − 8x − 6 ) ⁄ (2x − 1)²  — tidied, which the slide leaves to you

The roastery's average cost A(q) = C(q) ⁄ q, its rate of change by the quotient rule, and where it is lowest.

With C(q) = 10 000 + 200q + 5q², the quotient rule gives A′(q) = ( q · C′(q) − C(q) ) ⁄ q². Divide top and bottom by q and it says something you can use without any algebra:

A′(q) = ( C′(q) − A(q) ) ⁄ q  — marginal minus average, over q
The sign of A′ is the sign of MC − AC · find where A′ = 0

Marginal below average → the average falls; above → it rises; equal → the average is flat, at its lowest. Try it · the deck's three quick ones

Differentiate f(x) = 2 · x³ ⁄ 5,  f(x) = 4 ⁄ (7 · x³),  f(x) = (5 · x² − 3 · x) ⁄ (4 · x).

2x³ ⁄ 5 → 6x² ⁄ 5  — a constant on the bottom is a constant factor, (2 ⁄ 5)x³. No quotient rule.
4 ⁄ (7x³) → −12 ⁄ (7x⁴)  — a constant on top: (4 ⁄ 7)x−3, the power rule. No quotient rule.
(5x² − 3x) ⁄ (4x) → 5 ⁄ 4  — divide through first: (5x − 3) ⁄ 4, a line with slope 5 ⁄ 4.
🔍The deck puts these straight after the quotient rule to show when not to use it. All three can be done with the quotient rule and all three come out right — after three times the algebra. Before reaching for a rule, ask: is the bottom a constant (constant factor)? Is the top a constant (write it as a negative power)? Does the bottom divide into every term of the top (divide through)? Only when the answer to all three is no is it a real quotient.

Section 5.3 · The consumption function

Of the next franc of income, how much is spent — and how much is saved?

Consumption function — the deck's definitions

Let C(I) be the consumption function, as a function of the income I.

The marginal propensity to consume is

C′(I)

Let S(I) be the saving function. Thus

I = C(I) + S(I)

The marginal propensity to save is equal to one minus marginal propensity to consume:

S′(I) = 1 − C′(I)
🔍The middle line is the whole idea: every franc of income is either spent or saved, so income is consumption plus saving. Differentiate both sides with respect to I — the left side is I, whose rate is 1 — and you get 1 = C′(I) + S′(I): of one extra franc, the part spent and the part saved add up to the franc. That is why MPS is 1 − MPC, and why the deck only ever asks you to compute one derivative. "Marginal propensity" is economics' name for "the rate of change of". An MPC of 0.8 means: of the next CHF 100 of income, CHF 80 is spent and CHF 20 is saved. Read it at a particular income — the propensity of a student and of a banker are not the same number.

Section 5.4 · The chain rule and the power rule

A function of a function: the outer rate times the inner rate

Chain rule

If y is a differentiable function of u and u is a differentiable function of x, then y is a differentiable function of x and

(d ⁄ dx)( f(g(x)) ) = f′(g(x)) · g′(x)
🔍Differentiate the outside, leaving the inside alone; then multiply by the derivative of the inside. The "leaving the inside alone" part is f′(g(x)): the outer function's rate, read at wherever the inner function has put you. In Leibniz notation it looks like fractions cancelling, dy ⁄ dx = (dy ⁄ du) · (du ⁄ dx), and that is the right intuition: rates in a chain multiply. Customers per ad times francs per customer is francs per ad. Gears: if the first turns the second twice as fast and the second turns the third three times as fast, the first turns the third six times as fast — the reading's one picture.

In particular — the power rule, which is the chain rule with an outer function that is a power:

(d ⁄ dx)( g(x)n ) = n · g(x)n−1 · g′(x)
🔍Week 4's power rule, n xⁿ⁻¹, with one thing added: the inner derivative g′(x) on the end. When the inside is plain x, g′ = 1 and the factor is invisible — which is why last week never needed it. The commonest single error in this course is leaving that factor off: (x³ − 1)⁷ → 7(x³ − 1)⁶, full stop. It is option A of quiz question 2, and it is wrong by a factor of 3x².

Section 5.5 · Zoom on exponential and logarithmic functions

eu, bu, ln u — and xx, by rewriting it as e to a power

Derivatives of exponential functions — the deck's four

The table's two rows, and the same two with an inside.

FunctionDerivativeWhat it says
exexWeek 4's row: the function whose rate of change is itself.
eu(x)eu(x) · u′(x)The chain rule with outer function e(·): the exponential copies itself, then multiplies by the exponent's rate. e3x → 3e3x; ex² → 2x ex².

Derivatives of logarithmic functions

(ln x)′ = 1 ⁄ x, and with an inside, u′ ⁄ u.

(d ⁄ dx)( ln(x) ) = 1 ⁄ x,   x > 0
(d ⁄ dx)( ln(u(x)) ) = ( 1 ⁄ u(x) ) · u′(x),   u > 0
🔍The second line is last week's u′ ⁄ u — "derivative of the inside over the inside" — and now you can see where it comes from: the chain rule with outer function ln, whose rate at u is 1 ⁄ u, times the inner rate u′. The condition u > 0 is real: a derivative exists only where the function does, and ln of zero or a negative number does not. ln(x² + 1) is defined everywhere (the inside is never below 1); ln(x − 3) only for x > 3.
On the coffee → XY Coffee's subscription app has S(t) = 2 000 e0.05t subscribers t months after launch. By row 2, S′(t) = 0.05 × 2 000 e0.05t = 0.05 S(t): at 10 months, about 165 new subscribers a month. And the relative rate S′ ⁄ S = 0.05 at every t — "growing at 5 % a month" is the statement that the derivative of the log is constant, (ln S)′ = 0.05. Exponential growth is exactly the growth whose percentage rate never changes. Brand awareness after x thousand francs of advertising, A(x) = 20 ln(1 + x), is the opposite shape: A′(x) = 20 ⁄ (1 + x), so the fourth thousand francs buys 4 points and the fortieth buys under half a point.

Differentiating power functions again — the deck's xa

Find f′(x) when f(x) = xa.

f(x) = xa = ea · ln(x)  — any positive number is e to its own log
f′(x) = ( ea · ln(x) )′ = xa · a · x−1 = a · xa−1  — row 2, with u = a ln x and u′ = a ⁄ x
🔍This is the power rule, proved — for any exponent a, fractional, negative or irrational, not just the whole numbers the definition handled in Week 4. The move is the one that will matter on the next slide: when x is somewhere awkward, rewrite the function as e to a power, because eu is the one function whose derivative is easy whatever u is. Why xa = ea ln x? Because eln x = x, so xa = (eln x)a = ea ln x. Last week's board summary, used.

Section 5.6 · Higher-order derivatives

The rate of the rate — rising faster, or rising slower?

Higher-order derivatives — the deck's notation

We can take the derivative of the derivative…

NotationWhat it measures
First derivativef′(x),  y′,  dy ⁄ dxHow fast f is changing. Marginal cost.
Second derivativef″(x),  y″,  d²y ⁄ dx²How fast the rate is changing. Is marginal cost itself going up?
Third derivativef‴(x),  y‴,  d³y ⁄ dx³How fast that is changing. Rarely interpreted; often computed.
Fourth derivativef(4)(x),  y(4),  d⁴y ⁄ dx⁴From here on, a number in brackets — four primes are unreadable.
🔍Each one is the derivative of the one before — nothing new to learn, just the same rules applied again. The notation d²y ⁄ dx² is "d ⁄ dx, twice": the 2 on the d and the 2 on the dx are not squares of anything, they count how many times you differentiated. And f(4) with brackets is the fourth derivative; f⁴ without them would be f to the fourth power. The brackets are the difference.
On the coffee → The roastery: C(q) = 10 000 + 200q + 5q², C′(q) = 200 + 10q, C″(q) = 10. The first derivative says what the next thousand doses cost; the second says that cost rises by CHF 10 with every thousand you add — costs rising, and rising faster. Profit, P(q) = −5q² + 600q − 10 000, has P″ = −10: the profit curve bends downward everywhere, so where its slope is zero (q = 60) it can only be a peak. In Week 8 that sentence — f′ = 0 and f″ < 0 means a maximum — is the method.

Try it · read a launch with f, f′ and f″

XY Coffee's new cold-brew app: weekly downloads D(t) = 500 · t · e−t ⁄ 4, t weeks after launch.

A product of a power and an exponential, so its derivatives need this week's rules: D′(t) = 500 e−t ⁄ 4(1 − t ⁄ 4) by the product and chain rules, and once more, D″(t) = 500 e−t ⁄ 4(t ⁄ 16 − 1 ⁄ 2). Slide t and read the two signs together.

sign of D′ · sign of D″ · what the launch is doing
Try at least five weeks, including 4 and 8, to complete this half.
On the coffee → Four phases, and each is a pair of signs. Weeks 0–4: rising (D′ > 0), but already rising more slowly (D″ < 0). Week 4: the peak, D′ = 0 — about 736 downloads. Weeks 4–8: falling, and falling faster (D′ < 0, D″ < 0). Week 8: D″ = 0 — the steepest decline, about 68 fewer downloads a week. After week 8: still falling, but the fall is slowing (D′ < 0, D″ > 0). Most launch reports look only at D and D′ — "downloads are falling" — when the second derivative is what tells you whether the fall has started to ease. Exercise 8 is a decision that turns on it.

f″ is not (f′)²The second derivative is the derivative of the derivative — differentiate twice. Squaring f′ gives a number that is never negative, and would make "falling faster" and "falling slower" impossible to tell apart. f″(x), y″, d²y ⁄ dx²: all three mean "differentiate, then differentiate again".
