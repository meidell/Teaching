Fall Week 8 · Quantitative Methods I · HEG Genève

Where is profit highest — and how do you know it is not lowest?

Weeks 4 to 7 taught you to compute a derivative and to use it as a rate, a forecast and a solver. This week the derivative chooses. Where a curve stops rising and starts falling, its slope is zero: that is where a profit peaks, a cost bottoms out, an order size balances. The week has four moves. Find the turning points with f′ = 0 and a sign chart. Check the ends when the choice is limited to an interval. Tell a peak from a floor with the second derivative. And the hardest one: turn a business sentence into a function to maximise or minimise.

1Turning pointsf′ = 0 or undefined · the sign chart · relative max and min
2Closed intervalsthe critical values AND the endpoints · absolute max and min
3Concavitythe sign of f″ · inflection points · the second-derivative test
4Applied problemsvariable, objective, constraint · fence, average cost, lot size

The session is three hours in two parts. Part 1 (~1 h 15) is the lesson: your instructor presents the deck and you follow the same numbered sections here — every slide names its section. Part 2 is eight exercises worked together, given on the slides and as forms below; solutions open as your instructor reveals them.

By the end of Fall Week 8 you will be able to

  • Find the critical values of a function and locate its relative maxima and minima with a sign chart of f′.
  • Use the first and second derivatives to say where a function is increasing, decreasing, concave up or concave down, and where it has inflection points.
  • Find the absolute maximum and minimum of a function on a closed interval — critical values and endpoints.
  • Apply the first-order (f′ = 0) and second-order (sign of f″) conditions correctly, and know when the second one says nothing.
  • Translate a business goal into an objective function — choose the variable, write the constraint, substitute — and solve it.

Section 8.1 · Turning points

Where a function stops rising and starts falling

J
"Operations sent me an energy curve for the roastery and asked at what output the plant uses the least energy. Before I answer, tell me what the question even means — and whether the curve has an answer at all."
Jane · Head of Insights, XY Coffee Co.
The deck's opening example

Energy consumption depends on production output: E(P) = 0.02P³ − 0.1P² + 10P. How can output be adjusted to minimise energy consumption?

The printed slide asks the question and leaves it open. Step through what the derivative says — it is not what most of the room expects.

E(P) = 0.02P³ − 0.1P² + 10P · five moves
Step through all five.
A question can have no answer in the function you wrote downE′(P) = 0.06P² − 0.2P + 10 is never zero (its discriminant is 0.04 − 2.4 < 0) and always positive: total energy rises with every unit produced, so its "minimum" is to produce nothing. The useful question is energy per unit, E(P) ⁄ P = 0.02P² − 0.1P + 10, which is lowest at P = 2.5 (9.875 per unit). Choosing the right function to optimise is the first step of every applied problem — section 8.4.
The deck's definitions

Increasing, decreasing, relative extrema, critical values.

Rule 1. If f′(x) > 0 on (a, b), f is increasing there; if f′(x) < 0, f is decreasing.
Relative maximum at a: f(a) ≥ f(x) for every x in some open interval around a. Relative minimum: f(a) ≤ f(x) there. Absolute max or min: the same, but for every x in the domain.
Critical value: a value a in the domain where f′(a) = 0 or f′(a) does not exist. (a, f(a)) is a critical point.
relative extremum at a  ⇒  f′(a) = 0  or  f′(a) does not exist
🔍The arrow goes one way only. Every peak and every floor is a critical value — at the top of a smooth hill the tangent is flat, at a sharp corner there is no tangent at all. But not every critical value is a peak or a floor: x³ is flat at 0 and keeps rising through it. This is why the deck calls Rule 2 a necessary condition. Solving f′ = 0 gives you the candidates; Rule 3 — watching the sign of f′ change — decides which candidate is what.
f′ changes + to − at a ⇒ relative max  ·  − to + ⇒ relative min  ·  no change ⇒ neither
🔍Rule 3, the first-derivative test, read as a walk. Walk along the curve from left to right. Going uphill (f′ > 0) and then downhill (f′ < 0), you passed a summit. Downhill then uphill: a valley. Uphill, a flat step, uphill again: neither — a ledge. The sign chart is that walk written down: the critical values on a line, the sign of f′ in each gap between them, the verdict at each critical value. The deck draws one for x²eˣ: + 0 − 0 +, so a max at −2 and a min at 0.
On the coffee → Week 3's café profit, P(q) = 8q − 0.01q² − 900, has P′(q) = 8 − 0.02q: positive below 400 cups, zero at 400, negative above. Uphill, summit, downhill — a relative maximum at 400 cups, CHF 700 a day. Week 3 found it as the vertex of a parabola; the derivative finds it for any shape.

Section 8.2 · Absolute extrema

When the choice is limited: check the critical values and the ends

J
"The roastery cannot run below 50 tonnes or above 80 — the contracts and the machines decide that, not us. So don't give me the best output in theory. Give me the best output we can actually run."
Jane · Head of Insights, XY Coffee Co.
The deck's theorem and procedure

Extreme-value theorem: a function continuous on a closed interval [a, b] has both a maximum and a minimum on it.

1. Find the critical values of f.
2. Evaluate f at the endpoints a and b and at the critical values inside (a, b).
3. The largest value is the absolute maximum; the smallest is the absolute minimum.
candidates = { a, b } ∪ { critical values in (a, b) }  →  compare f at each
🔍A highest point has only two places to hide. Either it is inside the interval — then the curve turns there, and it is a critical value — or it is at an end, where the curve is simply cut off while still rising or falling. So the list of candidates is short and complete: the ends plus the critical values inside. No sign chart, no second derivative needed: just evaluate and compare. The deck's two pictures show both cases — in the first the highest point is inside, in the second it is the left end.
Try it · the deck's example, one move at a time

Find the absolute extrema of f(x) = x² − 4x + 5 on [1, 4].

critical values → evaluate → compare · four moves

Try it · move the window

The deck's profit P(Q) = −2Q³ + 10.5Q² − 15Q + 5. Set the range of outputs you can produce, and find the best one.

The deck asks whether Q = 1 and Q = 2.5 are a minimum or a maximum of profit. Here is the harder question behind it: is the relative maximum at 2.5 the best output?

P(Q) on [a, b] · the candidates · the absolute max and min
Try at least five windows, including [0, 4] and [0.5, 3], to complete this half.
The most-lost mark of the semester: a relative maximum reported as the bestOn [0, 4] the profit at Q = 2.5 is 1.875 — but at Q = 0 it is 5, larger. The summit of the hump is a relative maximum; the absolute maximum on [0, 4] is at the left end. On [0.5, 3] the summit wins. Same function, same derivative, different answer — because the interval changed. Always list the ends.

Section 8.3 · Concavity and the second derivative

Which way a curve bends — and the faster test for peak or floor

The deck's definitions and criteria

Concave up where f′ is increasing; concave down where f′ is decreasing.

Concave up on (a, b): f′ is increasing there — the slope gets steeper (or less negative). The curve holds water: ∪.
Concave down on (a, b): f′ is decreasing there. The curve sheds water: ∩.
Inflection point at a: f is continuous at a and changes concavity there.
f″(x) > 0 ⇒ concave up  ·  f″(x) < 0 ⇒ concave down
🔍f″ is the slope of the slope. Week 5 met it as the acceleration of a launch. Concave up does not mean "going up": the deck's three ∪ pictures include a falling curve that flattens out — its slope is negative but increasing. Concavity is about the slope's trend, not the slope's sign. An inflection point is where that trend reverses — the deck's (x − 1)³ + 1 at x = 1. The word "continuous" in the definition is there for 1 ⁄ x: it changes concavity at 0, but 0 is not in its domain, so there is no inflection point.
The second-derivative test

Suppose f′(a) = 0. If f″(a) < 0, a relative maximum; if f″(a) > 0, a relative minimum.

f′(a) = 0 and f″(a) < 0 ⇒ max  ·  f″(a) > 0 ⇒ min  ·  f″(a) = 0 ⇒ the test says nothing
🔍A flat point on a ∩ is a summit; on a ∪ it is a valley. That is the whole test: instead of checking the sign of f′ on both sides, check which way the curve bends at the point itself — one number instead of two. When f″(a) = 0 the test is silent, not negative: x⁴ has f″(0) = 0 and a minimum at 0; x³ has f″(0) = 0 and no extremum. The deck's 6x⁴ − 8x³ + 1 at x = 0 is exactly this case — go back to the sign chart. "Use the second-derivative test, if possible" means: if it says nothing, use the first.

Section 8.4 · Applied maxima and minima

From a business sentence to a function — then the calculus is the easy part

J
"Nobody in this building will ever hand you a function. They will say 'fence the yard as cheaply as possible' or 'stop ordering beans every week, it costs a fortune'. Your job is to find the number they should choose — and the hard part is writing down what you are choosing."
Jane · Head of Insights, XY Coffee Co.
The deck's method

Goal: set up an optimisation problem, then apply absolute max and min techniques.

1. Find the unknown(s) — what can you choose? Name it: x, q, the length of a side, the units per run.
2. Write the function(s) and the equation(s) — the objective to maximise or minimise, and any constraint that ties the unknowns together.
3. Solve — use the constraint to reduce to one variable, differentiate, set to zero, check it is a max or a min, and answer the question asked.
objective C(x, y)  +  constraint g(x, y) = k  →  C(x)  →  C′(x) = 0  →  C″ > 0?
🔍Two unknowns and one equation linking them means one real choice. The fence has a length and a width, but once the area is fixed at 10 800 ft², choosing the length decides the width: y = 10 800 ⁄ x. Substituting turns a two-variable cost into a one-variable function, and Weeks 4–8's tools apply. The calculus at the end is the same three lines every time. Marks are lost in step 2 — a cost that forgets one side of the fence, an average cost written as C(q) instead of C(q) ⁄ q — and in the last sentence, which must answer the question asked: "how much of each fence", not just "x = 120".
Try it · the deck's fence, one move at a time

Fence 10 800 ft² against a building. The side facing the highway costs $3 a foot, the other two sides $2. Minimise the cost.

unknowns → cost → constraint → one variable → solve → answer · six moves
Step through all six.
Try it · the economic lot size, by hand

The deck's last example: 10 000 units a year, $40 per production run, carrying cost 10 % of $20 on the average stock. Choose the run size.

Small runs mean many set-ups; big runs mean a big average stock sitting in the warehouse. Slide the run size and watch the two costs pull against each other.

set-up cost 400 000 ⁄ q · carrying cost q · their sum
Try at least five run sizes, including 625, to complete this half.
On the coffee → At the optimum the two costs are equal: q = 632.5 makes the carrying cost 632.5 and the set-up cost 400 000 ⁄ 632.5 = 632.5. That is not a coincidence — C′(q) = 1 − 400 000 ⁄ q² = 0 says exactly that the two terms balance. It is the rule behind every economic order quantity: order so that what you spend on ordering equals what you spend on storing. The deck rounds to 16 runs of 625 units because a run cannot be a fraction: 16 runs cost $1 265 a year in set-ups and carrying, 15 runs $1 266.67.
