Chi-square tests and F-tests: counts, spreads and several means, computed step by step

Weeks 7 to 9 tested averages of numbers. This week tests three other things. A chi-square test (written χ², said "khi-square") tests counts: how many people fall in each category, and whether those counts fit a claim or depend on another category. An F-test compares two spreads (two variances), and the same F-distribution lets a single test compare three or more averages at once, which is called ANOVA. Each test has one formula, one table and one rule, and this week explains all three for each test: what the statistic measures, how to compute it by hand, why it works, and where Excel prints it.

Every example is either a question from a past exam of this course or a small made-up data set you can recompute with a calculator. Every section has a widget where the numbers move, two worked examples, and one exercise the page checks.

Section 10.1 · The chi-square statistic and its distribution

How far the counts you observed are from the counts you expected

Why this section: both chi-square tests of the week (10.2 and 10.3) use the same statistic and the same table. Learn the statistic once, with its three design choices, and learn to read the table once; the two tests then differ only in how the expected counts and the degrees of freedom are computed.

Theory · the statisticO = observed count, E = expected count

Suppose a claim says how many people should fall in each category (the expected counts E), and you count how many did (the observed counts O). The statistic adds up, over every category, the squared miss divided by the expected count:

χ² = Σ (O − E)² ⁄ E (one term per category, then add them all)

Three choices are built into this formula, and each has a reason:

  • The miss is squared, so that a category with too many people and a category with too few both push χ² up. The misses always add up to zero (the O's and the E's have the same total), so adding them unsquared would always give 0.
  • Each squared miss is divided by E, because a miss of 2 is big when you expected 10 (12 instead of 10 is 20% off) and nothing when you expected 1000. Dividing by E measures the miss relative to what was expected: 2² ⁄ 10 = 0.4, but 2² ⁄ 1000 = 0.004.
  • The terms are added, so one number summarises the whole table. χ² is 0 when every count is exactly as expected, and it grows with every miss. It can never be negative.
Theory · the chi-square distribution and its tableFigure 12.4 of the book · =CHISQ.INV.RT in Excel

If the claim is true, χ² is still not exactly 0, because samples vary. Its typical values follow the chi-square distribution, a curve that starts at 0, rises, and has a long tail to the right. The curve depends on one number, the degrees of freedom df (how many counts are free to vary; each test says how to count them). Its mean is df, so a χ² near df is ordinary and a χ² far above df is rare.

Reject H₀ when χ² > χ²α(df), the critical value in the row df and the column α of the table
Excel: =CHISQ.INV.RT(α, df) gives the critical value · =CHISQ.DIST.RT(χ², df) gives the P-value

The test is always right-tailed: only a large χ² is evidence against the claim, because only misses make it large. A small χ² means the counts fit; there is nothing to reject. The table (Figure 12.4) has one row per df and one column per α: 0.10, 0.05, 0.025, 0.01, 0.005. You read one cell.

Example A · reading the tablethe 2026 exam, question 6

A test on a table of counts gave χ² = 16.59 with df = 2. Decide at the 5% level.

Read the critical value, compare, and say in one sentence what the comparison means.

The critical value: row df = 2, column α = 0.05 of Figure 12.4: χ²0.05(2) = 5.991. In Excel, =CHISQ.INV.RT(0.05, 2) = 5.991.

The comparison: 16.59 > 5.991, so χ² is in the rejection region. We reject H₀.

What it means: if the claim were true, a χ² this large would happen less than 5 times in 100 samples. In fact =CHISQ.DIST.RT(16.59, 2) = 0.00025: about 2 times in 10 000. The counts do not fit the claim.

Resultχ² = 16.59 > 5.991 → reject H₀ at 5% (P-value 0.00025)
Example B · the other outcomethe 2024 exam, question 6

Another table gave χ² = 2.35 with df = 2. Decide at the 1% level.

The critical value: row df = 2, column α = 0.01: χ²0.01(2) = 9.210. A smaller α moves the critical value to the right: you need stronger evidence to reject.

The comparison: 2.35 < 9.210, so χ² is not in the rejection region. We fail to reject H₀.

What it means: the counts are as close to the claim as ordinary sampling variation allows (P-value 0.309: a χ² of 2.35 or more happens in 31% of samples when the claim is true). This is not proof that the claim is true; it is the absence of evidence against it. The exam expects exactly that wording.

Resultχ² = 2.35 < 9.210 → fail to reject H₀ at 1% (P-value 0.31)

Section 10.2 · The test of independence

Two ways of sorting the same people: are they related?

Why this section: this is the chi-square question the exam asks most often (2023, 2024 and 2026 all had one): a table of counts with rows for one sorting (field of study) and columns for another (plans to start a business), and the question "are the two related, or independent?". The whole test is five written steps, and the exam gives points for each step.

Theory · the expected counts, and why they are computed that waya contingency table = counts sorted two ways

H₀: the two ways of sorting are independent (knowing the row tells you nothing about the column). Hₐ: they are not independent.

If H₀ is true, the share of "Plan" is the same in every row, and equals the share of "Plan" in the whole table. So the count expected in a cell is its row total times that share:

E = (row total × column total) ⁄ n for every cell
χ² = Σ (O − E)² ⁄ E over every cell df = (rows − 1) × (columns − 1)

Why (r − 1)(c − 1): the row totals and the column totals are fixed by the data, so once you have filled all cells but the last of each row and the last of each column, the rest are forced. In a 3 × 2 table only 2 × 1 = 2 cells are free; df = 2.

The rule: reject H₀ when χ² > χ²α(df). The test is valid when every E is at least 5 (merge small categories if not). In Excel, type the E's with the formula above, then =CHISQ.TEST(observed range, expected range): it returns the P-value directly, not χ².

Theory · the five steps the exam marks2 + 4 + 4 points in 2026
  1. Hypotheses, in words: H₀ "field of study and the plan to start a business are independent"; Hₐ "they are related".
  2. Expected counts: one E per cell, row total × column total ⁄ n. Check they add up to the same totals as the observed counts.
  3. The statistic: one term per cell, (O − E)² ⁄ E, then the sum χ².
  4. The critical value: df = (r − 1)(c − 1), the row of the table, the column α.
  5. The conclusion, in words, with the comparison: "χ² = 16.59 > 5.991, reject H₀: field of study and the intention to start a business are related."

Section 10.3 · Goodness of fit

One way of sorting against a claimed distribution: do the counts fit?

Why this section: the second chi-square test looks like the first (same statistic, same table) but answers a different question: not "are two sortings related?" but "do these counts match what a claim says they should be?" — a die is fair, four suits are equally likely, 40% of orders are espresso. The exam of 2022 asked exactly this, and the trap is to use the wrong df.

Theory · expected counts from claimed sharesk categories, n observations

H₀: the categories have the claimed probabilities p₁, p₂, …, pk (which add up to 1). Hₐ: at least one probability differs. The claim says how many of the n observations to expect in each category:

Ei = n × pi (equal shares: E = n ⁄ k for every category)
χ² = Σ (O − E)² ⁄ E over the k categories df = k − 1

Why k − 1: the k observed counts must add up to n, so once k − 1 of them are known the last is forced. Only k − 1 are free. Four suits: df = 3. Six faces of a die: df = 5.

The rule is the same as in 10.2: reject H₀ when χ² > χ²α(k − 1); every E should be at least 5. In Excel, type the E's as n × pi and use =CHISQ.TEST(observed, expected) for the P-value.

Example A · equal sharesthe 2022 exam, question 5 (20 points)

A poker machine dealt 1400 cards: Spades 354, Hearts 370, Diamonds 350, Clubs 326. Could the suits be equally likely? Test at the 5% level.

1. H₀: each suit has probability ¼. Hₐ: at least one suit has a different probability.

2. E = 1400 × ¼ = 350 for every suit.

3. (354 − 350)² ⁄ 350 = 16 ⁄ 350 = 0.046; (370 − 350)² ⁄ 350 = 400 ⁄ 350 = 1.143; (350 − 350)² ⁄ 350 = 0; (326 − 350)² ⁄ 350 = 576 ⁄ 350 = 1.646. χ² = 2.83.

4. df = 4 − 1 = 3; χ²0.05(3) = 7.815. (Not df = 2: this is one list of four categories, not a table.)

5. 2.83 < 7.815: fail to reject H₀. The dealing is consistent with fair suits. The official key printed 3.93, having miscopied the Spades term as 1.14; recomputed, 2.83, same conclusion.

ResultE = 350 · χ² = 2.83 · df 3 · critical 7.815 → fail to reject
Example B · unequal claimed sharesmade-up data, a coffee kiosk

A kiosk's owner says that 40% of orders are espresso, 30% cappuccino, 20% latte and 10% tea. In 200 orders you count 92, 54, 38 and 16. Does the claim fit, at the 5% level?

1. H₀: p = (0.40, 0.30, 0.20, 0.10). Hₐ: at least one share differs.

2. E = 200 × 0.40 = 80; 200 × 0.30 = 60; 200 × 0.20 = 40; 200 × 0.10 = 20. They add to 200, as the counts do.

3. (92 − 80)² ⁄ 80 = 144 ⁄ 80 = 1.80; (54 − 60)² ⁄ 60 = 0.60; (38 − 40)² ⁄ 40 = 0.10; (16 − 20)² ⁄ 20 = 0.80. χ² = 3.30.

4. df = 4 − 1 = 3; critical 7.815.

5. 3.30 < 7.815: fail to reject. The 200 orders fit the owner's shares (P-value 0.35). Twelve more espressos than expected is within ordinary variation.

Resultχ² = 3.30 on 3 df → fail to reject: the claimed shares fit

Section 10.4 · The F distribution and the test of two variances

Is one spread larger than the other? Divide the two variances.

Why this section: two reasons. First, "are these two samples equally spread?" is a question on its own (is one machine less regular than the other?), and it is also the assumption behind the pooled two-sample t test of Week 8. Second, the F distribution you learn here is the one that ANOVA (10.5) and the regression printout (Week 9, the Significance F cell) use. Learn it once.

Theory · the F statistic and its distributionFigures 12.5–12.6 of the book · =F.INV.RT in Excel

Take two samples from normal populations, with sample variances s₁² and s₂². The statistic is their ratio:

F = s₁² ⁄ s₂² df₁ = n₁ − 1 (the numerator), df₂ = n₂ − 1 (the denominator)

Why a ratio: if the two population variances are equal, s₁² and s₂² estimate the same number, so F is near 1. A much larger s₁² gives F well above 1; a much smaller one gives F well below 1. The units cancel, so F has no unit.

When the population variances are equal, F follows the F distribution with (df₁, df₂) degrees of freedom: positive, piled up around 1, with a long right tail. It needs two df, so its table (Figures 12.5–12.6) is one grid per α: df₁ across the top, df₂ down the side. The cell is Fα(df₁, df₂), the value with area α to its right.

Left tail from the same table: F1−α(df₁, df₂) = 1 ⁄ Fα(df₂, df₁) (swap the two df, take the reciprocal)

Why the swap: if F = s₁² ⁄ s₂² is in the bottom α of its distribution, then 1 ⁄ F = s₂² ⁄ s₁², whose df are swapped, is in the top α of its own. Excel: =F.INV.RT(α, df₁, df₂) for a critical value, =F.DIST.RT(F, df₁, df₂) for a right-tail P-value.

Theory · the test, one-tailed or two-tailed=F.TEST in Excel gives the two-tailed P-value
Hₐreject H₀: σ₁² = σ₂² when
σ₁² > σ₂² (right-tailed)F > Fα(df₁, df₂)
σ₁² < σ₂² (left-tailed)F < F1−α(df₁, df₂) = 1 ⁄ Fα(df₂, df₁)
σ₁² ≠ σ₂² (two-tailed)F > Fα/2(df₁, df₂) or F < F1−α/2(df₁, df₂)

A practical habit: for a two-tailed test, put the larger sample variance in the numerator. Then F ≥ 1 and you only need the right critical value Fα/2, and you never need the reciprocal. For a one-tailed test, the numerator is the variance that Hₐ says is larger.

The test assumes both populations are normal; it is sensitive to that assumption, more than the t tests are.

Example A · one-tailedmade-up data, two baristas

Barista A's preparation times have s = 12 seconds over 10 coffees; barista B's have s = 20 seconds over 13 coffees. Is B less regular, at the 5% level?

1. "Less regular" means a larger variance, so B goes in the numerator. H₀: σB² = σA². Hₐ: σB² > σA² (right-tailed).

2. F = sB² ⁄ sA² = 20² ⁄ 12² = 400 ⁄ 144 = 2.78, with df₁ = 13 − 1 = 12 and df₂ = 10 − 1 = 9.

3. F0.05(12, 9) = 3.07 (column 12, row 9 of the α = 0.05 table; Excel =F.INV.RT(0.05, 12, 9) = 3.073).

4. 2.78 < 3.07: fail to reject. B's times look more spread (a variance 2.8 times larger), but with 13 and 10 coffees that ratio is not rare when the true spreads are equal (P-value 0.066). More coffees would settle it.

ResultF = 2.78 on (12, 9) · critical 3.07 → fail to reject
Example B · two-tailedmade-up data, two filling machines

Machine 1 filled 16 bottles with s = 2.4 ml; machine 2 filled 25 bottles with s = 1.5 ml. Do the two machines have different variability, at the 5% level?

1. H₀: σ₁² = σ₂². Hₐ: σ₁² ≠ σ₂² (two-tailed). The larger variance is machine 1's, so it goes on top and only the right critical value is needed.

2. F = 2.4² ⁄ 1.5² = 5.76 ⁄ 2.25 = 2.56, df₁ = 15, df₂ = 24.

3. Two-tailed at α = 0.05 means α ⁄ 2 = 0.025 in each tail: F0.025(15, 24) = 2.44. (The left critical value, if you needed it: 1 ⁄ F0.025(24, 15) = 1 ⁄ 2.70 = 0.37.)

4. 2.56 > 2.44: reject H₀. The two machines do not fill with the same regularity; machine 1 is more variable. Excel: =F.TEST(range1, range2) gives the two-tailed P-value, 0.039.

ResultF = 2.56 on (15, 24) · critical 2.44 at α⁄2 → reject

Section 10.5 · One-way ANOVA

Three or more averages at once: compare the spread between the groups to the spread within them

Why this section: Week 8 compared two averages with a t test. With three delivery services, three suppliers or four branches, you would need three or six t tests, and each one carries its own 5% chance of a false alarm: three tests at 5% give about a 14% chance that at least one cries wolf. ANOVA (analysis of variance) asks the question once, with one F. It is also the test behind the middle block of every regression printout you read in Week 9.

Theory · two ways to measure spread, and their ratiok groups, n observations in all

H₀: all k population means are equal. Hₐ: at least one differs. The idea: if the means are all equal, the group averages x̄₁, x̄₂, … x̄k should differ from each other only as much as the values differ inside each group. So compute both spreads and divide:

Between the groups: SST = Σ ni (x̄i − x̄)² (x̄ = the grand mean of all n values), MST = SST ⁄ (k − 1)
Within the groups: SSE = Σ (ni − 1) si² (add up each group's squared deviations), MSE = SSE ⁄ (n − k)
F = MST ⁄ MSE with df = (k − 1, n − k) · reject H₀ when F > Fα(k − 1, n − k)

Why it works: MSE estimates the common variance σ² whatever the means are, because it only looks inside the groups. MST estimates σ² too when H₀ is true, because then the group averages scatter around the grand mean exactly as averages of ni values do; but when the means differ, MST is inflated by those differences. So F is near 1 under H₀ and large when the means differ: the test is right-tailed.

Why these df: k group averages lose one degree of freedom to the grand mean, so k − 1 between; each group's si² has ni − 1, and they add up to n − k within. The two add to n − 1, the df of the whole sample.

Example A · by handmade-up data, three delivery services

Fifteen deliveries, five per service, in minutes. Are the three average delivery times equal, at the 5% level?

Service A2831253026
Service B3430363332
Service C2927312627

1 · The averages. x̄A = 140 ⁄ 5 = 28, x̄B = 165 ⁄ 5 = 33, x̄C = 140 ⁄ 5 = 28; grand mean x̄ = 445 ⁄ 15 = 29.667.

2 · Between. SST = 5(28 − 29.667)² + 5(33 − 29.667)² + 5(28 − 29.667)² = 5(2.778) + 5(11.111) + 5(2.778) = 83.33; MST = 83.33 ⁄ (3 − 1) = 41.67.

3 · Within. Squared deviations inside A: 0 + 9 + 9 + 4 + 4 = 26 (sA² = 6.5); B: 1 + 9 + 9 + 0 + 1 = 20 (sB² = 5.0); C: 1 + 1 + 9 + 4 + 1 = 16 (sC² = 4.0). SSE = 62; MSE = 62 ⁄ (15 − 3) = 5.167.

4 · F. F = 41.67 ⁄ 5.167 = 8.06 on (2, 12) df. F0.05(2, 12) = 3.89.

5 · Conclusion. 8.06 > 3.89: reject H₀. The three services do not have the same average delivery time (P-value 0.006). The test does not say which one differs; the averages suggest B is slower.

Example A · the written answer the exam expectsthe 2026 question, marked 2 + 4 + 4

Write the answer to the 2026 question (section 10.2, Example A) as it should appear on the exam sheet

(a) Hypotheses — 2 points. "H₀: the intention to start a business is independent of the field of study. Hₐ: the intention depends on the field of study." Both lines, in words about the variables, not "H₀: χ² = 0".

(b) Define and calculate χ² — 4 points. "χ² = Σ(O − E)² ⁄ E, where E = row total × column total ⁄ n." Then the six E's in a table (39.30, 40.70, 39.30, 40.70, 34.39, 35.61), the six terms (5.50, 5.31, 1.36, 1.31, 1.59, 1.53) and the sum χ² = 16.59. Points are given for the formula, the E's, the terms and the sum: write all four.

(c) Critical region and conclusion — 4 points. "df = (3 − 1)(2 − 1) = 2. At α = 0.05 the critical value is 5.991; the rejection region is χ² > 5.991. Since 16.59 > 5.991, we reject H₀: the intention to start a business depends on the field of study." A conclusion that stops at "reject H₀" loses the points for the sentence in words.

Example B · an ANOVA question in exam stylethe four cafés of 10.5

"The printout below compares the daily sales of four cafés. State the hypotheses, explain what F measures, and conclude at the 5% level." Write the answer.

Hypotheses. "H₀: the four cafés have the same mean daily sales, μ₁ = μ₂ = μ₃ = μ₄. Hₐ: at least one mean differs."

What F measures. "F = MS Between ⁄ MS Within = 47 089.93 ⁄ 1838.54 = 25.61. MS Between measures how far the four averages are from the grand mean; MS Within measures how far the days are from their own café's average. Under H₀ both estimate the same variance and F is near 1."

Conclusion. "df = (3, 20); F crit = 3.098; 25.61 > 3.098, equivalently the P-value 4.71E−07 is below 0.05. We reject H₀: the cafés do not all have the same mean daily sales." Add which café stands out if the question asks for an interpretation: Eaux-Vives, at CHF 1402 a day against about 1200–1260 for the others.

ResultF = 25.61 on (3, 20), P-value 4.7E−07 → the means are not all equal
