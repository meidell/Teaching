A flipped, data-driven statistics course

The interactive weeks deliver and drill the content; the contact hours become the things a screen can't do; and the instructor dashboard tells you, each week, exactly where the class is stuck. This plan follows the official syllabus — sixteen weeks, Saylor's Introductory Statistics, 70 / 15 / 15 — and borrows its supervision model from the E1410 course: one running project, earned progress, everything visible before the next session.

Course hub →Dashboard →
The core move: before each session, open the dashboard and look at which sections stalled, which self-check questions scored lowest, and which workshop fields are empty. Aim that week's class at those gaps. A statistics course taught with live data about the class itself is the whole thesis, lived.

1. Assessment — the syllabus, and how the site evidences it

The weighting is the syllabus's own. What the site adds is evidence: presence and the project are both observable week by week rather than reconstructed at the end.

70%
Final exam · Week 16
Individual, 90 min, closed book. Authorised: a simple non-programmable calculator and two hand-written sheets (two-sided). Six exercises × 10 points, graded to the half point. Every week's self-check quiz and the exam cards rehearse exactly its archetypes.
15%
Individual project
The pizzeria file: every week's workshop page asks the student to apply that week's method to their pizzeria, and the answers are saved. The project is that file, finished and argued. The dashboard's assignment reader shows it per student, with the gaps spelled out.
15%
Presence
Graded to the half point. The in-room checkpoint (the self-check quiz, run live at the end of each session) timestamps who was there and working — a second source next to the register.
0%
Homework · every week
Assigned weekly, not graded, with hints and worked solutions. Scores still reach the dashboard, which is how the weakest problem of the week is found before the session.
Two safeguards, so the project and presence marks are defensible:
  • First attempt wins. Quiz and classify picks are recorded once and replayed on reload; a student cannot overwrite their own record. The dashboards show the honest first number. built ✓
  • Per-student randomised numbers in the practice arena and exam cards — copying is pointless. built ✓
  • Time-on-task sanity. The dashboard logs active minutes; near-zero time with full completion is a flag, and the weekly in-room checkpoint is the cross-check.
  • The blank test (Week 15) is taken at home and corrected in class — informative, never graded, and the moment students discover what their two sheets are missing.

2. The repeatable session

4. Supervising students — the E1410 model, applied here

Four instructor views, all reading the same data the week pages write. Nothing is typed twice and nothing is reconstructed at the end of term.

  • Dashboard — what do I do before the next session? Ranked actions, the cohort's shape, one roster table with a per-student drawer: sections done, time on task, self-check scores, the pizzeria file so far. Flags are relative to the cohort's own median, never a fixed threshold.
  • Wide table — every student's state on every week and on the project, in one scrollable table with sticky names. The per-question quiz stats live here: the two worst-answered questions of last week are the retrieval opener of the next.
  • Insights — where is the course failing them? Section-level stall points, week-to-week drop-off, workbook fill rates — including which classify items misled people, with the scenario text.
  • Messages — the student panel is on every page (bottom-right); the instructor's end shows threads with one student, a group or the whole class. Announcements post from the dashboard and appear as a banner on every page.
Identity: students sign in once with their name and a six-digit code (/shared/login.js), so progress follows them between laptop and phone and reaches the namespace statistics. Progress is always written locally first and back-filled on sign-in, so nothing done before signing in is lost. Lost codes are on the roster in the dashboard. The site is public and listed; there is deliberately no class password.
