About this course

What it's about

Inferential statistics — hypothesis testing and confidence intervals — for a variety of situations, and the beginnings of multivariate statistics: simple and multiple linear regression. We learn each method by applying it to a real decision the pizzeria has to take, so that the statistics of a professional role — a data analyst's, say — is practised, not just heard.

Prerequisites

Courses on descriptive statistics and probability theory. Weeks 1–2 rebuild the descriptive part from scratch and Week 3 opens with the probability rules you need — so if the prerequisite is rusty, the first three weeks are where you fix it.

Use of AI

For the project you are encouraged to use ChatGPT and other AI tools freely to research, check and refine your work. The final exam is closed book: no AI, no computer, no phone. AI helps you learn and prepare; it will not be in the room when you are asked to demonstrate what you know.

What you'll be able to do

By the end of the course you will be able to:

  • Apply the theory of sampling distributions.
  • Estimate statistical parameters — mean, proportion, variance — and construct confidence intervals.
  • Perform statistical tests in a variety of situations.
  • Estimate differences between two populations and test them; tell independent from paired samples.
  • Apply simple linear regression and compute its coefficients; use multiple regression for model selection.
  • Understand the concept of machine learning and the main estimation methods.
  • Use Python (pandas, SciPy, statsmodels) for data analysis.

Assessment · how the grade is built

70% · Final exam — Week 16

Individual, 90 minutes, graded to the half point. Closed book: the only authorised material is a simple non-programmable calculator and a personal summary of two sheets, both sides, hand-written. No computers, phones or communication devices.

Your rehearsal is built into the courseEvery week's self-check quiz is written in the exam's style — one attempt per question, every answer explained. The exam cards pool every question type the paper can ask, with fresh numbers each time. And a blank test in Week 15 is taken at home and corrected in class: informative, not graded.
🃏 Exam cards → 🎯 Practice arena →

Self-check scores are not graded, but they reach the instructor dashboard — which is how I see, before each session, which concepts the class has not yet landed.

15% · Individual project  +  15% · Presence

The project grows out of the course: every week's workshop page asks you to apply that week's method to your pizzeria, in your own words, and what you write is saved. By the end of the semester those pages are a pricing file you could take to a bank — the project is that file, finished and argued. Graded to the half point.

Presence is 15% and graded to the half point too — the class in the room is where the theory gets argued out, and it is not recoverable from the site alone.

Homework is assigned every week and does not count toward your grade. It is where you find out whether the lesson stuck — do it by hand, with a calculator, the way the exam will be.

Computer sessions during the semester prepare the project. Week 11 uses DataTab; Week 12 is Python in Google Colab — nothing to install.

The running case

Across all sixteen weeks we run one business: a pizzeria near Plainpalais, and the very first number it must commit to is the price of a Margherita. Week 1 makes you guess it on instinct; Week 2 describes the competition; Weeks 5–6 say how sure you can be about "the Geneva price" from a sample; Weeks 7–8 test whether a difference is real; Week 9 asks what predicts price; Week 10 whether topping preference depends on neighbourhood. By Week 16 that gut number has become a defended interval — a recommendation you could stand behind in front of your partner, your bank, and your customers.

The datasets are small and hand-computable on purpose: the exam is by hand. The practice arena and Python (Week 12) are where the big datasets live.
