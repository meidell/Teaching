Chapter 10 · the questionSaylor, chapter 10 — introduction ↗

Our interest in this chapter is in situations in which we can associate to each element of a population or sample two measurements x and y, particularly in the case that it is of interest to use the value of x to predict the value of y. For example, the population could be the air in automobile garages, x could be the electrical current produced by an electrochemical reaction taking place in a carbon monoxide meter, and y the concentration of carbon monoxide in the air. In this chapter we will learn statistical methods for analyzing the relationship between variables x and y in this context.

Chapters 7 to 9 dealt with one variable at a time — a mean, a proportion, a difference between two groups. Now each pizzeria carries two numbers, distance x and price y, and the question is how one moves with the other. The tools are new; the logic is not. r measures, the least squares line estimates, and the slope is tested with the same five steps as Week 7, on a t-distribution with n − 2 degrees of freedom.

A line can always be drawn through a cloud of points. The chapter is about how much that line deserves to be believed.

Section 9.2 · Linear relationships and r (§10.1–10.2)

How strong, and which way

Learning ObjectiveSaylor §10.1 ↗
  • To learn what it means for two variables to exhibit a relationship that is close to linear but which contains an element of randomness.

The following table gives examples of the kinds of pairs of variables which could be of interest from a statistical point of view.

x — Predictor or independent variabley — Response or dependent variable
Temperature in degrees CelsiusTemperature in degrees Fahrenheit
Area of a house (sq.ft.)Value of the house
Age of a particular make and model carResale value of the car
Amount spent by a business on advertising in a yearRevenue received that year
Height of a 25-year-old manWeight of the man

The first line in the table is different from all the rest because in that case and no other the relationship between the variables is deterministic: once the value of x is known the value of y is completely determined. In fact there is a formula for y in terms of x: y = (9 ⁄ 5)x + 32. […] The relationship between x and y is called a linear relationship because the points so plotted all lie on a single straight line. The number 9 ⁄ 5 in the equation is the slope of the line, and measures its steepness. It describes how y changes in response to a change in x: if x increases by 1 unit then y increases (since 9 ⁄ 5 is positive) by 9 ⁄ 5 unit. […] The number 32 is the y-intercept of the line; it identifies where the line crosses the y-axis.

In contrast, all the other relationships listed in the table above have an element of randomness in them. Consider the relationship described in the last line of the table, the height x of a man aged 25 and his weight y. If we were to randomly select several 25-year-old men and measure the height and weight of each one, we might obtain a collection of (x, y) pairs […]. Such a plot is called a scatter diagram or scatter plot. Looking at the plot it is evident that there exists a linear relationship between height x and weight y, but not a perfect one. The points appear to be following a line, but not exactly. There is an element of randomness present.

Learning ObjectiveSaylor §10.2 ↗
  • To learn what the linear correlation coefficient is, how to compute it, and what it tells us about the relationship between two variables x and y.
Definition · the linear correlation coefficientSaylor §10.2

The linear correlation coefficient for a collection of n pairs (x, y) of numbers in a sample is the number r given by the formula

r = SSxy ⁄ √(SSxx · SSyy)

where

SSxx = Σx² − (1 ⁄ n)(Σx)²  ·  SSxy = Σxy − (1 ⁄ n)(Σx)(Σy)  ·  SSyy = Σy² − (1 ⁄ n)(Σy)²

The linear correlation coefficient has the following properties:

  1. The value of r lies between −1 and 1, inclusive.
  2. The sign of r indicates the direction of the linear relationship between x and y: if r < 0 then y tends to decrease as x is increased; if r > 0 then y tends to increase as x is increased.
  3. The size of |r| indicates the strength of the linear relationship between x and y: if |r| is near 1 (that is, if r is near either 1 or −1) then the linear relationship between x and y is strong; if |r| is near 0 (that is, if r is near 0 and of either sign) then the linear relationship between x and y is weak.

What the three SS numbers are. SSxx is the sum of the squared distances of the x-values from their mean — Week 2's spread, before dividing by n − 1. SSyy is the same for y. SSxy multiplies the two distances pair by pair: a pizzeria both further out than average and cheaper than average contributes a negative product, and if most do, SSxy is negative. r divides that co-movement by the two spreads, which is what makes it a pure number between −1 and 1, whatever the units.

Section 9.3 · The model and the least squares line (§10.3–10.4)

The line that misses least

Learning ObjectiveSaylor §10.3 ↗
  • To learn the framework in which the statistical analysis of the linear relationship between two variables x and y will be done.

For each fixed value of x a sub-population of the full population is determined, such as the collection of all houses with 2,100 square feet of living space. For each element of that sub-population there is a measurement y, such as the value of any 2,100-square-foot house. Let E(y) denote the mean of all the y-values for each particular value of x. […]

Our first assumption is that the relationship between x and the mean of the y-values in the sub-population determined by x is linear. This means that there exist numbers β1 and β0 such that E(y) = β1x + β0. […] Our next assumption is that for each value of x the y-values scatter about the mean E(y) according to a normal distribution centered at E(y) and with a standard deviation σ that is the same for every value of x. […] Our last assumption is that the random deviations associated with different observations are independent.

Saylor §10.3

Simple Linear Regression ModelSaylor §10.3

For each point (x, y) in data set the y-value is an independent observation of

y = β1x + β0 + ε

where β1 and β0 are fixed parameters and ε is a normally distributed random variable with mean 0 and an unknown standard deviation σ.

The line with equation y = β1x + β0 is called the population regression line.

Deterministic Part. The first part β1x + β0 is the equation that describes the trend in y as x increases. […] Random Part. The second part ε is a random variable, often called the error term or the noise. […] The slope parameter β1 represents the expected change in y brought about by a unit increase in x. The standard deviation σ represents the magnitude of the noise in the data.

Learning ObjectivesSaylor §10.4 ↗
  • To learn how to measure how well a straight line fits a collection of data.
  • To learn how to construct the least squares regression line, the straight line that best fits a collection of data.
  • To learn the meaning of the slope of the least squares regression line.
  • To learn how to use the least squares regression line to estimate the response variable y in terms of the predictor variable x.

To each point in the data set there is associated an "error," the positive or negative vertical distance from the point to the line: positive if the point is above the line and negative if it is below the line. The error can be computed as the actual y-value of the point minus the y-value ŷ that is "predicted" by inserting the x-value of the data point into the formula for the line: error at data point (x, y) = (true y) − (predicted y) = y − ŷ.

A first thought for a measure of the goodness of fit of the line to the data would be simply to add the errors at every point, but the example shows that this cannot work well in general. The line does not fit the data perfectly (no line can), yet because of cancellation of positive and negative errors the sum of the errors […] is zero. Instead goodness of fit is measured by the sum of the squares of the errors. Squaring eliminates the minus signs, so no cancellation can occur.

Definition · the least squares regression lineSaylor §10.4

Given a collection of pairs (x, y) of numbers (in which not all the x-values are the same), there is a line ŷ = β̂1x + β̂0 that best fits the data in the sense of minimizing the sum of the squared errors. It is called the least squares regression line. Its slope β̂1 and y-intercept β̂0 are computed using the formulas

β̂1 = SSxy ⁄ SSxx    and    β̂0 = ȳ − β̂1x̄

where SSxx = Σx² − (1 ⁄ n)(Σx)², SSxy = Σxy − (1 ⁄ n)(Σx)(Σy), x̄ is the mean of all the x-values, ȳ is the mean of all the y-values, and n is the number of pairs in the data set.

The numbers β̂1 and β̂0 are statistics that estimate the population parameters β1 and β0.

Before going deeper · our examples, not the book's

How to read a regression line in plain words: four everyday lines

A regression line ŷ = β̂0 + β̂1x answers three questions, and each has a fixed sentence. The slope: "when x is one unit higher, y is on average β̂1 units higher" (lower if β̂1 is negative). The intercept: "the average y when x is 0", which means something only if x = 0 lies inside the data. A prediction: put an x into the line; it gives the average y for that x, not the exact y of one case. Read each line below, say the three sentences aloud, then press the button.

The four lines are made up for practice; their numbers are rounded so that they can be read without a calculator.

Our example · used again in 9.4not in the book

The tea shop: does advertising bring orders?

Lina runs a small online tea shop in Geneva (a made-up business). For eight weeks she varied what she spent on Instagram ads and counted the orders each week. x = ad spend that week, in hundreds of CHF (2 = CHF 200); y = orders that week.

x23456789
y4150516264757486

a. Look at the scatter plot: is the relationship linear, and is it positive or negative? b. Compute the least squares line. c. Say what the slope and the intercept mean for Lina. d. Predict the orders in a week with CHF 550 of ads. e. What does the line say about a week with CHF 3,000 of ads, and should Lina believe it?

Solution

a. The points rise from left to right, close to a straight line: a strong positive linear relationship (r = 0.984).

b. From the eight pairs: Σx = 44, Σy = 503, SSxx = 42, SSxy = 254.5. Slope β̂1 = 254.5 ⁄ 42 = 6.06; intercept β̂0 = ȳ − β̂1x̄ = 62.875 − 6.06 × 5.5 = 29.55. The line: ŷ = 29.55 + 6.06x.

c. The slope: in a week with CHF 100 more of ads, Lina gets on average about 6 more orders. The intercept: 29.55 would be the average orders in a week with no ads at all, but Lina never spent less than CHF 200, so x = 0 is outside her data and the line cannot tell her that number. The intercept only places the line.

d. ŷ = 29.55 + 6.06 × 5.5 = 62.9 orders: the average for weeks with CHF 550 of ads. A single such week could have 58 or 67.

e. At x = 30 the line gives 29.55 + 6.06 × 30 = 211 orders. CHF 3,000 is far outside the CHF 200–900 she tried: that is extrapolation, and she should not believe it. At some point more ads reach the same people again and the orders stop rising.

And one thing the line cannot say. It shows that more ads and more orders went together. It does not prove that the ads caused the orders: if Lina spent more in weeks she already expected to be busy (before Christmas, say), part of the 6 orders belongs to the season, not to the ads. Section 9.4 asks the next question: could a slope of 6.06 appear by chance, if ads did nothing at all?

Key TakeawaysSaylor §10.4
  • How well a straight line fits a data set is measured by the sum of the squared errors.
  • The least squares regression line is the line that best fits the data. Its slope and y-intercept are computed from the data using formulas.
  • The slope β̂1 of the least squares regression line estimates the size and direction of the mean change in the dependent variable y when the independent variable x is increased by one unit.
  • The sum of the squared errors SSE of the least squares regression line can be computed using a formula, without having to compute all the individual errors.
Section 9.4 · Statistical inferences about β1 (§10.5)

Is the slope real?

Learning ObjectivesSaylor §10.5 ↗
  • To learn how to construct a confidence interval for β1, the slope of the population regression line.
  • To learn how to test hypotheses regarding β1.
Before the formulas · in plain words, on the tea shop

"Is the slope real?" asks one thing: could this slope appear by chance if x did nothing?

Lina's eight weeks gave a slope of 6.06 orders per CHF 100. Eight other weeks would give a slightly different slope, because orders also move for reasons that have nothing to do with ads. So the question is: if ads had no effect at all (a true slope β1 of 0), how likely is it that eight weeks would still show a slope as far from 0 as 6.06?

  1. How much does the slope wobble from sample to sample? That is its standard error. Every regression program prints it next to the slope; for the tea shop it is 0.449. You do not compute it by hand: in Excel it is in the output of the regression tool, in the row of x, column "Standard Error".
  2. How many standard errors is the slope away from 0? 6.06 ⁄ 0.449 = 13.5. That is the test statistic T of this section.
  3. Is that far? With 8 − 2 = 6 degrees of freedom, a slope more than 2.447 standard errors from 0 happens by chance less than 5 % of the time when x does nothing. 13.5 is far beyond 2.447: the slope is real, at any usual level.
  4. How large is it, honestly? The 95 % confidence interval: 6.06 ± 2.447 × 0.449 = 4.96 to 7.16 orders per CHF 100. Lina can tell her bank "each CHF 100 of ads goes with between about 5 and 7 more orders a week".
The tea shop8 weeks · slope 6.06 · standard error 0.449 · T = 13.5 → the slope is real.
A second shop (made up)5 weeks · slope 1.2 · standard error 1.5 · T = 0.8, below the 3.182 needed with 3 degrees of freedom → this sample cannot tell whether ads matter at all. Not "no effect": not enough evidence.

The rest of this section is the book's version of the same four steps, with the formulas behind the standard error. The tea shop's numbers: sε = 2.908, SSxx = 42, so sε ⁄ √SSxx = 0.449.

The parameter β1, the slope of the population regression line, is of primary importance in regression analysis because it gives the true rate of change in the mean E(y) in response to a unit increase in the predictor variable x. For every unit increase in x the mean of the response variable y changes by β1 units, increasing if β1 > 0 and decreasing if β1 < 0. We wish to construct confidence intervals for β1 and test hypotheses about it.

Saylor §10.5

FormulaSaylor §10.5

100(1 − α)% Confidence Interval for the Slope β1 of the Population Regression Line

β̂1 ± tα/2 · sε ⁄ √SSxx

where sε = √(SSE ⁄ (n − 2)) and the number of degrees of freedom is df = n − 2. The assumptions listed in Section 10.3 must hold.

The statistic sε is called the sample standard deviation of errors. It estimates the standard deviation σ of the errors in the population of y-values for each fixed value of x.

Our note: in practice you read sε from the regression output (Excel calls it "Standard Error" in the regression statistics), and the whole fraction sε ⁄ √SSxx is printed as the standard error of the slope. The examples below compute it so that you see where it comes from.

Why n − 2. A line has two numbers, a slope and an intercept, and each was estimated from the same n points. Week 6's one-sample t spent one degree of freedom on x̄; a line spends two. With five points only three pieces of information are left to judge the scatter around the line — which is why five-point examples need t from the df = 3 row, and why their intervals are so wide.

Solution

Hypotheses regarding β1 can be tested using the same five-step procedures, either the critical value approach or the p-value approach, that were introduced in Section 8.1 and Section 8.3 of Chapter 8. The null hypothesis always has the form H0: β1 = B0 where B0 is a number determined from the statement of the problem. […] The value zero for B0 is of particular importance since in that case the null hypothesis is H0: β1 = 0, which corresponds to the situation in which x is not useful for predicting y. For if β1 = 0 then the population regression line is horizontal, so the mean E(y) is the same for every value of x and we are just as well off in ignoring x completely and approximating y by its average value. Given two variables x and y, the burden of proof is that x is useful for predicting y, not that it is not. Thus the phrase "test whether x is useful for prediction of y," or words to that effect, means to perform the test H0: β1 = 0 vs. Ha: β1 ≠ 0.

Saylor §10.5 · Testing Hypotheses About β1

FormulaSaylor §10.5

Standardized Test Statistic for Hypothesis Tests Concerning the Slope β1 of the Population Regression Line

T = (β̂1 − B0) ⁄ (sε ⁄ √SSxx)

The test statistic has Student's t-distribution with df = n − 2 degrees of freedom. The assumptions listed in Section 10.3 must hold.

What the denominator of T measures. The slope β̂1 comes from one sample. Another fourteen pizzerias would give another slope. The number sε ⁄ √SSxx is the standard error of the slope: how far the slope typically moves from one sample to the next. It is small when the points sit close to the line (small sε) and when the x-values are spread out (large SSxx), because points far apart along x hold a line steady. T counts how many of these standard errors separate the slope from B0. The degrees of freedom are n − 2 because the line used two numbers estimated from the data, the slope and the intercept, before sε could be computed.

Section 9.5 · The coefficient of determination (§10.6)

How much of y does x explain?

Learning ObjectiveSaylor §10.6 ↗
  • To learn what the coefficient of determination is, how to compute it, and what it tells us about the relationship between two variables x and y.

If the scatter diagram of a set of (x, y) pairs shows neither an upward or downward trend, then the horizontal line ŷ = ȳ fits it well […]. The lack of any upward or downward trend means that when an element of the population is selected at random, knowing the value of the measurement x for that element is not helpful in predicting the value of the measurement y.

The sum of the squared errors computed for the regression line, SSE, is smaller than the sum of the squared errors computed for any other line. In particular it is less than the sum of the squared errors computed using the line ŷ = ȳ, which sum is actually the number SSyy that we have seen several times already. A measure of how useful it is to use the regression equation for prediction of y is how much smaller SSE is than SSyy. […] We can think of SSE ⁄ SSyy as the proportion of the variability in y that cannot be accounted for by the linear relationship between x and y […]. Seen in this light, the coefficient of determination, the complementary proportion of the variability in y, is the proportion of the variability in all the y measurements that is accounted for by the linear relationship between x and y.

Definition · the coefficient of determinationSaylor §10.6

The coefficient of determination of a collection of (x, y) pairs is the number r² computed by any of the following three expressions:

r² = (SSyy − SSE) ⁄ SSyy = SSxy² ⁄ (SSxxSSyy) = β̂1 · SSxy ⁄ SSyy

It measures the proportion of the variability in y that is accounted for by the linear relationship between x and y. If the correlation coefficient r is already known then the coefficient of determination can be computed simply by squaring r, as the notation indicates, r² = (r)².

Section 9.6 · Estimation and prediction (§10.7)

The average pizzeria — or the next one?

Learning ObjectivesSaylor §10.7 ↗
  • To learn the distinction between estimation and prediction.
  • To learn the distinction between a confidence interval and a prediction interval.
  • To learn how to implement formulas for computing confidence intervals and prediction intervals.

Consider the following pairs of problems, in the context of Note 10.19 "Example 3" in Section 10.4, the automobile age and value example.

  1. (a) Estimate the average value of all four-year-old automobiles of this make and model. (b) Construct a 95% confidence interval for the average value of all four-year-old automobiles of this make and model.
  2. (a) Shylock intends to buy a four-year-old automobile of this make and model next week. Predict the value of the first such automobile that he encounters. (b) Construct a 95% confidence interval for the value of the first such automobile that he encounters.

The method of solution and answer to the first question in each pair, (1a) and (2a), are the same. […] The answers to the second part of each question differ. In question (1b) we are trying to estimate a population parameter: the mean of the all the y-values in the sub-population picked out by the value x = 4, that is, the average value of all four-year-old automobiles. In question (2b), however, we are not trying to capture a fixed parameter, but the value of the random variable y in one trial of an experiment […]. In the first case we seek to construct a confidence interval in the same sense that we have done before. In the second case the situation is different, and the interval constructed has a different name, prediction interval.

Saylor §10.7

FormulaSaylor §10.7

100(1 − α)% Confidence Interval for the Mean Value of y at x = xp

ŷp ± tα/2 sε √( 1 ⁄ n + (xp − x̄)² ⁄ SSxx )

where xp is a particular value of x that lies in the range of x-values in the sample data set used to construct the least squares regression line; ŷp is the numerical value obtained when the least square regression equation is evaluated at x = xp; and the number of degrees of freedom for tα/2 is df = n − 2. The assumptions listed in Section 10.3 must hold.

FormulaSaylor §10.7

100(1 − α)% Prediction Interval for an Individual New Value of y at x = xp

ŷp ± tα/2 sε √( 1 + 1 ⁄ n + (xp − x̄)² ⁄ SSxx )

The formula for the prediction interval is identical except for the presence of the number 1 underneath the square root sign. This means that the prediction interval is always wider than the confidence interval at the same confidence level and value of x. In practice the presence of the number 1 tends to make it much wider.

Why the prediction interval never becomes narrow. The confidence interval answers a question about the average price of all pizzerias at a given distance. With more data that average is known more precisely, and the interval shrinks towards zero width. The prediction interval answers a question about one pizzeria. Even if the line were known exactly, one pizzeria still sits above or below it by its own error ε, whose standard deviation is σ. The 1 under the square root is that error. More data cannot remove it, so the prediction interval never gets narrower than about ±tα⁄2sε.
