Session 06 · Friday 20 November 2026 · after Session 5, the same evening

The update you trusted, and putting a number on it: supply-chain attacks and risk assessment

Bring a laptop. Reflection 5 (ENISA 2021) is due before 17:00 today.

Why this session exists. A company can do everything right and still be attacked through software it bought, a provider it pays, or an update it installed because it was told to. The attacker never touches the company directly: it goes through someone the company trusts. This session shows how that works in five real cases, maps the suppliers Arvane trusts, and then asks the question every board asks next: how big is this risk, in money, compared with the others? The answer to that question is what risk frameworks are for.

Two words used all evening. A third party is a supplier that has access to Arvane's systems or data: its IT provider, its software makers, its cloud services. A fourth party is a supplier of that supplier, a company Arvane has no contract with and often does not know exists.

✅ Step 2 · third and fourth parties

Arvane's vendor map: which suppliers can reach inside, and how

Why this step exists. ENISA, the EU's cybersecurity agency, found in its 2021 study (your reading) that in most supply-chain attacks the attacker went after the supplier's code, the software the supplier sends to its customers, and that customers usually trusted it without checking. To protect itself, Arvane first needs to know which suppliers can reach inside, and through which door.

Three doors a supplier can open.

Its code runs at ArvaneThe supplier sends software or updates that Arvane's computers install and run. If the supplier is compromised, the attacker's code runs inside Arvane (SolarWinds, NotPetya, CrowdStrike).
It logs in to ArvaneThe supplier's staff or tools have an account on Arvane's systems, often with administrator rights. If the supplier is compromised, the attacker logs in as the supplier.
It holds Arvane's dataArvane's data sits on the supplier's systems. If the supplier is compromised, Arvane's data leaves without Arvane's systems being touched (MOVEit).

Do this. Read each of Arvane's nine suppliers (fictional, but typical of a trading firm) and choose the door it mainly uses. Each row shows a green answer and the reason when you are right.

Fourth parties: the supplier of the supplier

On 2 July 2021 the REvil gang attacked customers of Kaseya VSA, a tool that IT service providers use to manage their clients' computers. Kaseya counted about 60 service providers and up to 1,500 businesses behind them. One of those businesses was the Swedish supermarket chain Coop: its tills were managed by an IT provider, Visma EssCom, which used Kaseya, and about 800 Coop stores closed for days. For Coop, Visma was a third party and Kaseya a fourth party: Coop had no contract with Kaseya.

The gate every release passes through

A company that ships tens of thousands of software releases a year cannot have a person read each one. Its defence is automated validation: every release passes automatic tests before it reaches customers, and it reaches them in stages, first a small share of computers, then more only if nothing breaks (a staged rollout). After 19 July 2024, CrowdStrike said its content updates would also be deployed in stages; the faulty update had gone to every customer at once. On the buyer's side, the matching defence is a software bill of materials (SBOM): a list of every component inside a product, so that a company can tell in an hour whether it uses, for example, the XZ tool. ✅ Step 3 · putting a number on it

Putting a number on a risk: the risk matrix, the expected loss, and why they disagree

Why this step exists. Arvane's board cannot fix every risk this year. It has to rank them. Most companies do this with a risk matrix: each risk gets a score from 1 to 5 for how likely it is and a score from 1 to 5 for how bad it would be, and the two scores are multiplied. A second way is the expected annual loss: how often per year the event is expected (as a probability) times what one event would cost. This step puts both side by side on four of Arvane's risks.

The matrix bands used here: likelihood 1 = under 1 % a year, 2 = 1–5 %, 3 = 5–20 %, 4 = 20–50 %, 5 = over 50 %; impact 1 = under CHF 0.1 million, 2 = up to 1 million, 3 = up to 5 million, 4 = up to 20 million, 5 = above 20 million. Real companies choose their own bands, which is part of the problem.

Why the matrix misleads. The analyst Tony Cox showed in 2008 that risk matrices can rank risks wrongly, and even in reverse, because they squeeze very different numbers into the same box and then multiply scores that are only ranks ("What's wrong with risk matrices?", Risk Analysis). A risk that is rare and ruinous scores the same as one that is frequent and small. The expected loss keeps the numbers, but it needs a frequency, and for a state attack on a terminal nobody has one: it has happened too rarely to count.

What to do when there is no base rate. A base rate is how often something has happened before, in similar companies. When none exists, risk teams use scenarios: they write out one concrete story of the attack, step by step, ask what each control would change, and estimate the loss of that one story, without pretending to know how often it happens. Session 8's tabletop is such a scenario.

✅ Step 4 · the frameworks in one page

NIST CSF 2.0 and ISO 27001: two frameworks that sort what a company does about cyber risk

Why this step exists. A framework is an agreed list of what a company should do, sorted into headings, so that two companies, an auditor and a regulator can talk about the same things. The midterm expects you to name the two most used ones and say what they are for.

NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0Published by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology in February 2024, free and voluntary, used worldwide. It sorts everything into six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. Version 2.0 added Govern, and put managing suppliers inside it.
ISO/IEC 27001:2022An international standard for an information security management system: the rules, roles and checks a company runs to manage its security risks. A company can be certified against it by an auditor. Its annex lists 93 controls in four themes; controls 5.19 to 5.23 deal with suppliers.

The six NIST functions in one line each. Govern: who decides, how much risk is accepted, what suppliers must do. Identify: know what you have and what could go wrong. Protect: make attacks harder. Detect: notice when something happens. Respond: act during an incident. Recover: get back to work afterwards.
