Commit, before you know

An incident, without its names Early on a Thursday morning, a wind-turbine manufacturer in northern Germany loses remote monitoring and control of about 5,800 of its turbines, roughly 11 gigawatts of capacity spread across Europe. The turbines keep turning on automatic, but nobody can see them or steer them. Thousands of other customers of the same communications service lose their connection at the same moment.

Nobody had broken into the company. Its own systems were never touched.

Guess. You are not expected to know, and a wrong answer written down now is worth more to you than a vague right one.

Once sealed it cannot be edited. That is what makes it a prediction.

What happened

In the first hours of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, a cyberattack disabled tens of thousands of satellite modems on Viasat's KA-SAT network. That network carried communications for Ukrainian authorities and the military, and it also served ordinary customers across Europe. Viasat later explained that the attackers came in through a misconfigured VPN appliance and pushed destructive commands to the modems through the network's own management system. Researchers named the wiping malware AcidRain.

The wind-turbine maker was Enercon. Its turbines reported home through modems on that network. It was not the target. It was collateral, and restoring the links meant replacing modems in the field over the following weeks. On 10 May 2022 the European Union, the United Kingdom and the United States publicly attributed the attack to Russia.

Four things to take from it, all term

  1. Target and victim are different words. The organisation that pays is often not the one that was aimed at. Most of this course is about the companies in the second group.
  2. The dependency was not Enercon's. It did not own the satellite network, did not run it and could not defend it, but it could not work without it. That is critical infrastructure from the user's side, and it is Session 4.
  3. Cyber was part of a war, not a war on its own. The attack came alongside tanks, not instead of them. Whether "cyber war" happens on its own is Session 3's debate.
  4. Attribution took eleven weeks, and it was a political act, not just a technical finding. A company cannot wait eleven weeks to decide what to do.

The threshold game

Eight real incidents. For each one, choose the word you would use for it as the company's lawyer, insurer or board would need to. That matters, because the label decides who pays, whether insurance covers it, and whether a government is expected to respond. There is no answer key. The disagreement in the room is the result.

Do this now · 6 minutes

Pick one word per incident, then press Put my answers on the wall. Go with your first reading; don't search them.

Then, out loud · 15 minutes

We only discuss the incidents where the room split. For each, one person defends each of the two biggest bars. Then the question that matters: if you were the company, which label would you want, and which would your insurer want?

✅ 18:00 · 15 min · Step 4

Three ideas for the term

Everything above you worked out for yourselves. Here is the vocabulary for it, and the map of the next thirteen sessions.

1 · The threshold

Most hostile state activity today is designed to stay below the level that would justify a military response. It is ambiguous enough to deny, gradual enough that no single step looks decisive, and often carried out through proxies, criminals or "accidents". Michael Mazarr calls this the gray zone. It is the subject of Session 2 and your first reading. Several of the incidents you just sorted were built to be hard to label: the difficulty of labelling them is part of the design.

2 · Asymmetry, in both directions

The attacker can be small, cheap and far away, and the defender large, expensive and exposed everywhere. You will often hear that the defender has to be right every time and the attacker only once. That is half true. An intrusion is a chain of steps (reconnaissance, access, moving inside, acting on the goal), and the defender wins by breaking any one link. This is the "kill chain" idea from Session 3. Knowing which half applies to a given threat is the technical risk literacy this course promises.

3 · The enterprise as the front line

Private companies own and run most of what states fight over: cables, satellites, software, ports, pipelines. When states fight, companies pay. Sometimes they are the target. More often they are collateral, like Enercon, or Maersk in 2017. And the label matters financially: since 2023 the Lloyd's market has required its standalone cyber policies to exclude losses from state-backed cyberattacks. Whether something was "war" is a question with a price.

The arc of the courseSessions 1–6 ask what threatens an organisation and through what: the gray zone, state cyber operations, cables and satellites, software supply chains, and how to put a number on risk. Session 7 is the midterm. Sessions 8–13 ask what the organisation does about it: resilience, a live crisis exercise, governance and regulators, physical protection, partnership with the state, and real company cases. Session 14 is the final. The full plan is on the course page.

✅ 18:45 · 20 min · Step 6

Be the adversary

Each team now plays one of Arvane's possible adversaries. Your profile follows from your team letter. The question is not how to hack anything. It is strategic: what is the cheapest way for this actor to get what it wants from Arvane, and how would it stay deniable? Stay at the level of a board briefing, not a technical manual.

1. What it wants. 2. Which of Arvane's assets it goes through. 3. What it costs the adversary, roughly: a week of a small team, or a year of a state agency? 4. What it costs Arvane, and whether anyone could prove who did it.
Cost to the attacker against cost to Arvane. Which way does it cut for your adversary?
