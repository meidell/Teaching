✅ 17:10 · 12 min · Step 2

Mazarr, applied

Mazarr's Summary defines a gray-zone strategy by four marks. In his words, it is "a form of conflict that pursues political objectives through integrated campaigns"; it relies mainly on non-military or non-kinetic tools; it is built to stay under key escalation thresholds so that no conventional war follows; and it moves gradually rather than seeking conclusive results in a short time (Mazarr, 2015, Summary).

1 · Political objective, as a campaignNot one act but a sequence, all pointing at one result: a territory, an alliance decision, a government's choice.
2 · Mostly non-military toolsLaw, money, media, proxies, "accidents", cyber intrusions, coast guards instead of navies.
3 · Below the thresholdEvery step is calibrated to the defender's known red lines. A public red line is a map for the attacker.
4 · GradualNo single step is worth a war. The sum of the steps is the objective.

Gray zone is therefore not a synonym for "anything short of war". A crime without a political aim is not gray zone; an open invasion is above it; a transparent diplomatic protest has nothing to hide. Test the definition on six cases. Each one has a defensible answer, and the reason appears when you pick.

✅ 17:45 · 15 min · Step 4

One instrument, in detail: GNSS

Three of the eight slices involved a satellite navigation signal. It is the most common gray-zone instrument Arvane's vessels already meet, and understanding it properly is what separates a board briefing from a rumour. The mechanism, stage by stage.

How a receiver knows where it is

Each GNSS satellite (GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, BeiDou) broadcasts its own orbital position and a timestamp from an atomic clock, on a fixed frequency: for GPS L1 and Galileo E1 it is 1575.42 MHz. A receiver measures when at least four of those signals arrive. Light covers about 30 cm per nanosecond, so each arrival time becomes a distance, called a pseudorange; four pseudoranges solve four unknowns, the receiver's x, y, z and its own clock error. The satellites orbit at about 20,200 km, so a signal takes roughly 67 ms to arrive, and when it does it is extraordinarily faint: around −130 dBm, about 10−16 W, below the receiver's own thermal noise. The receiver can only find it because it knows the satellite's spreading code in advance and correlates the incoming noise against it, which lifts the signal out by about 43 dB of processing gain.

Jamming: being louder

Anything transmitting on or near 1575.42 MHz that arrives at the antenna louder than the satellites denies the fix. How much louder a jammer arrives follows from the free-space path loss, FSPL (dB) = 20 log10 d + 20 log10 f − 147.55, with d in metres and f in hertz. At 10 km a 1 W jammer, 30 dBm, loses 116 dB on the way and arrives at −86 dBm: about 44 dB, or twenty-three thousand times, stronger than the signal it is drowning. The receiver's tracking loops lose lock. On the bridge the symptom is a position that freezes or disappears, and an alarm on the electronic chart.

Link budget · move the sliders
Free-space loss at 1575.42 MHz, no terrain, no antenna gain either side; the GNSS signal taken as −130 dBm at the antenna. A real jammer with an antenna and a clear horizon does better than this; terrain and the curve of the earth do worse.

Spoofing: being plausible

A spoofer does not shout. It broadcasts counterfeit satellite signals, correctly formatted, carrying a false time and position, a few dB stronger than the real ones. The receiver's loops lock onto the stronger copy and report a fix with no alarm at all. Done carefully, the false position is then walked away from the true one slowly enough that nothing looks wrong, which is called pull-off. A cruder variant, meaconing, records real signals and rebroadcasts them with a delay. The best public dataset is C4ADS's Above Us Only Stars (2019): 9,883 suspected spoofing instances affecting 1,311 civilian vessels between February 2016 and November 2018, with ships' positions placed on airfields, notably Gelendzhik on the Black Sea. The pattern fitted a system built to keep drones away from protected persons; the ships were, once again, collateral.

Aviation shows the same ambiguity with a date on it. In April 2024 Finnair suspended its Helsinki–Tartu route for a month after two aircraft could not complete GNSS-dependent approaches and diverted; Estonia's foreign minister attributed the interference to Russia; Finnair said it did not know its source. A non-GNSS approach was installed and the flights resumed. The company acted; the attribution never arrived.

What else breaks

  • Time. GNSS is the clock for AIS transmission slots, for mobile base stations, for financial timestamps and for the terminal's control-system logs. A spoofed clock corrupts evidence.
  • AIS. A vessel's broadcast position is GNSS-derived, so every port, insurer and competitor watching the map sees the lie too.
  • The chart and the autopilot. ECDIS, dynamic positioning and track control all consume the fix.
  • Trust. A bridge that has been spoofed once distrusts every instrument afterwards, which is itself a cost.

Where the defender breaks the chain

  1. Physics checks. A signal that is too strong is a tell: real GNSS is weak by nature, so receivers monitor carrier-to-noise ratio. An inertial unit knows a 40 km jump in one second is impossible.
  2. Diversity. Two constellations and two frequencies (L1 and L5, E1 and E5) force the attacker to counterfeit everything at once.
  3. Geometry. Real satellites are spread across the sky; counterfeit ones all come from one direction. Controlled-reception-pattern antennas detect that.
  4. Authentication. Galileo's OSNMA signs its navigation message, so a receiver can reject an unsigned one.
  5. Procedure. Radar, visual bearings, dead reckoning, a trained master. The cheapest link to harden, and the one Arvane can act on this month.
The asymmetry, statedA jammer costs less than a bridge chair; the hull it endangers is worth two hundred million. But items 1, 2 and 5 are cheap too. This is the Session 1 argument in one instrument: the attacker needs every stage to work, and the defender needs one check to hold. Which side the asymmetry favours depends on whether the defender has done anything at all.

✅ 18:30 · 20 min · Step 6

Arvane's tripwires

The game showed the line moving with every slice. The fix is to write it down before the first one. This is the first page of the crisis protocol the course ends with (Session 9 plays it; Session 14 examines it). Three parts, written by the Scribe, argued by the Analyst, attacked by the Adversary.

Three kinds of event Arvane records, dates, and does not escalate on their own. Say what "log" means: where, by whom, in what format a lawyer or insurer could later use.
Three conditions that trigger action by themselves, no meeting needed. Make them observable: a number, a count, a duration. "Two GNSS incidents on the same vessel in a week" is a tripwire; "serious interference" is not.
For each tripwire: the board, the insurer, the banks, the NCSC in Bern, the terminal's Romanian regulator under NIS2, the press. Who decides at 02:00 on a Saturday, and who is never told?
Last five minutes

Each Scribe reads the three tripwires only, under a minute. The room listens for one thing: which tripwire would the attacker in the game simply stay under? A tripwire that can be calibrated against is a map, not a defence.
