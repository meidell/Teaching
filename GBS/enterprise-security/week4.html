✅ Step 2 · 25 min · kill-chain stages 1–3

The way in: a link, a password

Most intrusions do not begin with clever code. They begin with a person giving away something that opens a door: a password typed into the wrong page, or a document opened that runs code. Colonial Pipeline, which shut down the fuel supply of the US East Coast in May 2021, was entered through one password for an old VPN account that had no second factor, as its chief executive told the US Senate; Mandiant, which investigated, said the password had turned up in a batch of leaked passwords. Three gadgets, each one piece of that door.

1 · Who does a link really belong to?

A web address is read from the right. The part that decides who owns the page is the registered domain: the last label before the ending (.ch, .com) plus that ending. Everything to its left is a subdomain, which the owner of that domain can name anything at all, including arvane.ch. Paste or pick a link; the highlighted part is the owner.

URL dissector runs locally

The fourth example deserves a second look. It is printed with a Cyrillic а, which looks exactly like a Latin a. The browser has to turn it into plain ASCII to look it up (the xn-- form), and that is the only place the difference shows. Modern browsers display the xn-- form when scripts are mixed in a suspicious way; an email client often does not.

The second factor, and how it is beatenA one-time code from an app stops a stolen password from being enough. Two techniques get around it, and both are routine. The relay: the fake page sits between the victim and the real login page, passes the password and the code through in real time, and keeps the session cookie the real site hands back. With that cookie the intruder is logged in and never needs the code again. Fatigue: the intruder triggers push approvals again and again, at night, until the tired victim taps "approve". The defences are a second factor that is bound to the real site's address (a security key or passkey, which a relay page cannot use) and push prompts that require typing a number shown on screen.

3 · How long does a password last?

When a company's password database leaks, the attacker does not get the passwords; they get hashes, one-way fingerprints of them. They then guess: hash a candidate, compare, repeat. How long that takes depends on how many candidates there are and how fast each guess is. Type a made-up password (never a real one). Nothing leaves this page.

Guessing time illustrative speeds, orders of magnitude
Here is the SHA-256 hash of what you typed — the one-way fingerprint a breach hands the attacker instead of your password. Change one character and the whole fingerprint changes, yet it stays the same length however long the password is, and nothing in it spells out the letters.

The meter's lesson is in its last row. A long random password guessed offline holds for centuries; any password used on another site that leaked holds for zero seconds, because the attacker simply tries the leaked pair everywhere (credential stuffing). That is why the defences that matter are a second factor bound to the site, a password manager so that nothing is reused, and slow hashing on the company's side.

✅ Step 3 · 25 min · kill-chain stage 4

The break-in: making a program do what it was never meant to

Exploitation means feeding a program input its author did not expect, so that the input is treated as instructions rather than data. Every exploit in the news, from a website's login form to the Windows flaw NotPetya used, is a version of that confusion. Two of the oldest and still most common, then the question that decides whether either matters: how fast the hole is closed.

1 · SQL injection: the login form that logs anyone in

Arvane's supplier portal checks a login by building one sentence for its database out of what you type, and asking it for a matching row. Type a name and a password and watch the sentence being built. Then try the three inputs under the form. The database below is a real, if tiny, interpreter: it reads the sentence exactly as written.

The o'brien try shows why this bug is so common. To look you up, the site wraps your name in quote marks and glues it into the sentence, so the name has to start with a quote and end with a quote. The name o'brien carries an apostrophe — a quote mark — in the middle, so the database now sees three quote marks where it expected two, loses track of where the name ends, and errors out. That is an ordinary customer with an Irish surname, not an attacker; the attacker simply does the same slip on purpose, adding ' OR '1'='1 to turn the broken sentence into one that matches everyone.

Suppose the developer reacts by deleting every apostrophe people type. That blocks this one trick and locks out Mr o'brien, but it does not fix the real problem: the text you typed was glued into the sentence at all. The real fix is to never build the sentence out of your text. The site writes the sentence once, with a blank where the name goes, and hands your name to the database separately, labelled "this is only a value to match, never part of the instruction." The database then compares it as data and can never run it as a command — this is the parameterised query, the checkbox marked "the developer's fix" above. Every database tool has offered it for twenty years.

Where an attacker actually types this

There is no special hacker screen for this. The attacker types the trick into the ordinary boxes the website already offers everyone, and very often into the web address itself. A website asks its database a question every time you log in, search, or open a page that shows one item. Anywhere the site takes what you supplied and builds a database sentence from it, this trick can be tried — a login box, a search field, a "find my order" form.

The web address is one of those boxes. Many pages carry a value inside the address, after the question mark. That part is called the query string: the site reads it to decide what to show you. When you open one supplier's page, the address looks like this, and the 4071 is the supplier number the site then drops straight into its database sentence, exactly as the login form dropped your name into it:

https://arvane.ch/supplier?id=4071

The site returns supplier number 4071.

So the attacker edits that value in the address bar — no login needed — and presses Enter:

https://arvane.ch/supplier?id=4071' OR '1'='1' --

The same confusion of code and data happens, and the condition is true for every row, so the page returns the whole supplier list instead of one. Nothing was used but the browser's address bar.

Anyone on the internet can reach that address, that login box and that search field, so attackers run automated tools that fire thousands of these inputs at thousands of sites. That is why the fix — keep what the visitor supplied as data, never as part of the sentence — has to cover every place the site reads input, not the login alone.

2 · A memory bug: twenty characters that make you an administrator

Picture the computer's memory as a long row of numbered boxes, each holding one character, standing side by side with no wall between them. To do its job the program sets boxes aside in advance: here it reserves the first 16 boxes for your user name, which is what "16 bytes" means — room for 16 characters. The very next box is a yes/no switch called is_admin, where 0 means "treat this person as an ordinary user, not an administrator". The boxes after that hold a note telling the program where to carry on once it has finished this task.

Now the catch. The routine that writes your name copies one character into one box and keeps going down the row; in C and C++ it is the programmer's job to make it stop at box 16, and this one forgot. So if you type a name of 20 characters, the first 16 fill the name boxes and the last 4 spill straight past them into the is_admin switch and the note beyond it, writing over whatever was there. You were never shown an "administrator" option, yet by typing a long enough name you can flip that switch to yes — only because the program trusted that your name would fit. This is a buffer overflow: a buffer is the set-aside boxes, and the overflow is your input running off the end of them into what sits next door. Type a longer name below and watch the characters cross into the switch.

Overwriting a flag is the gentle version. Overwriting the return address is the serious one: the program then jumps wherever the attacker's bytes point, and runs the attacker's instructions with the program's own rights. Modern systems add hurdles (randomised addresses, memory that cannot be executed), and attackers chain several bugs to clear them. Microsoft's security response centre reported in 2019 that about 70 % of the vulnerabilities it fixes each year are memory-safety bugs of this family, which is why governments now urge vendors to move to memory-safe languages.

How a cyber-pirate uses this

In the demo you typed into a name box, but the set-aside boxes exist anywhere a program reads a chunk of input of some length — the name on a login, a file someone opens, a photo, a document, or a message that arrives over the network. The attacker almost never sits at Arvane's keyboard. Instead they send the vulnerable program a specially-built chunk of data where it expects an ordinary one, and they make it longer than the boxes set aside for it.

Because the program copies that input without checking its length, the attacker's extra characters run off the end, and the attacker chooses exactly what those characters are. The gentle move sets the is_admin switch to yes, as you just did. The dangerous move writes over the "where to continue next" note so that it points at instructions the attacker hid inside the very same input. The program then stops doing its own task and starts carrying out the attacker's commands, with the program's own rights.

Often nothing has to be clicked. A booby-trapped document emailed to Arvane's staff fires the overflow the moment it is opened; a crafted message sent to a server that Arvane exposes to the internet fires the moment the server reads it, with no login at all. If that server runs with administrator rights, the attacker is now running commands as the administrator of that machine — reading its data, installing more tools, reaching the next machine along. Taking a program over from across the network with one crafted input like this is called remote code execution, and it is the most prized bug of all: the 2017 worms in the patch-gap chart below used a memory bug of this family to jump from machine to machine over the network, while people slept.

A real case — WannaCry, May 2017. The part of Windows that lets office computers share folders and printers had exactly this kind of overflow, in the code that reads a message coming from the network. An unknown group built a worm — a program that copies itself from one computer to the next — which sent that service a crafted message, longer than the boxes set aside, with its own instructions riding in the overflow. Nobody had to click anything; a computer only had to be switched on and reachable. The smuggled instructions did two things: they locked up the machine's files and demanded about $300 in Bitcoin to unlock them, and they sent the same crafted message on to every computer they could reach, so the worm spread on its own. Within days it hit about 200,000 computers in roughly 150 countries (Europol estimate, May 2017), and in Britain it froze hospital systems and forced about 19,500 medical appointments and operations to be cancelled (UK National Audit Office, 2017).

Here is the part to remember as a manager. Microsoft had already published the fix two months earlier, on 14 March 2017 — it is the WannaCry bar in the chart just below. Every computer that had installed that update was untouched, and all of the damage fell on machines that had not yet applied a patch that already existed. The flaw was clever; the reason it became a global disaster was ordinary — the gap between a fix being available and a fix being installed, which is exactly what the next section measures.

3 · The patch gap

A bug that has been fixed is only fixed on machines that installed the fix. The time between the patch and the installation is the attacker's window, and it is measured in weeks. Three real windows, drawn to scale:

Patch published → attack days

Microsoft bulletin MS17-010, 14 Mar 2017 · WannaCry, 12 May 2017 · NotPetya, 27 Jun 2017 · Apache Struts fix for CVE-2017-5638, 7 Mar 2017; Equifax intruders in from 13 May 2017, records of about 147 million people taken (Equifax's final count; timeline from US GAO-18-559, 2018).

WannaCry — 59 days. This is the worm from the box above. Microsoft published the fix on 14 March 2017; the worm broke out on 12 May 2017 and spread by itself to about 200,000 computers in roughly 150 countries. Every machine still unpatched 59 days after the fix was a target, British hospitals among them.
NotPetya — 105 days. On 27 June 2017 malware dressed up as ransomware was pushed out through a hijacked update of a Ukrainian tax-accounting program that almost every firm trading in Ukraine had installed. It used the same Windows file-sharing flaw as WannaCry, plus passwords stolen from each machine it landed on, to race across whole company networks in minutes — and it was built to destroy, not to collect: paying the ransom unlocked nothing. It spread from Ukraine into multinationals; the shipping line Maersk had to rebuild about 4,000 servers and 45,000 PCs in ten days, and the worldwide damage was later put at about $10 billion (White House assessment reported by Wired, 2018), the costliest cyberattack on record. The fix had been available for 105 days.
Equifax — 67 days. Equifax, a United States credit bureau that holds the financial histories of hundreds of millions of people, ran a public website where consumers disputed what was on their record. That site was built on Apache Struts, a widely used web framework whose makers published a fix for a remote-code-execution flaw — the same "a crafted request runs commands on the server" problem — on 7 March 2017. Equifax left that one server unpatched. 67 days later, around 13 May 2017, attackers walked in through it and stayed undetected for about ten weeks, taking records on about 147 million people: names, Social Security numbers, birth dates and addresses (US Government Accountability Office, GAO-18-559, 2018).
A zero-day — 0 days. The other three rows each had a fix that was sitting there waiting to be installed. A zero-day is the case where no fix exists yet, because the company that makes the software does not even know its program has the flaw — and you cannot repair a hole nobody has found. The name says how much warning the defender gets: zero days. The attacker found the flaw first and uses it before anyone else knows about it, so on that day there is simply nothing to download, and even a company that installs every patch the moment it appears is still exposed. Finding a brand-new flaw in well-tested software takes rare skill and months of work, or a great deal of money on a grey market, and the first time it is used it tends to be noticed and fixed — so it is a one-shot weapon that is used up once fired. That cost is why zero-days are saved for high-value targets and mostly used by governments, not sprayed at ordinary companies. Stuxnet — the sabotage program that the United States and Israel are widely reported to have used to wreck the centrifuges enriching uranium at Iran's Natanz plant in 2010 — chained four zero-days at once, which was almost unheard of and a fingerprint of a government-scale effort. For a company like Arvane the lesson runs the other way: nearly every real incident uses a flaw whose patch already existed and was simply not installed in time — so what protects you is not whether a fix exists, but how fast you install it.

✅ Step 4 · 25 min · kill-chain stages 5–6

Inside: how one machine becomes every machine

The first machine an intruder controls is rarely the one they want. They want the domain controller, the server that holds every account and can push software to every computer, and from there the file servers, the backups and, at Arvane, the terminal's control systems. Getting there is called lateral movement, and it mostly uses no exploit at all. Windows keeps the credentials of whoever logged on recently in memory; a free tool (Mimikatz, published in 2011) reads them out; the intruder logs on to the next machine as that person. NotPetya carried exactly such a credential reader, which is how it reached machines that had been patched.

Lateral movement
Why each machine falls — and the switch that closes itAll five situations below are ordinary in a real office, and each matches one checkbox in the panel. (1) Every PC was given the same local-administrator password when it was first set up, so one stolen password opens all of them → give every PC its own password. (2) An IT administrator set up Lucie's scanner last week using his powerful domain-administrator account, and Windows left that account's password sitting in her PC's memory, so the intruder picks it up from her machine → let admins sign in only from a separate admin-only PC, so their password is never left on an ordinary one. (3) The terminal administrator signs in to the control system from his normal office PC, leaving his password there too → same fix as (2). (4) The terminal's control computers share one login system (one "domain") with the office, so an office admin account can log straight into them → keep the terminal network separate, with its own accounts. (5) The backups sit on a server that administrators can log on to, so whoever takes the admin account takes the backups as well → keep one copy offline, out of reach of any login. No single switch keeps the intruder on Lucie's PC, and no pair is enough on its own either — you are hunting for the one set that closes every way out of her machine at the same time.

2 · The beacon: find the machine that is calling home

An installed backdoor has to receive instructions. It does so by calling out, because outgoing web traffic is allowed almost everywhere: every so often it asks an outside server "anything for me?". ✅ Step 5 · 20 min · kill-chain stage 7

The payloads: lock, wipe, flood, cut

Everything so far was preparation. The last stage is the one that makes the news, and it comes in four kinds.

1 · Ransomware, and the wiper wearing its mask

Ransomware does not damage files; it encrypts them with a key only the attacker holds. The encryption is the same mathematics that protects online banking, which is exactly why it cannot be broken. Below are three Arvane files. The page will encrypt them with real AES-256, in your browser.

Constanța terminal · file share plain

In real ransomware the file key is itself locked with the attacker's public key, so the only thing that can unlock it is a private key that never leaves the attacker; that is the product sold for the ransom. Two refinements matter to a company. Double extortion: since around 2019 the gangs copy the data out before encrypting it, so a company with perfect backups still faces publication. And the wiper: NotPetya displayed a ransom note, but the identifier it showed the victim was random, so even the attacker could not have produced the right key. Paying bought nothing. A wiper wearing ransomware's mask buys the attacker deniability and the victim nothing.

2 · The flood: turning a small request into a large one

A denial-of-service attack sends more traffic than a link can carry. The cheap way is amplification: the attacker sends small questions to public servers on the internet, with the victim's address forged as the sender, and the servers send their much larger answers to the victim. The multiplier depends on the service. Set the attacker's own bandwidth and pick a service.

Amplification factors: CISA alert TA14-017A
Attacker's own upload ·

The record at the time it happened: on 28 February 2018 GitHub was hit by 1.35 terabits per second of memcached amplification. It was unreachable for about five minutes, until its traffic was routed through a scrubbing service large enough to absorb it. The defence against floods is almost never on the victim's own premises: it is capacity rented from someone larger.

3 · Four weapons that are not code

The anchorA ship drags its anchor across a seabed cable or pipeline. Balticconnector, October 2023: the Finnish investigation found the anchor of the Newnew Polar Bear, recovered from the seabed beside the damaged pipe. Cheap, deniable, and slow to repair. Session 5.
The jammerA transmitter of a few watts drowns the satellite navigation signal. Session 2 measured it: a 1 W jammer at 10 km arrives about 23,000 times stronger than the satellites. Spoofing goes further and sends a false position.
The insiderAn employee, contractor or cleaner with legitimate access does what no outsider can: carries a USB stick across an air gap (Stuxnet's probable route), photographs a screen, or approves a payment.
The storyDisinformation is a payload too: a forged document about Arvane's cargo, released the day a contract is signed, does damage without touching a computer. Session 7.
