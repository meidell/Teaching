✅ Step 2

The kill chain: the defender's map

An intruder who wants to do damage inside a company cannot do it in one move. They must complete a sequence of steps, and each step depends on the one before. In 2011 three analysts at Lockheed Martin wrote that sequence down as the intrusion kill chain and made one argument from it: the defender does not have to stop the last step, the damage. Breaking any one earlier step stops the whole operation, and the earlier the cheaper. The seven stages, in order (Hutchins, Cloppert & Amin, 2011):

Click a stage to see what a defender can do there. The intruder must win every stage; the defender needs to win once. That asymmetry is the opposite of the one in Session 2, and it is the reason security work concentrates on the early, cheap stages — a blocked attachment, a patched server, an account that cannot be reused — rather than on catching the damage as it happens.

The matrix behind the mapFor each stage the original paper lists six defensive actions — detect, deny, disrupt, degrade, deceive, destroy. Patching, for example, denies the exploitation stage outright; a firewall rule denies delivery; an intrusion-detection system only detects. A defender's real question is not "are we secure" but "at which stage, with which action, do we stop this particular intruder, and what does that cost us versus them".

e.g. "Delivery — deny, by blocking macro attachments at the mail gateway". One line.

What the three share, and what separates themAll three reached actions on objectives because an early stage held: a trusted USB, a trusted vendor update, a trusted remote-access path. The difference is the objective. Stuxnet aimed at one target and stayed there. The grid attack switched a service off and then back on. NotPetya aimed at a country and could not be contained — the same stages, but with no brake on the last one. That difference is exactly what the week's question turns on.

✅ Step 4

Rid's three tests

Thomas Rid's claim, from the reading, is that an act of war has three properties at once, and that no cyber attack has ever had all three. Following Clausewitz, an act of war is violent (it does, or threatens, physical harm), instrumental (it is a means to a political end, not an end in itself), and political (it is carried out by, or attributed to, a political actor, openly enough to carry a message). Short of all three, Rid says, what looks like "cyber war" is really one of three older things: sabotage, espionage or subversion (Rid, 2012).

ViolentPhysical harm to people or property, or a credible threat of it. Data loss alone does not count; a wrecked centrifuge or a blacked-out hospital does.
InstrumentalA means to an end: force, or the threat of it, to make the other side do something. Not theft for its own sake, not disruption for its own sake.
PoliticalDone by a state or its proxy, and attributable — an act of war has a return address, because its purpose is to send a message.

Score each case on the three tests. A case is an act of war only if it scores yes on all three; miss one and Rid would file it as sabotage, espionage or subversion. The verdict column updates as you click. There is no single key — the fourth and fifth rows are exactly where the room will disagree, which is the discussion.

Then, out loud

And if NotPetya was not an act of war because it was not instrumental — nobody was being coerced — then what was it? Hold that answer; the insurers needed it too.

✅ Step 5

"Act of war", in a contract

The question stops being academic the moment an insurer uses it to refuse a claim. Every property policy excludes loss from war. When NotPetya hit companies that had nothing to do with any war, their insurers invoked that exclusion — and the definition Rid argued about became a dispute worth hundreds of millions of francs.

The pattern: courts read a war exclusion written for armies narrowly, and declined to stretch "warlike action" to a cyber attack on a company that was not a combatant. The insurance market's answer was not to argue the old words again but to rewrite them — from 31 March 2023 Lloyd's of London required standalone cyber policies to carry an explicit state-backed cyber-attack exclusion, with an agreed way to decide attribution. The lesson for a buyer of insurance is blunt: the word "war" in your own policy may not mean what you think, and the only safe time to find out is before the loss.

Arvane's positionArvane carries property, marine and a new cyber policy. If a NotPetya-style worm, released in a war Arvane is not part of, encrypts the Constanța terminal's systems and halts trading for a week, which policy pays — and does the war exclusion apply? The team writes the clause it would want before renewal, knowing the attribution may never be certain.

Three short lines. 1 When does Arvane expect to be paid even though a state was behind the attack? 2 What single exclusion would it accept — and how is "state-backed" to be decided, and by whom, when attribution is contested? 3 One number or trigger in the policy Arvane can measure (e.g. a waiting period, a sub-limit), so the clause is a rule, not a judgement.
To finish

One person per team reads line 2 aloud. The room listens for one thing: who decides whether an attack was "state-backed", when the attacker denies it and attribution takes years? If the answer is "the insurer, alone", the exclusion can swallow the cover. That is the gap Rid's argument leaves in a contract.
