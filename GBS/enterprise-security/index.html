What the course is about

Three ideas, all term

The threshold. Most hostile state activity today is designed to stay below the level that would trigger a military response: ambiguous, deniable, gradual. Mazarr calls this space the gray zone. It is where most of what we study takes place, and where international law has the least to say.

Asymmetry. An attack can cost a few thousand francs and the defence many millions. A small actor can hold a large one at risk. But the attacker has to get every step right, and the defender can win by breaking any one of them. Both statements are true, and the course teaches you to tell which one applies.

The enterprise as the front line. Private companies own and run most of the infrastructure that states fight over: cables, satellites, software, ports, grids. When states fight, companies pay. Often they pay as bystanders, with insurance policies that exclude acts of war.

How it is taught

  • Predict, then reveal. Real incidents, introduced without their names, and you commit to a reading of them before the facts arrive.
  • Short input, long work. No more than twenty minutes of me in any session. The rest is the case, the debate and the exercise.
  • Teams with roles. Analyst, Adversary (who thinks like the attacker, on purpose) and Scribe, rotating every week.
  • A tabletop in December. Session 9 is a 72-hour crisis played in two hours, against the clock, with injects you do not see coming.
  • Break my claim. Each session ends with one falsifiable statement from me. A real counterexample the following week counts toward the discussion.

This is a course for decision-makers, not engineers. You will not write code or attack anything. You will learn enough of how the technology works to know when someone is selling you fear, and when you should be afraid.

The running case

Arvane Commodities SA · Geneva

A mid-sized trading house on the Rue du Rhône: about 380 staff, grain and refined products, offices in Geneva, Singapore and Dubai, a grain terminal it owns at Constanța on the Black Sea, and twelve chartered vessels that navigate by satellite and talk to shore by satellite. Its trading floor runs on market data, a cloud ERP and a trade-finance platform it did not build. Its chief executive thinks security is the IT department's problem.

Arvane is fictional, so that its weaknesses can be planted where the course needs them. Everything around it is real: the Black Sea, the GNSS jamming, the Red Sea cables, the sanctions regimes, the software it depends on. Each session adds one layer to its threat picture.

The weekly reflection, exactly as the syllabus sets it

  • Pick one phrase, one to five lines, from the session's required reading that captures a core aspect of the text. Quote it with a proper reference: (Author, date, p.).
  • Write at most 300 words: why that phrase is crucial to the rest of the text, and what the author is trying to say. One important aspect, not a summary of the whole text.
  • Submit it before the class begins. It is submitted on that session's page here. Absent? Submit anyway, because you can still be picked. A medical justification to the Registrar is the only exemption.
  • Each week three are picked at random and graded, and their authors open the discussion. Submitted on time means at least 50/100. Not submitted means 0/100, whether or not you were picked. Your 10% is the average of your graded reflections.

The exams, and how they are marked

Both exams use the same scheme: 50 points for concise answers that show knowledge of the fundamental concepts, with explicit reference to the material covered in class; 30 for applying that knowledge to discuss and critically analyse; 20 for a clear, well-substantiated answer in a logical flow.

In practice, "explicit reference to the material covered in class" means naming the case, the reading or the framework. "It depends on the threat" earns little. "NotPetya shows that a firm can be destroyed as collateral, which is why…" earns a lot. Every session page ends with what you should be able to say about it in an exam.

Grading scale (Master's): A 93–100 · A− 90–92 · B+ 87–89 · B 83–86 · B− 80–82 · C+ 77–79 · C 73–76 · F below 73.
