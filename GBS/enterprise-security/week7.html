Session 07 · Friday 27 November 2026 · the first session of part two

Hybrid threats and resilient firms: pressure that mixes cyber, money, law and lies, and how a company prepares for it

Bring a laptop. Reflection 6 (European Commission 2016) is due before 17:00 today.

Why this session exists. Sessions 1 to 6 looked at one weapon at a time: a jammer, a phishing mail, a cut cable, a poisoned update. A state that wants something from a company rarely uses only one. It combines a cyber attack with a bank that suddenly asks questions, a lawsuit in a foreign court and a false story in the press, so that each part looks small and the company's managers argue about what is happening while it happens. This session names that pattern, shows it in real cases, plays it against Arvane, and then turns to the answer: resilience, which a company builds before the crisis, not during it.

Two words used all evening. A hybrid threat is pressure that mixes several kinds of means (cyber, economic, legal, informational, military) on purpose, to reach a goal without open war. Resilience is a company's ability to keep doing its essential work when something fails, and to get back to normal quickly afterwards.

✅ Step 2 · what a hybrid threat is

What the EU means by a hybrid threat, and eight real pressures sorted by the instrument they used

Why this step exists. A company cannot defend against something it cannot name. Your reading gives the definition the EU uses, and the final exam expects you to use it. The definition is long, so this step takes it apart into four marks, and then sorts eight real events by the instrument each one used.

"While definitions of hybrid threats vary and need to remain flexible to respond to their evolving nature, the concept aims to capture the mixture of coercive and subversive activity, conventional and unconventional methods (i.e. diplomatic, military, economic, technological), which can be used in a coordinated manner by state or non-state actors to achieve specific objectives while remaining below the threshold of formally declared warfare. There is usually an emphasis on exploiting the vulnerabilities of the target and on generating ambiguity to hinder decision-making processes."European Commission and High Representative (2016), Joint Framework on countering hybrid threats, JOIN(2016) 18 final, 6 April 2016, section 1

The four marks in that definition.

1 · Several instrumentsThe attacker mixes diplomatic, military, economic and technological means. One instrument alone, for example a single cyber attack, is not yet hybrid.
2 · Coordinated on purposeThe instruments serve one goal and are timed together. Two unrelated problems in the same month are bad luck, not a hybrid campaign.
3 · Below declared warThe attacker stays under the level that would trigger a military answer or an alliance's defence clause. This is the gray zone of Session 2 (Mazarr 2015).
4 · Weak spots and confusionThe attacker looks for the target's weak points and keeps it unsure of what is happening, because a target that cannot agree on the facts decides too late.

The same framework set up two bodies that are still running. The EU Hybrid Fusion Cell, inside the EU's intelligence and situation centre (EU INTCEN), collects warnings from the EU institutions and the member states so that someone sees the pieces together. The European Centre of Excellence for Countering Hybrid Threats (Hybrid CoE) was set up in Helsinki in 2017 for research and exercises. The framework lists 22 actions in all.

Do this. Sort eight real events by the main instrument each one used. Each row shows a green answer and the reason when you are right. Some are less obvious than they look.

How the Lithuania case unfolded. Taiwan opened a representative office in Vilnius on 18 November 2021, under the name "Taiwanese" rather than "Taipei". China downgraded its diplomatic relations with Lithuania. According to the European Commission, from December 2021 Chinese customs rejected goods from Lithuania, imports were restricted for multinational companies that used parts made in Lithuania, and Chinese exports to Lithuania were cut; from February 2022 China banned Lithuanian alcohol, beef, dairy, logs and peat, giving plant- and animal-health reasons. The EU took the case to the World Trade Organization on 27 January 2022 (dispute DS610). The pressure on the multinationals was the hybrid part: German and other firms that had nothing to do with Taiwan were told, in effect, to choose between their Lithuanian suppliers and the Chinese market.Sources: European Commission, "WT/DS610 China — Measures concerning trade in goods and services"; South China Morning Post and Taipei Times reporting, 2021–2022.

The EU's answer for the future is the Anti-Coercion Instrument, Regulation (EU) 2023/2675, in force since 27 December 2023: when a country pressures the EU or a member state through trade, the EU can answer with its own trade measures. It protects states; a company caught in the middle still has to decide on its own suppliers.

Two or three sentences. Name the two instruments, say what each one did, and say what the company could not do because both came together.
✅ Step 3 · one campaign, five desks

A six-week campaign against Arvane: every pressure lands on a different desk

Why this step exists. The fourth mark of the definition, confusion, works inside a company because a company is divided into departments. Each department sees its own part of a campaign and judges it normal. This step plays a campaign against Arvane, move by move, so that you can see where each move lands.

The eight moves below are fictional, invented for the exercise. Each one is modelled on something that has happened to real companies, as the earlier sessions showed: a DDoS (Session 4), GNSS spoofing (Session 2), a forged document (Session 4, the story payload).

Do this. For each move, choose the desk at Arvane that would see it first. When all eight are placed, the grid below shows the whole campaign.

The campaign, as nobody at Arvane saw it

Each desk saw one or two moves, and each move on its own had an ordinary explanation. Only a person who received all eight would have seen one actor working towards one goal: to make the Black Sea too costly for Arvane. This is the task the EU gave its Hybrid Fusion Cell at the level of states; the framework calls it "a single focus for the analysis of hybrid threats". In a company the same task belongs to one named person, usually the incident director of Who answers, who receives every unusual event from every desk. The tripwires your team wrote in Session 2 are the rules for when a desk must pass an event on.

Two sentences. The first names the seat (a role, not a person's name). The second says which moves, put together, showed the pattern.
✅ Step 4 · pressure on the shipping lane

The Black Sea in July 2023 and the Red Sea since November 2023: how a threat changes a route through the price of insurance

Why this step exists. Arvane earns its money by moving cargo by sea. The two sea areas that matter most to it were both put under pressure in 2023. In neither case were the shipping companies' computers attacked. The pressure reached them through their insurers, and the insurers' prices changed the routes.

The Black Sea, July 2023. Under the Black Sea Grain Initiative, agreed in Istanbul on 22 July 2022 with the United Nations and Türkiye, ships carried grain out of Ukraine's ports. Russia let the agreement expire on 17 July 2023. The next day the broker Marsh paused its insurance facility for cargo leaving Ukraine, which had covered up to USD 50 million per voyage. On 19 July Russia's defence ministry announced that from midnight Moscow time on 20 July "all vessels travelling to Ukrainian ports in the Black Sea will be considered potential carriers of military cargo", and that their flag states would be treated as parties to the conflict. Ukraine's defence ministry answered with the same warning for ships heading to Russian ports, from 21 July. Lloyd's of London already listed the area as high risk, and war-risk premiums there are set week by week.Sources: Insurance Times and Insurance Journal (Reuters), 17–20 July 2023; The Moscow Times and Al Jazeera, 19–20 July 2023, quoting the Russian defence ministry; Ukrinform, 20 July 2023.
The Red Sea, from November 2023. Houthi forces in Yemen began attacking merchant ships in November 2023. The large container lines sent their ships around Africa, past the Cape of Good Hope, instead of through the Red Sea and the Suez Canal. The IMF's PortWatch data showed trade through the Suez Canal 50 % lower in the first two months of 2024 than a year earlier, transits around the Cape about 74 % higher, and delivery times longer by ten days or more on average.Source: P. Kamali, R. Koepke, A. Sozzi and J. Verschuur, "Red Sea attacks disrupt global trade", IMF Blog, 7 March 2024.

A war-risk premium is an extra insurance price for a voyage through an area the insurers list as dangerous, usually stated as a percentage of the ship's insured value and charged per transit. The premium is how a military threat becomes a number in a company's spreadsheet.

✅ Step 5 · resilience designed in advance

Resilience is built before the crisis: redundancy, flexibility, and Arvane's single points of failure

Why this step exists. A company cannot stop a state from applying pressure. It can decide in advance that no single failure stops its essential work. Your reading puts it in one sentence:

"Resilience is the capacity to withstand stress and recover, strengthened from challenges."European Commission and High Representative (2016), JOIN(2016) 18 final, section 4

The international standard on the subject, ISO 22316:2017 Organizational resilience, describes the same idea for companies: the ability to absorb change and adapt to it, so that the company still reaches its objectives. Yossi Sheffi of MIT (The Resilient Enterprise, 2005) names the two ways to get there. Redundancy means keeping a spare: a second supplier, extra stock, a second bank. Flexibility means being able to switch quickly: people who can do each other's jobs, contracts that allow a change, a plan that is decided in advance.

One fire, two companies (March 2000). A small fire at a Philips chip factory in Albuquerque, New Mexico, contaminated the clean rooms where radio-frequency chips for mobile phones were made. Nokia and Ericsson both bought these chips there. Nokia noticed the delays within days, raised the problem to its senior managers and secured the remaining capacity of other Philips plants and other suppliers. Ericsson had no other supplier for these chips. The Wall Street Journal reported in January 2001 that Ericsson's officials put its loss at at least USD 400 million in potential revenue. Nokia had flexibility; Ericsson had a single point of failure.Source: A. Latour, "Trial by fire: a blaze in Albuquerque sets off major crisis for cell-phone giants", The Wall Street Journal, 29 January 2001; Sheffi (2005).
The only supplier burns (February 1997). On 1 February 1997 a fire destroyed the lines at Aisin Seiki that made almost all the brake proportioning valves (P-valves) for every Toyota car. Toyota had one supplier for this small part and only a few days of stock. Its other suppliers, which had never made the part, rebuilt production in their own workshops from Aisin's drawings, and the first usable valves reached Toyota on 5 February. Toyota's production was set back by days, not the weeks first expected. Toyota had no redundancy for the part, but it had flexibility in its supplier network.Source: T. Nishiguchi and A. Beaudet, "The Toyota group and the Aisin fire", Sloan Management Review 40(1), 1998.

A single point of failure is one thing (a supplier, a system, a person) whose failure alone stops an essential activity. The framework you read names one for all of Europe: depending on a single satellite navigation signal for timing "does not offer the resilience required to counter hybrid threats" (JOIN(2016) 18, section 4.1.3, Action 8), which is why it asks for Galileo as a second source.
