Cables, satellites and the sky: where the internet physically runs, and what breaks it

Bring a laptop. Reflection 4 (Bueger, Liebetrau & Franken 2022) is due before 17:00.

Why this session exists. Sessions 1 to 4 treated an attack as something that travels through software. Under the software there is a physical network: glass fibres on the sea floor, a few buildings on the coast where they come ashore, and satellites with ground stations. A company that never meets an attacker can still lose its connection to a market because a ship drags its anchor across a cable, or because someone it never heard of attacks the network its satellite modems run on. This session opens that layer, runs it on the page, and ends with the question Session 3 left sealed: whether a cable that carries a country's critical traffic may be attacked in a war.

Arvane in this session. Arvane Commodities SA trades from Geneva and runs a desk in Singapore. Its traders in Geneva need a fast connection to Singapore all day, its terminal in Constanța sends data to Geneva, and its twelve chartered vessels talk to the office by satellite. In each step, you trace one of these links back to the physical thing it depends on.

✅ Step 2 · the cable

Where the internet physically runs: one cable, end to end

The scale. TeleGeography, the research firm that maps the network, counts 607 submarine cable systems in service in 2026, with 102 more planned, and over 1.5 million km of cable. The European Parliament study you read for today puts it in one figure: submarine cables "carry approximately 99 % of international internet traffic" (Bueger, Liebetrau & Franken 2022, p. 16). Satellites carry the rest, and matter where there is no cable: at sea, in remote places, and in war zones.

What a cable is made of. An undersea data cable is a garden-hose-thick tube that carries a few pairs of glass fibres. Light pulses in the fibres carry the data. Light fades over distance, so every 50 to 100 km the cable has a repeater, an amplifier that boosts the light, and the repeaters are powered by electricity sent down the cable from the shore. Near the coast, where anchors and fishing gear reach the bottom, the cable is armoured with steel wire and often buried in the seabed; in the deep ocean it is thin and lies on the sea floor.

The fibresA few pairs of glass threads. Each pair carries many wavelengths of light; the capacity of a modern cable is counted in hundreds of terabits per second.
The repeatersAmplifiers every 50 to 100 km on the sea floor, powered from the shore. A fault in the power line can take the whole cable down without cutting a fibre.
The landing stationThe building on the coast where the cable comes ashore and connects to the land networks. Few in number, easy to find, and a single point where many cables meet.
The ownersA consortium of telecom operators, or today often a single company such as Google or Meta. Repairs are paid by the owners and carried out by a small fleet of specialised ships.

Why the route matters to a trader. Light in glass travels at about two thirds of its speed in a vacuum, roughly 200,000 km per second, which is 5 microseconds per kilometre. A longer route means a slower connection, and a trading desk measures that delay. ✅ Step 3 · breaking and repairing

How a cable breaks, how the break is found, and how long the repair takes

How often cables break, and why. The International Cable Protection Committee (ICPC), the industry body of cable owners, and the ITU report that cables "experience around 200 faults each year … over 80 per cent of which are caused by fishing and anchoring" (ITU, 2 February 2026). The European Parliament study gives lower figures from older sources: about 100 ruptures a year, 40 % from fishing gear and 15 % from anchors (pp. 13 and 22). Both say the same thing: most breaks are accidents, which is exactly what makes a deliberate one hard to prove. The study adds that a repair takes on average "more than two weeks" (p. 23), and that only three repair ships are based in the EU: "the repair capacities are hence very limited" (p. 29).

How the break is found. The operator sends a short pulse of light into the fibre from the landing station and measures the faint light that comes back. A break reflects the pulse; the time until the reflection returns gives the distance, because the light travels there and back at about 204,000 km per second in the fibre (the speed of light divided by the glass's refractive index, about 1.468). The instrument is called an optical time-domain reflectometer, OTDR. The distance tells the repair ship where to go to within a few hundred metres.

How long the repair takes. A repair ship has to sail from its depot to the fault, wait for permission to work in that country's waters, lift both ends of the cable with a grapnel (a hook dragged along the sea floor), splice a new piece in on board, and lower it again. Each part takes days; in a war zone or a disputed sea, the permission can take weeks. The calculator adds them up.

✅ Step 4 · accident or sabotage

Accident or sabotage: what the Baltic and the Red Sea show

Why this step exists. When a cable breaks, the first question is whether anyone did it on purpose, because the answer decides who responds: the owner's insurer, the coast guard, the police, or a government. The difficulty is that a deliberate cut can be made to look exactly like an accident: a ship drags its anchor. Read the cases, then sort the evidence.

8 October 2023 · Gulf of Finland · the Balticconnector gas pipeline and two data cables. Finland's National Bureau of Investigation found that "the damage was caused by the anchor of the vessel Newnew Polar Bear flying the flag of Hong Kong". The criminal investigation was still "ongoing" in May 2025.Finnish Government, 9 May 2025, valtioneuvosto.fi
17–18 November 2024 · Baltic Sea · BCS East-West Interlink (Lithuania–Sweden) and C-Lion1 (Finland–Germany). Both data cables were cut within hours, along the route of the bulk carrier Yi Peng 3. The foreign ministers of Finland and Germany said they were "deeply concerned about the severed undersea cable". Sweden's accident investigators later reported, according to the press, that the ship had dragged its anchor for a long distance and that none of the explanations could be ruled out.German Federal Foreign Office, 18 November 2024; Swedish Accident Investigation Authority as reported by AP, April 2025
25 December 2024 · Gulf of Finland · the Estlink 2 power cable and four data cables. Finnish police seized the tanker Eagle S and investigated "aggravated criminal mischief". The prosecutor said the ship dragged its anchor "over a distance of approximately 90 kilometres". On 3 October 2025 the Helsinki District Court dismissed the charges: it ruled that the event was "an incident of navigation within the meaning of Article 97 of UNCLOS" (the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea), so Finnish criminal law did not apply. On 27 August 2026 the Helsinki Court of Appeal reversed that and held that Finland can try the crew; the case is not closed.Finnish Police, 26 December 2024; Helsinki District Court, judgment R 706/2025/12270, 3 October 2025; The Record, 27 August 2026
February–March 2024 and September 2025 · Red Sea. In 2024, after the cargo ship Rubymar was hit by a Houthi missile and drifted with its anchor down, three cables (AAE-1, Seacom, EIG) were damaged. Seacom said that "permitting could take up to eight weeks to obtain". Operators' estimates of the share of Europe–Asia traffic affected ranged from about 25 % to about 70 %. On 6 September 2025 two more cables, SMW4 and IMEWE, were cut near Jeddah; Microsoft reported "increased delays" on its Azure cloud for traffic through the Middle East, while "network traffic that does not traverse through the Middle East is not impacted".Seacom via TechCentral, 8 March 2024; press estimates (HGC, RETN), March 2024; Microsoft via AFP, 7 September 2025

What the cases show: in each one the ship was identified quickly, from its tracking signal and the operator's measurements. What stayed open, for months or years, is whether anyone meant it, and which court has the right to decide. The European Parliament study expected this: it evaluates a major breakdown as unlikely, "considering it could equate to an act of war", but warns that "symbolic attacks on cable connections are to be expected" and that attacks can be done "by weaponizing civil vessels … and using improvised cutting devices (ICDs) such as anchors" (pp. 10 and 30). It also names the weakest points on land: "Landing stations in which the undersea cable connects to the land are particularly vulnerable sites" (p. 30).

Sort the evidence: does it point to an accident, to intent, or to neither?

✅ Step 5 · satellites

The sky: satellite links, and the attack on Viasat's network on 24 February 2022

How a satellite link works. A ship or a remote site has a dish and a modem, a box that turns data into radio signals. The signal goes up to a satellite and down to a ground station on land, which connects to the internet. A geostationary satellite sits about 35,786 km above the equator and seems to stand still, so one satellite covers a third of the Earth, but every message travels that distance four times (up and down, there and back). A low-Earth-orbit satellite, such as Starlink's, flies a few hundred kilometres up, so the delay is much shorter, but many satellites are needed and each passes overhead in minutes.

Try it · the delay of a satellite link

Before you choose, write down your guess: how many times longer is the minimum round trip through a geostationary satellite than through a satellite 550 km up?

The minimum is physics only: four times the altitude at the speed of light, for a satellite straight overhead. Real links add the slant distance, the ground network and processing.

The attack. Viasat's KA-SAT network sold satellite internet across Europe through resellers. One hour before Russian troops crossed into Ukraine, its modems began to fail. Click each line of the chain to see what would have broken it.

1
The attackers got in by "exploiting a misconfiguration in a VPN appliance to gain remote access" to the network that ran KA-SAT's ground segment. A VPN appliance is the box that lets staff connect from outside.
What breaks it: the remote-access box configured and tested like the front door it is, with a second factor for every login and an alarm on logins from new places.
2
From there they moved into "a specific network segment used to manage and operate the network": the part of the system from which every customer's modem receives its settings and updates.
What breaks it: the management network separated from everything else, reachable only from a few named machines (Session 4's admin workstations).
3
They sent commands to the modems from there, and "these destructive commands overwrote key data in flash memory on the modems". SentinelLabs, a security research team, later named the wiper program AcidRain.
What breaks it: commands that change thousands of devices at once need a second person's approval, and the modems accept only signed software.
4
"Tens of thousands of modems … dropped off the network." Many had to be replaced rather than repaired remotely. In Germany the wind-turbine maker Enercon lost remote monitoring of its turbines; on 22 March, a month later, "communication continues to be disrupted in 618 wind farms".
What breaks it: for a user like Enercon, a second way to reach its sites, and a plan for running them without remote monitoring.
5
On 10 May 2022 the EU said the attack "took place one hour before Russia's unprovoked and unjustified invasion" of Ukraine, and the UK said it was "almost certain Russia was responsible". The damage spread to users across Europe who had nothing to do with the war.
What it shows: the victims were customers of a reseller of a satellite operator. None of them was the target, which is Session 1's question again.

✅ Step 6 · the close

The close: is a cable a lawful target, and where is Arvane weakest?

Session 3's sealed prediction

What the law says. In an armed conflict, the rules of international humanitarian law apply. Article 52(2) of Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions (1977) defines what may be attacked: "military objectives are limited to those objects which by their nature, location, purpose or use make an effective contribution to military action and whose total or partial destruction, capture or neutralization, in the circumstances ruling at the time, offers a definite military advantage." A cable that also carries military traffic can meet this test by its use, which is why lawyers call such objects dual-use. But meeting the test is not the end: an attack is still forbidden if the harm to civilians that can be expected is excessive compared with the military advantage (the rule of proportionality, Article 51(5)(b)), and the attacker must take precautions to limit that harm (Article 57). A cable that carries a country's hospitals, banks and power grid as well as its army therefore raises the question of how much civilian harm is excessive, and states do not agree on the answer. That disagreement is one reason why cables are cut in ways that can be denied, outside declared wars.
