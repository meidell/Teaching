Step 1 · the letter from the buyer's advisers

Boreal's advisers have found that Meridia's accounts do not agree with each other in FY2024 and FY2025

Halden & Co. (fictional) checks Meridia's accounts for the buyer; this check is called financial due diligence. Their letter asks two things:

1. "Total assets do not equal total liabilities and equity in FY2024 or FY2025, and the closing cash in the cash flow statement does not agree with the cash in the balance sheet in either year." · 2. "The dividend for FY2025 is €6.4 m, against €3.4 m the year before. Please explain how it is reflected in the net debt we should use in our offer."

Why it matters. A buyer sets its price from these accounts. If the accounts do not agree with each other, the advisers doubt every number in them, including the right ones. In practice that means more questions, a slower sale, and a lower offer or an offer with conditions.

What the CFO asks your team: find what is wrong, correct it, say whether it changes the price, and draft the reply by the end of the evening.

Read question 2 aloud: it is not about an error. It comes back in Step 5.
Step 1 · what the three statements are

The three financial statements describe the same company, so their numbers must agree with each other line by line

The income statement

It shows the profit of one year: revenue, minus costs, depreciation, interest and tax, gives net income.

The balance sheet

It shows what the company owns (assets) and what it owes (debt, plus the owners' equity) on the last day of the year. Total assets must equal total liabilities and equity.

The cash flow statement

It explains how the cash in the bank changed between two balance sheets: cash from operations, from investment and from financing.

"Linked" means that a number in one statement must come from another statement. Net income from the income statement is the first line of the cash flow statement. The closing cash of the cash flow statement must be the cash in the balance sheet. Every such link is a place where the accounts can be checked.

Do Step 1 now: read the letter and the three facts on the page, look through the draft accounts without searching for the errors yet, type your team's name and members, and press the button.

Step 2b · what changes when one thing happens

Predict how much net income and cash change when each of four €1.0 m events happens

  1. Meridia buys €1.0 m of extra refrigeration equipment and pays cash.
  2. A warehouse is written down: €1.0 m of extra depreciation. Nobody is paid. Tax is 24 % of profit before tax.
  3. A customer pays a €1.0 m invoice in December instead of January, so receivables fall by €1.0 m.
  4. Meridia pays its owners an extra €1.0 m dividend.
Do Step 2b: for each event, type the change in net income and the change in cash (a minus sign for a fall, 0 for no change) and press Check. Then press the event's button to see every line that moves in the three statements. Finish with the bike-shop example and one sentence in the box.

The usual mistake: event 1 lowers profit. It does not; buying equipment is an investment, and profit only falls later through depreciation.
Step 2b · the answers

Every event moves two lines of the balance sheet by the same amount, so the balance sheet still balances

Event, €1.0 mNet incomeCashWhy
1 · buy equipment for cash0.0−1.0Cash falls and fixed assets rise by the same amount. Profit falls only later, through depreciation.
2 · a €1.0 m write-down−0.76+0.24Profit before tax falls by 1.0, so tax falls by 24 % × 1.0 = 0.24. Nobody is paid for the write-down, so the only cash effect is the tax saved.
3 · a customer pays early0.0+1.0The sale was already counted as revenue. Receivables fall and cash rises by the same amount.
4 · an extra dividend0.0−1.0A dividend is not a cost. Cash falls and retained earnings fall by the same amount.

In every case, the change in cash in the cash flow statement is exactly the change in the balance sheet's cash. That rule is what Step 3 checks.

Event 2 is the one to repeat: a write-down raises cash, because of the tax.
Step 3 · six checks

A check is a number that must be zero if two statements agree: it compares a figure with the same figure calculated another way

CheckHow to calculate it
1 · Balancetotal assets − total liabilities and equity
2 · Cash, balanceclosing cash in the cash flow statement − cash in the balance sheet
3 · Cash, movementchange in cash in the cash flow statement − (this year's balance-sheet cash − last year's)
4 · Retained earningsthis year's retained earnings − (last year's + net income − dividends)
5 · Working capitalthe increase in working capital from the balance sheet + the working-capital line in the cash flow statement
6 · Fixed assetsthis year's fixed assets − (last year's + capex − D&A)
Do Step 3: FY2023 is filled in, and every check is 0.0. Calculate the six checks for FY2024 and FY2025 from the draft accounts, one decimal, and type them in. Green is right, red is wrong. When all twelve are green, write where your team thinks the errors are.
Checks 1 and 2 compare balances, which carry every earlier error forward. Checks 3 to 6 compare one year's movement, which fails only in the year of the error.

Step 4 · reply part 1 · four kinds of error

Name what went wrong in each of the four cells, then type the correct figure

A sign error

The number is right but has the wrong sign: added instead of deducted. The error is then twice the amount.

A missing line

A payment really happened, but the statement has no line for it, so it reads 0.0.

A link to the wrong line

The cell takes its number from the wrong place, for example profit before tax instead of net income.

An error carried forward

The cell's own calculation is right, but it starts from last year's wrong figure, so the error appears again.

Do Step 4: for each of the four findings, choose the kind of error. Type the four corrected figures, with the sign they have in the statement: money leaving the company is negative in the cash flow statement. The checks below recalculate live; the accounts are correct when every check reads 0.0. Then write reply part 1, error by error.
The four cells: CF FY2024 working capital, CF FY2025 dividends paid, BS FY2024 retained earnings, BS FY2025 retained earnings.

Step 5 · reply part 3 · does the price change?

The price per share is the enterprise value minus net debt, divided by 40.0 m shares

price per share = (enterprise value 159.36 − net debt) ÷ 40.0 m shares

The enterprise value is what the whole business is worth to all its investors; Sessions 1 and 2 calculated it at €159.36 m from revenue, EBITDA, D&A, capex and working capital. Net debt is debt minus cash: €60.0 m in FY2025. The owners receive what is left after the debt.

Do Step 5: calculate four prices on the page and press Check on each: the price on the corrected accounts; the price if a buyer had used the draft's FY2025 cash (net debt 69.0 − 18.2 = 50.8); the price if the FY2025 dividend had not been paid (net debt 53.6); and the dividend per share the owners have already received. Then write reply part 3.
The errors did not touch any figure the valuation uses, and the balance sheet's cash was right all along.
Step 5 · the answers

The errors do not change the price, but a buyer who believed the draft would have paid €0.23 a share too much

The dividend is not an error. €6.4 m of cash left the company before the sale. That raises net debt, so the buyer pays less per share, and the owners have already received the difference: 6.4 ÷ 40.0 = €0.16 a share. Cash paid out to the sellers before the sale closes is called leakage. The buyer and the seller agree from which date it is no longer allowed.
