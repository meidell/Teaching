From Halden & Co., financial due diligence for Boreal Logistics · to Meridia Cold, CFO
We have begun our review of the historical financial statements in the data room (FY2023–FY2025). Before we go further, two points need an answer.

1. Total assets do not equal total liabilities and equity in FY2024 or FY2025, and the closing cash in the cash flow statement does not agree with the cash in the balance sheet in either year. Please identify the differences, correct the statements and confirm which figures in the pack are affected.
2. The dividend for FY2025 is €6.4 m, against €3.4 m the year before. Please explain how it is reflected in the net debt we should use in our offer.
Why it matters. A buyer prices from these statements. A pack that does not tie makes the advisers doubt every number in it, including the ones that are right; in practice that means more questions, a slower process and a lower or more conditional offer.
What the three statements are. The income statement shows the profit of a year. The balance sheet shows what the company owns and owes on the last day of the year. The cash flow statement explains how the cash moved between two balance sheets. They describe the same company, so they must agree with each other line by line: that is what "linked" means.
The figures you already know. Revenue, EBITDA, D&A, capex, the increase in working capital and net debt are the same as in Sessions 1 and 2 (net debt 62.0 · 60.5 · 60.0). Net debt = debt − cash.

2b · What moves when one thing happens? · 15 min

Four things could have happened to Meridia in FY2025, each worth €1.0 m. For each one, predict how much net income and cash change, type your two numbers (use a minus sign for a fall, 0 for no change) and press Check. Then press the event's button to see every line that moves in all three statements.

  1. Event 1. Meridia buys €1.0 m of extra refrigeration equipment and pays cash.
  2. Event 2. A warehouse is written down: €1.0 m of extra depreciation. No cash is paid to anyone. Tax is 24 % of profit before tax.
  3. Event 3. A customer pays a €1.0 m invoice in December instead of January: receivables fall by €1.0 m.
  4. Event 4. Meridia pays an extra €1.0 m dividend to its owners.
How to read itThe highlighted cells are the ones that move. Every event changes at least two lines of the balance sheet by the same amount, so it still balances, and the change in cash in the cash flow statement is always exactly the change in the balance sheet's cash. Event 2 is the one to remember: depreciation is not paid to anyone, so the only cash effect is the tax it saves (24 % × 1.0 = 0.24). Event 5 is free to look at: debt and cash both rise by 1.0 and net debt does not move.
The same structure in another business: a bike shop

A bike shop buys €5,000 of bikes from its supplier on 30 days' credit, then sells all of them for €8,000 cash before the month ends. Before you open the answer: what happens to profit, to cash and to the balance sheet in each half?

The answer

The purchase: no profit, no cash. Inventory +5,000 (an asset) and payables +5,000 (a liability): the balance sheet grows on both sides. The sale: revenue 8,000, cost of goods sold 5,000, profit before tax 3,000. Cash +8,000, inventory −5,000; the profit goes into retained earnings (+3,000, less tax). The cash flow statement starts from the 3,000 profit, adds the fall in inventory (+5,000) and the rise in payables (+5,000)… and must end at the +8,000 in the bank. The supplier is still unpaid: that is why profit and cash differ, and why working capital sits between them.

✅ Step 3 · 20 min

Run the checks

A check is a number that must be zero if the statements agree. Each one compares a figure with the same figure computed another way. FY2023 is filled in for you: every check is 0.0, so the pack starts right. Your job is FY2024 and FY2025.

  1. Use the draft pack (Step 1, or open it again under this list). For each check, compute FY2024 and FY2025 with the formula in the left column, one decimal, a minus sign where the result is negative.
  2. Type each result in its box. A right number turns green, a wrong one red: work it out again.
  3. When all twelve are green, read the pattern as a team: which checks fail, in which year, by how much? Write where you think the errors are.
Open the draft pack here
How to read itTwo kinds of check. Check 1 and check 2 compare balances: an error made in FY2024 stays in every later balance sheet, so they keep failing after the year that went wrong. Checks 3 to 6 compare one year's movement: they fail only in the year where the error was made. A balance check tells you that the pack is wrong; a movement check tells you where.

✅ Step 5 · 10 min · reply part 3

Does it change the price?

The valuation from Sessions 1 and 2 uses six figures from the past: revenue, EBITDA, D&A, capex, the increase in working capital, and FY2025 net debt. On the base case it gives an enterprise value of €159.36 m and €2.48 a share (equity = EV − net debt, ÷ 40.0 m shares).

  1. Work out the four numbers below and press Check on each.
  2. As a team, write reply part 3: did the errors change the price, what would have happened if a buyer had believed the draft, and how the FY2025 dividend enters the price.
How to read itNone of the three errors touched EBITDA, D&A, capex or the working-capital figure the valuation uses, and the balance sheet's cash was right all along: the price per share does not move. But a buyer reading the draft cash flow statement would have seen €18.2 m of cash, a net debt of €50.8 m and a price €0.23 a share higher — until due diligence found it. The dividend is not an error. It is cash that left the company before the sale: it raises net debt, so the buyer pays less per share, and the owners have already received the difference (€6.4 m ÷ 40.0 m shares = €0.16). Buyers call cash paid out to the sellers before closing leakage, and agree with the seller from which date it is no longer allowed.
