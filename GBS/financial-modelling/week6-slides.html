The road from the forecast to a price per share

A discounted cash flow valuation has six steps, and tonight walks them in order

1 · Free cash flowthe cash the business produces each year, before paying lenders or owners
2 · WACCthe yearly return lenders and owners require; the rate that converts future euros to today's
3 · Discounteach year's cash flow × its discount factor; add the five years
4 · Terminal valueone number for all the years after FY2030, two ways
5 · Enterprise valuethe five years + the terminal value, both in today's euros
6 · Per shareminus net debt 60.0, divided by 40.0 million shares

A discounted cash flow valuation (DCF) values a business as the cash it will produce, with each future euro converted into today's euros at a rate that pays investors for waiting and for the risk.

The result is the enterprise value: the value of the whole business, before the debt is paid off. The shareholders own what is left after the net debt.

This strip comes back at the start of each part with the current step in gold. Four parts tonight: free cash flow (steps 1), the WACC (2), discounting and the terminal value (3–6), then four tests of the terminal value.
Part 1 · free cash flow

Free cash flow to the firm is the cash Meridia produces in a year after tax, capex and working capital, before it pays its lenders or its owners

1 · Free cash flowthis part
2 · WACC
3 · Discount
4 · Terminal value
5 · Enterprise value
6 · Per share
FCFF = EBIT × (1 − tax rate) + D&A − capex − increase in working capital
  • EBIT, earnings before interest and tax = EBITDA − depreciation and amortisation (D&A).
  • EBIT × (1 − tax rate) is called NOPAT, net operating profit after tax: the profit the business would make if it had no debt.
  • + D&A: D&A is a cost in the income statement, but no cash leaves the company, so it is added back.
  • − capex − increase in working capital: real cash spent on fixed assets and on the money tied up in receivables and inventory.
Write the formula on the board; it stays there all evening. Lenders and owners are paid OUT OF this cash flow, which is why it measures what the whole business is worth. Students who know free cash flow to equity: that is FCFF minus what goes to the lenders; the course uses FCFF throughout.

This is the slide that most often needs a second explanation. The rule: the operating cash flow is debt-free; the financing is in the rate.

Part 2 · the cost of capital

The WACC is the yearly return that Meridia's lenders and owners together require, each weighted by its share of the financing

1 · Free cash flow
2 · WACCthis part
3 · Discount
4 · Terminal value
5 · Enterprise value
6 · Per share
WACC = E ÷ (D + E) × cost of equity + D ÷ (D + E) × cost of debt × (1 − tax rate)
  • D and E are the debt and the equity at market value: the shares of the financing that lenders and owners provide.
  • The cost of debt is what Meridia pays to borrow. After tax, because interest is deductible: this is where the 0.75 of slide 8 goes.
  • The cost of equity is what shareholders require. Nobody publishes it; it is estimated with a model, on slides 14–17.

FCFF goes to lenders and owners together, so it is discounted at their combined rate.

Session 1 gave Meridia 8.5 % without a source. Tonight each of the five numbers in the formula gets a source and a date. The order: cost of debt (easy), then the cost of equity in three pieces, then the weights.

Part 2 · the cost of debt

Meridia borrows at 4.6 %, and because interest is deductible the cost to the business is 3.50 % after tax

Cost of debt after tax = 4.6 % × (1 − 24 %) = 3.50 %
  • 4.6 % is what Meridia actually paid its lenders over FY2023–FY2025 (interest ÷ average debt, Session 4). It is 1.75 points above the Bund: the extra the bank charges for Meridia's risk.
  • Of every euro of interest, 24 cents come back as lower tax. That is the 0.75 of slide 8: it belongs to the debt, so it is counted here and nowhere else.

This is the easy half of the WACC: a bank states its rate. The owners do not, so the cost of equity has to be built.

If a student asks about the facility rate of Session 4: the same 4.6 %. A new loan today might be priced differently; the course keeps 4.6 % as the case figure.
Part 2 · the cost of equity

The capital asset pricing model says shareholders require the yield of a safe bond plus a premium for the risk they carry

Cost of equity = risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium
  • The risk-free rate is the yield of a government bond that will certainly be repaid, in the currency of the cash flows. Meridia's cash flows are in euros, and the safest euro bond is the German 10-year Bund: 2.85 % on 31 December 2025.
  • The equity risk premium is the extra yearly return investors expect from shares above that bond, for taking the risk of owning a business. Slide 15.
  • Beta measures how much more or less than the average share Meridia's risk is. Slide 16.

The model is called the CAPM. It is the one every bank and every valuation report uses, so the reply can cite it by name.

Why the Bund and not a Spanish bond for a Spanish company: the cash flows are in euros, and Spain's extra risk goes into its equity risk premium instead, so it is not counted twice.

Part 2 · beta

Meridia is not listed, so it borrows the beta of 56 listed European transportation companies, with their debt taken out and Meridia's put back in

  • Beta measures how much a company's share price moves when the whole market moves: 1.0 moves with the market, 0.5 half as much. It is measured from share prices, and Meridia has none.
  • Listed companies' betas include the effect of their debt: more debt makes the shares riskier. Damodaran publishes the industry beta with the debt removed, the unlevered beta: transportation, Western Europe, 0.54.
  • You then put back the debt of the company you value, the levered beta, using the comparables' debt ÷ equity of 0.77 (Meridia's own market value is what we are looking for, so it cannot be used yet).
Levered beta = 0.54 × (1 + (1 − 24 %) × 0.77) = 0.54 × 1.585 = 0.856

Meridia's shares move a little less than the market: a cold-chain operator keeps its warehouses full in good years and bad.

Damodaran's "unlevered beta corrected for cash" also removes the cash the companies hold (cash has a beta of zero); that is the column used. Trucking alone (7 companies) gives 0.65; transportation (56) is used because the larger group gives a more stable average. The page lets them try 0.65.

Part 3 · the terminal value

The forecast stops after FY2030, but Meridia does not: the terminal value is one number for all the years after it

42.66 is only the value of five years. A warehouse business that still exists in FY2031, FY2040 and FY2060 is worth much more than that. The terminal value stands for every cash flow from FY2031 onwards, as one amount at the end of FY2030.

Method 1 · perpetuity growth

Assume FCFF grows by a small rate g every year for ever, and value that stream with a formula. Slide 22.

Method 2 · exit multiple

Assume Meridia is sold at the end of FY2030 for what listed companies in its industry are worth today, as a multiple of EBITDA. Slide 23.

Both give a value at the end of FY2030, so both must then be multiplied by the FY2030 discount factor, 0.6937, to be worth something today.

Keep the two methods clearly apart on the board: left column perpetuity, right column exit multiple. Slide 24 puts them side by side.
Part 3 · method 1 · perpetuity growth

If Meridia's cash flow grows 2 % a year for ever, the years after FY2030 are worth 219.7 at the end of FY2030 and 152.4 today

Terminal value at the end of FY2030 = FCFF of FY2031 ÷ (WACC − g)
FCFF of FY2031 = FCFF of FY2030 × (1 + g) = 12.04 × 1.0212.28
Terminal value at the end of FY2030 = 12.28 ÷ (7.59 % − 2.0 %)219.7
× discount factor of FY2030, 0.6937 = terminal value today152.4
  • g = 2.0 % is the European Central Bank's inflation target: after FY2030 Meridia is assumed to grow only as fast as prices. Nothing grows faster than the whole economy for ever.
  • g must be below the WACC: at or above it the formula divides by zero or a negative number, because the cash flows would grow faster than they are discounted.
The formula is also called the Gordon growth formula. The terminal value takes the LAST forecast year's discount factor, because it is a value at the end of FY2030, not a cash flow in FY2031.

Part 4 · testing the terminal value

78 % of Meridia's value sits in one number that rests on two inputs nobody can observe, so a valuer tests it four ways before reporting it

The growth rate for ever and the multiple a buyer will pay in five years cannot be proved. They can be checked against each other and against the forecast. Boreal's committee will ask about these four tests first.

Test 1 · the share in the terminal value

How much of the value depends on g and the WACC instead of on the forecast you checked. Report it every time.

Test 2 · the multiple hidden in method 1

What EBITDA multiple does the perpetuity value assume a buyer pays? Compare with listed companies.

Test 3 · the growth hidden in method 2

What growth for ever does the exit multiple assume? Compare with 2 %.

Test 4 · the investment in the terminal year

Does FY2030 still invest for 6.7 % growth, when the years after grow 2 %?

Tests 2 and 3 are the same idea in both directions: each method hides an assumption of the other, and turning the formula around shows it.

Part 4 · test 3

The 9.1 multiple hides growth of 4.16 % a year for ever, twice the inflation target, which no company can sustain

g implied = (TV × WACC − FCFF of FY2030) ÷ (TV + FCFF of FY2030)
= (365.7 × 7.59 % − 12.04) ÷ (365.7 + 12.04) = (27.76 − 12.04) ÷ 377.74.16 %
The growth rate method 1 uses2.0 %

The formula is the perpetuity formula solved for g. It asks: for the exit multiple to be right, how fast would Meridia have to grow for ever? A company growing 4 % a year when the economy grows 2 % ends up larger than the economy.

So for Meridia, the exit multiple is the method to distrust: €5.91 assumes growth that cannot last.

Listed multiples are high when the market expects growth or when rates were low when they were measured. The test turns a market mood into a growth rate that can be judged.

What a good reply says

A reported value names its method, states its terminal value share, shows its range, and says which assumption moves it most

Part 3 of the reply, in three sentences: "We value Meridia at €3.38 a share by discounted cash flow at a WACC of 7.59 % built from market data of January 2026, with a terminal value by perpetuity growth at 2 %. With the terminal year normalised the value is €4.96; the exit multiple of 9.1 × gives €5.91 but assumes growth of 4.2 % for ever. The bank's lender case gives €2.27."
Part 4: "78 % of the enterprise value sits in the terminal value. The value depends most on the WACC: one point moves the price by about €0.80, and the 3-point premium for size, a judgement of the investment committee, is worth €2.15 a share on its own."

Every number in those sentences is sourced on the slides tonight. That is what "an input we can check" means in Boreal's letter.

€2.15 = 5.53 − 3.38. Read the two paragraphs aloud; this is the standard the reply on the page is marked against.
What to remember

Five sentences from tonight

  1. Free cash flow is EBIT after tax plus D&A minus capex minus the increase in working capital: the cash for lenders and owners together. For Meridia it is 30 % of EBITDA, because capex takes half.
  2. The WACC is built, not assumed: the Bund, a country-weighted risk premium, a relevered industry beta, a stated premium for size, the after-tax cost of debt. Meridia: 7.59 %.
  3. The five forecast years are worth 42.7; the terminal value by perpetuity growth 152.4 today; together 195.0, which is €3.38 a share after net debt of 60.0.
  4. Four fifths of the value is the terminal value, so test it: its share, the multiple it implies (5.47 ×), the growth the exit multiple implies (4.16 %), and a normalised terminal year (€4.96).
  5. Report a range with named ends, and say which input moves the value most. For Meridia: the WACC, and inside it the premium for size.
If one thing sticks: the discount rate matters more than the forecast, and the terminal value matters more than the five years.
