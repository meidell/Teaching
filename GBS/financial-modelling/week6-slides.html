Part 1 · free cash flow

Free cash flow to the firm is the cash the business produces before it pays its lenders or its owners

FCFF = EBIT × (1 − tax rate) + D&A − capex − increase in working capital
  • EBIT, earnings before interest and tax = EBITDA − depreciation and amortisation (D&A).
  • NOPAT, net operating profit after tax = EBIT × (1 − tax rate): the profit the business would make if it had no debt.
  • + D&A: a cost in the income statement, but no cash leaves the company.
  • − capex and − increase in working capital: real cash spent on growing the business.

Lenders and owners are paid out of FCFF. That is why it is the cash flow that measures what the whole business is worth.

Write the four lines on the board. Students who know "free cash flow to equity" will ask: that is FCFF minus what goes to lenders. The course uses FCFF throughout, discounted at the WACC.
Part 1 · two common shortcuts

Net income and EBITDA are both easier to find than free cash flow, and both give the wrong value

Net income is not free cash flow

Interest has already been subtracted, and interest is a payment to the lenders, who are paid out of FCFF.

Net income ignores capex and working capital, which are cash.

It subtracts D&A, which is not cash.

EBITDA is not free cash flow

EBITDA ignores tax.

EBITDA ignores capex: a business that must replace its trucks or freezers every few years spends a large part of its EBITDA on them.

EBITDA ignores working capital.

A business that grows fast can show rising net income and rising EBITDA while its free cash flow is negative. The next slide shows one.

Ask the room: which number does a company's press release lead with? Usually EBITDA. Which number pays the dividend? Free cash flow.

Part 1 · two checks on the same number

FCFF can be reached from the cash flow statement, and it must equal what goes to lenders, to owners and into the cash balance

Route 2 · from the cash flow statement

Cash from operations 222.0 − capex 500.0 + interest 30.0 × (1 − 20 %) = 222.0 − 500.0 + 24.0 = −254.0.

The interest is added back because FCFF is measured before any payment to lenders.

Route 3 · where the cash goes

Lenders: interest after tax 24.0 + repayment 100.0 − facility drawn 366.9 = −242.9.

Owners: dividend 38.9. Cash balance: 150.0 → 100.0 = −50.0.

Total: −242.9 + 38.9 − 50.0 = −254.0.

If the three routes do not agree, the model has an error. On the page, both reconciliation rows turn green for every year.

These are the Session 4 bakery figures with interest on the opening balance (30.0 = 5 % of 600). A negative amount to lenders means the lenders put money in: the bank financed the shop.
Part 1 · the tax line

FCFF uses the tax on EBIT, not the tax the company pays, because the tax saved on interest is counted in the cost of debt

Forno Lumen FY2026, € thousandTax
Tax the bakery actually pays: 20 % × (EBIT 192.0 − interest 30.0)32.4
Tax on EBIT, used in FCFF: 20 % × 192.038.4
Difference: the tax saved because interest is deductible = 20 % × 30.06.0

The 6.0 exists only because the bakery borrows. Part 2 puts it into the WACC as the cost of debt after tax. If it were also in the cash flow, it would be counted twice and the value would be too high.

A consequence: FCFF does not change when the dividend or the interest method of Session 4 changes. Neither changes EBIT, capex or working capital.
This is the slide that most often needs a second explanation. The rule to remember: the cash flow is computed as if the company had no debt; the debt and its tax advantage are in the discount rate.

Part 2 · the cost of capital

The WACC is the yearly return that lenders and owners together require, each weighted by its share of the financing

WACC = E ÷ (D + E) × cost of equity + D ÷ (D + E) × cost of debt × (1 − tax rate)
  • D and E: debt and equity at market value, not at the values in the balance sheet.
  • Cost of debt: what the company pays to borrow today. After tax, because interest is deductible (the 6.0 of the previous part).
  • Cost of equity: what shareholders require. Nobody publishes it, so it is estimated with a model, on the next slide.

FCFF goes to lenders and owners together, so it is discounted at their combined rate.

Session 1 gave Meridia 8.5 % without saying where it came from. Tonight each of the five numbers in the formula gets a source and a date.
Part 2 · the cost of equity

The capital asset pricing model says shareholders require a safe bond's yield plus a premium for the risk they cannot diversify away

Cost of equity = risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium
InputValueSource and date
Risk-free rate: 10-year German government bond (Bund), in euros2.85 %German Federal Ministry of Finance, Monatsbericht January 2026, yield on 31 December 2025
Equity risk premium, mature market (Germany, Switzerland)4.23 %A. Damodaran, NYU, implied premium, 1 January 2026
Equity risk premium: Spain · France · Italy5.78 · 5.01 · 6.69 %A. Damodaran, country risk premiums (rating-based), 1 January 2026

The equity risk premium is the extra yearly return investors expect from shares over the safe bond. It is higher in a country whose government bonds carry more risk.

Why the Bund and not a Spanish bond for Meridia: the cash flows are in euros, and the Bund is the euro bond closest to risk-free; Spain's extra risk goes into its equity risk premium instead of into the risk-free rate, so it is not counted twice. The bakery is valued in euros too, so it uses the same Bund.
Part 2 · beta

A company that is not listed borrows the beta of listed companies in the same business, after taking their debt out

Levered beta = unlevered beta × (1 + (1 − tax rate) × D ÷ E)
  • Beta measures how much a share price moves when the whole market moves: 1.0 moves with it, 0.5 half as much.
  • Listed companies' betas include the effect of their debt: more debt makes the shares riskier. Damodaran publishes them by industry with the debt removed, the unlevered beta.
  • You then put back the debt of the company you value, the levered beta.
Forno Lumen: European restaurants have an unlevered beta of 0.59 and a debt ÷ equity of 0.39 (Damodaran, Western Europe, 38 companies, 5 January 2026). Tax 20 %. What is the levered beta?
Answer: 0.59 × (1 + 0.80 × 0.39) = 0.59 × 1.312 = 0.774.
Damodaran's "unlevered beta corrected for cash" also removes the cash the companies hold (cash has a beta of zero). Use that column. The page does the same for Meridia with the "Transportation" row.

Part 3 · terminal value, method 1

Perpetuity growth: if the cash flow grows by g every year for ever, its value is next year's cash flow divided by WACC minus g

Terminal value at the end of the forecast = FCFF of the next year ÷ (WACC − g)
Forno Lumen: FCFF FY2028 138.7, g 2.0 % (the European Central Bank's inflation target), WACC 7.69 %. What is the terminal value, and what share of the value does it carry?
FCFF FY2029 = 138.7 × 1.02141.5
Terminal value at the end of FY2028 = 141.5 ÷ (7.69 % − 2.0 %)2,486.0
× discount factor of FY2028, 0.8007 = terminal value today1,990.6
Enterprise value = −9.4 + 1,990.61,981.2
Share in the terminal value = 1,990.6 ÷ 1,981.2100.5 %
A share above 100 % is not an error in the arithmetic: the forecast years are negative. It is the clearest possible signal that the value depends entirely on g and the WACC. The terminal value is discounted with the LAST forecast year's factor, because it is a value at the end of FY2028.

Use the net debt on the valuation date, the same date the cash flows are discounted to. The facility the bakery draws in FY2026 is not subtracted: that borrowing is already inside the FY2026 free cash flow, which is negative.

A common error: subtracting forecast net debt (for example FY2030) from today's enterprise value. Both numbers must be on the same date.

Part 4 · tests 1 and 2

Each method of terminal value hides an assumption of the other method, and turning the formula around shows it

Test 1 · the share in the terminal value

Bakery: 100.5 %. Report it every time. The higher it is, the more the value depends on g and the WACC instead of on the forecast.

Test 2 · the multiple hidden in the perpetuity value

Perpetuity terminal value ÷ EBITDA FY2028 = 2,486.0 ÷ 324.6 = 7.66 ×.

The perpetuity method says a buyer pays 7.7 times EBITDA; listed restaurants trade at 11.5 times.

A large gap means the two methods disagree about the business. Either the bakery deserves less than listed restaurant groups (it is small, local and unlisted), or the perpetuity inputs are too cautious. The reply must say which.

Test 2 is free: it uses numbers already on the sheet. A valuer who reports a perpetuity value should always print the multiple it implies next to it.
Part 4 · test 3

The exit multiple hides a growth rate for ever, and a growth rate above the economy's cannot last for ever

g implied = (TV × WACC − FCFF of the last year) ÷ (TV + FCFF of the last year)
Forno Lumen: exit terminal value 3,733.0, WACC 7.69 %, FCFF FY2028 138.7. What growth for ever does the exit multiple assume?
Answer: (3,733.0 × 0.0769 − 138.7) ÷ (3,733.0 + 138.7) = 148.4 ÷ 3,871.7 = 3.83 % a year, for ever: almost twice the 2 % inflation target. A company that grows faster than the economy for ever ends up larger than the economy.

The formula is the perpetuity formula solved for g: TV = FCFF × (1 + g) ÷ (WACC − g).

Listed multiples are high when the market expects growth, or when interest rates were low when the multiple was measured. A multiple taken from today's market and applied in five years carries today's mood into the terminal year.
Part 4 · test 4

The terminal year must invest for the growth of the terminal value, not for the growth of the forecast

A business growing 2 % a year for ever needs capex equal to what wears out (D&A) plus 2 % more fixed assets, and 2 % more working capital. The normalised year uses exactly that.

The bakery's FY2028 capex of 150 is below its D&A of 157.2, so its fixed assets shrink; it cannot grow 2 % for ever on that. Normalising lowers the value by a quarter. For Meridia the page shows that the test can go the other way.

For Meridia, FY2030 capex of 8 % of revenue is still sized for 6.7 % growth, so the normalised year needs LESS capex and the value rises. Do not give the Meridia figure: it is check 5d.
