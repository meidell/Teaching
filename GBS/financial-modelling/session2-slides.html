Thursday 29 October 2026 · the mandate is on the course site: Session 2 · every figure in this deck is your Session 1 model

Open with the situation, not the theory. One sentence: the company is being sold, a document goes to the buyers on Monday, and the board has to approve it. Everything tonight serves that decision. The hour is the logic of the mandate; after it, the teams work through the page on their own.
The question, in the CFO's words

Three questions, one memo

"Is this plan credible enough for the board to put its name to? What range of value does it imply? And what is the lowest price we should accept?"

Credible?

Does the plan follow from what Meridia has actually done — or from what management hopes?

Worth what?

Not one number: the range the three plans imply, and what moves it.

Walk away at?

The lowest price per share, fixed before any offer arrives.

Deliverable: a one-page plan review memo to the board — recommendation · value range · the two assumptions that decide the price · walk-away price.

These three questions are the memo's skeleton. Say now that the answer to the first is rarely yes or no; it is usually "approve with caveats", and the caveats are the value of the review.

Part 1 · The deal

How a company is sold: the auction

1 · DecideThe owners and the board decide to sell and hire an adviser.
2 · TeaserA short anonymous profile goes to likely buyers; they sign a confidentiality agreement.
3 · Data roomBuyers receive the information memorandum and the confidential documents — including the plan.
4 · BidsFirst non-binding offers, then due diligence: the buyers' advisers check everything.
5 · SignBinding offers, negotiation, the sale contract, closing.

Meridia is at step 3. Whatever is in the data room is what both buyers will price from — and what their advisers will try to break.

The typical stages of a controlled auction; real processes vary in the number of rounds.

Students often think valuation happens after the offer. In a sale it happens before: the seller's plan anchors every bid. Step 3 is where the board still controls the story.

Part 1 · The deal

What exactly is for sale: the equity bridge

A buyer takes over the €60 m of net debt, so the owners receive enterprise value − net debt. Every €1 m of EV is 2.5 cents a share.

The bridge matters for the walk-away price: the board negotiates in price per share, the model produces enterprise value. Note the split of EV: only €26 m comes from the three plan years; €133 m is what the model assumes after FY2028. We come back to that.
Part 1 · The deal

Why the board hesitates

Too optimistic

The buyers' advisers find it in due diligence. They cut the price, ask for part of it to depend on future results (an earn-out), or walk away. Arco's bank refuses to lend on it.

The board loses credibility in the one negotiation it gets.

Too cautious

Every bid anchors on a low number. The owners sell the company for less than it is worth — and nobody will pay more than the seller's own plan says.

A forecast is usually not something the seller guarantees in the sale contract. That is exactly why buyers price its credibility instead.

The asymmetry: an unbelievable plan costs more than a cautious one, because it is discovered and punished, while the cautious plan is never contested. Hence the reviewer's job: not to make the plan prettier, but to make it defensible.

Part 2 · What drives the value

Change one thing at a time

Management's plan moves four assumptions at once. Then nobody can see which one creates the extra €0.50 a share.

An independent reviewer separates them: from the base case, move one assumption to the plan's figure, read the change, put it back. Then the next.

This is one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis. Drawn sideways, largest swing on top, it is called a tornado chart.

Its limit: it ignores interactions — the four changes together give a little more than the sum of the four alone (€2.98, not €2.95).

This is Step 2 of the page. Stress the discipline: ceteris paribus is the only way to attribute value to a single assumption. Mention the limit honestly; with four drivers the interaction is small here.

Part 2 · Theory

Why growth is worth almost nothing here

Follow one extra euro of revenue through Meridia's cash flow, at the base-case assumptions:

The year it arrivesEach year after

Faster growth costs cash first: at 9 % instead of 6.7 %, free cash flow is €0.24 m lower in FY2026 and €0.10 m lower in FY2027; it is ahead only from FY2028, by €0.06 m.

This is the mechanism behind the tornado. Growth needs investment — capex and working capital — before it pays. With only three years of faster growth and 2 % after, the payback is barely positive at an 8.5 % WACC. Growth is not bad: it needs cash before it pays, and in this model the faster growth lasts only three years, so there is little time for the payback.

Part 3 · Is the plan credible?

The margin jump: +1.4 points in one year

At the plan's revenue, 17.5 % instead of 16.1 % is €2.75 m more EBITDA in FY2026 alone — and it is assumed every year after.

Meridia's best year added about 0.8 points. The plan asks for 1.4 in a single year, while also growing faster. Possible, but it needs a reason: price increases at contract renewal, lower energy costs (refrigeration is energy-heavy), higher warehouse occupancy, automation. The reviewer's job is to ask for that reason in writing.
Part 3 · Theory

Why plans are optimistic: the inside view

The inside view

Forecasting from the details of our own plan: the contracts we hope to win, the savings we intend to make. Each step is plausible, so the whole looks plausible.

The outside view

Asking first how comparable cases actually turned out — a reference class. For Meridia the first reference class is Meridia itself: three years of history.

Kahneman and Lovallo called this the pairing of timid choices and bold forecasts: decision-makers are systematically optimistic about their own projects. Flyvbjerg turned the cure into a method — reference class forecasting.

D. Kahneman & D. Lovallo (1993), "Timid Choices and Bold Forecasts", Management Science 39(1) · B. Flyvbjerg, reference class forecasting, applied to large projects.

Management is not lying; the inside view is how every plan is built. The board's protection is the outside view: compare the plan with the base rate. Tonight the base rate is Meridia's own history; in Session 7 it becomes listed comparables.
Part 3 · Is the plan credible?

What the board should ask for

Approving "with caveats" means naming the evidence that would make the plan defensible in due diligence:

  • Margin: which contracts renew in FY2026, at what price increase; the energy cost assumption and any hedges; occupancy by warehouse.
  • Growth: signed or near-signed new contracts behind the 9 %; the share of revenue already under contract for FY2026.
  • Working capital: why customers would pay faster than the 12–13 % history implies.
  • Capex: what the extra 0.6 points buys — if it is growth capex, show the return; if it is caution, say so.

A caveat the board can check is worth more than a revision nobody can.

This slide feeds memo part 3. Push the teams to be concrete: "more evidence" is not a caveat; "the renewal schedule for the five largest contracts" is.

Part 5 · The walk-away price

Decide the floor before the first offer

  • Reservation price: the lowest price the seller will accept. Below it, the owners are better off not selling.
  • BATNA — Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement: what the owners do if no bid is good enough. Here: keep running Meridia, worth about the base case to them.
  • Anchoring: the first number on the table pulls every later number toward it. A floor decided in advance resists it.

A walk-away price is a judgement — but it must rest on one assumption you can defend: "€2.40, because it needs only the 16.0 % margin Meridia already earns".

R. Fisher & W. Ury (1981), Getting to Yes, on BATNA · A. Tversky & D. Kahneman (1974), "Judgment under Uncertainty", Science, on anchoring.

Step 5 of the page. The tool there answers: what margin does this price need? Good answers tie the floor to the base case (the owners' alternative), adjusted for what the team found credible. Weak answers pick a round number.

Part 6 · Model risk

Why a board reviews models at all

JPMorgan, 2012

The "London Whale" losses, about $6 bn. The bank's own task force found the risk model ran on spreadsheets with manual copy-and-paste, and a formula that divided by a sum instead of an average.

Reinhart & Rogoff, 2013

An influential paper on public debt and growth. Other researchers found a spreadsheet range that left out five countries — one of several problems that changed the result.

TransAlta, 2003

A Canadian power company lost about $24 m on electricity transmission contracts because of a cut-and-paste error in its bid spreadsheet.

Your Session 1 file had twelve planted errors. Real models have unplanted ones. A plan the board has not reviewed is a plan the buyers will review for it.

JPMorgan Chase, Report of the Management Task Force regarding 2012 CIO losses (Jan 2013) · Herndon, Ash & Pollin (2013), Cambridge Journal of Economics · TransAlta, press reports, June 2003.

Keep this short: the point is that model review is ordinary corporate hygiene, not paranoia. Tie back to Session 1's broken file. Tonight's review is about assumptions rather than formulas, but the habit is the same: someone who did not build it checks it.
