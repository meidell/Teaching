What the course is actually about

Four models, one company, in the order they are built

Financial modelling is often taught as a gallery of finished models. That produces people who can recognise a DCF and cannot build one — and, worse, who cannot tell when someone else's is wrong. So here the work is constructive: eleven weeks, one blank sheet, and each session adds a layer that the next one depends on.

It starts with structure, because a model's real failure mode is not arithmetic, it is opacity: inputs scattered through formulas, hardcoded numbers, no check row, nobody able to say where a figure came from. Then the three statements, linked, and the moment every modeller meets — the balance sheet that will not balance and the circular reference you created fixing it.

Then valuation: unlevered free cash flow, WACC, terminal value — and the discovery that the output moves more on your terminal growth assumption than on anything you spent four weeks forecasting. Then the two transaction models, M&A and LBO, which are the same company valued by two buyers with completely different objectives. Which is the setup for the last session.

The claim this course is built onA model is an argument about the future with the assumptions made visible. Its value is not the number at the bottom — it is that a reader can find the three cells the number actually depends on and disagree with you about them. Everything about how we build here follows from that.

How it is taught, and why

The syllabus names hands-on workshops, case studies, team projects and class discussion. Those are real formats and they only work if the room is genuinely building — so the mechanics are fixed and they repeat every week:

  • Twenty minutes of me, at most. Enough for the vocabulary and the trap; the rest of the two hours your hands are on the keyboard.
  • Predict the number first. You write down what you think the output will be before you build it. Being an order of magnitude out, in writing, is the fastest way to develop judgement.
  • Build alone, review in pairs. Modelling is a solo skill and reviewing is a social one. You build your own file; every session ends with someone else trying to find an error in it.
  • Break my model. I bring a plausible, professionally-formatted model with a planted fault. Finding it is worth more than building something pretty, and the faults get subtler each time.
  • No templates. Not in the first ten sessions. You may build your own re-usable structure — that is exactly the skill — but nothing is handed over pre-wired.
  • Name the beneficiary. Every contentious assumption gets a sentence saying who gains if it is generous. That is the ethics component, and it is assessed.

Cold-calling is normal here and "pass" is always an acceptable answer — I will come back to you later. The point is that everyone is thinking, not that anyone is caught out.

The company · one case, all term

MERIDIA COLD, and the two people who want to buy it

Meridia Cold is a Barcelona-headquartered temperature-controlled logistics operator: fourteen warehouses across Spain, France and Italy, about €180 m of revenue, most of it on multi-year contracts to grocery chains and pharmaceutical distributors. It is capital-intensive, moderately levered, and its cash flows are boringly predictable — which is precisely why two very different buyers want it.

Boreal Logistics is a larger listed Dutch operator making a strategic bid: it can take out overlapping depots and claims synergies. Arco Partners is a private-equity sponsor making a financial bid: no synergies, but a great deal of debt and a five-year exit. Same company, same cash flows, two answers about what it is worth — and the reason the answers differ is the whole of Modules 4 and 5.

In presentation week your team advises Meridia's board. Not "here is our model" — take this bid, at this price, for these three reasons, and here is what we are assuming that could make us wrong. The panel will go looking for the assumption you were quietest about.

Why a fictional company and not a listed oneBecause I can put the faults in, and because a real target's answer is one search away. Meridia's financials are generated, internally consistent and deliberately awkward in the places that teach something — a lease reclassification, a one-off disposal in the comparative year, working capital that swings with the season. The techniques, the conventions and the failure modes are entirely real, and Session 6 benchmarks your output against genuine listed comparables.

Software · Excel, because that is what the job uses

Every model in this course is built in Microsoft Excel. It is the tool every bank, fund and corporate finance team on earth actually runs on, and the fluency is a large part of what the course is worth to you — so we use the real thing rather than something that resembles it. Bring a laptop with Excel installed and working before Session 1 (your GBS Microsoft 365 account includes it; the browser version is not enough, because it is missing the data tables and the keyboard shortcuts we rely on from week one).

Assessment · how the grade is built

Two components, set by the school, and no written exam. Half of your grade is participation — which in a course like this is either the fairest possible arrangement or the vaguest, depending entirely on whether it is evidenced. So it is evidenced: every session produces a file with your name on it.

50%
Participation
Not attendance, and not talking a lot. It is the eleven models you built: the predicted number you wrote before each session, the working file you left with, whether your check row ties, the errors you found in someone else's model, and the assumptions you named an owner for. Recorded every week, not recalled in January.
50%
Final project · group presentation
The board advice: your team's integrated model, live, and a recommendation between the two bids. Twelve minutes, then questions — including the panel opening a sheet and asking where a number came from. The model is submitted with it, and it is read.

There is no individual project mark — so participation is it

Worth saying plainly, because it changes how you should work: the school's scheme for this course has no continuous-assessment component and no individually-marked project. Fifty per cent is the group's presentation, and the only place your own work is graded on its own is participation.

That is why every session ends with your file rather than the team's, and why I ask for the link. It is the only evidence that distinguishes you from the person next to you — so a term of turning up without building leaves me nothing to mark generously, and a term of quietly building excellent models is fully visible even if you never speak first.

On free-ridingIn the presentation the panel may open any sheet and ask any team member where a number came from. That is not a trick; it is the same question a real investment committee asks, and it is the only defence a 50% group mark has.

Attendance, and generative AI

School policy: a maximum of two absences. More than two results in a fail or a dropped course, and justified absences go to the Academic department with their documentation (for example a medical certificate), not to me. With eleven sessions that is already nearly a fifth of the course — and because each session adds a layer to your own file, a missed week is a hole in the thing you are graded on, not a set of slides to borrow.

On AI: use it for formula syntax, for explaining a function, for finding the error you cannot see. That is ordinary tooling and I would rather you were good at it. What it must not do is choose your assumptions or write your interpretation. Any AI use in submitted work must be disclosed in full, including your prompts, and whether it was for form or for substance — unattributed use is plagiarism under the school's protocols and is treated as such.

The one that matters in this subjectA language model will produce a confident terminal growth rate, a plausible synergy figure and a WACC to two decimal places. Every one of those is a judgement about the future that somebody profits from. Outsourcing it is not cheating so much as missing the entire point of the course.
