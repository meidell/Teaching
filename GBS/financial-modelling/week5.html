What a forecast "from drivers" means. A driver is a business quantity that causes a line of the statements: the number of pallet positions Meridia rents out, the price per position, the days customers take to pay. A forecast from drivers computes each line from these quantities, so that anyone can ask "what if customers pay ten days later?" and see the answer. A forecast that writes "last year plus 3 %" cannot answer any question.
The bank's two covenants. A covenant is a promise in a loan contract that the bank checks every year; breaking one lets the bank demand its money back. Banca del Litorale asks for two: net debt ÷ EBITDA at most 3.0 (net debt = term debt + facility − cash), and EBITDA ÷ interest at least 4.0 (the interest cover).

✅ Step 2 · the operating forecast

Forecast the business from drivers

Read first · how each line is driven
  1. Revenue = pallet positions × price. Meridia rented out on average 220,000 pallet positions in FY2025 (a pallet position is one space for one pallet in a cold warehouse) at an average €820 a year each: 220,000 × €820 = €180.4 m. The base case grows the positions by 3.5 % a year and the price by 3.1 %: revenue grows by 1.035 × 1.031 − 1 = 6.7 %, the growth rate of Sessions 1 and 2, now explained by two things a manager can check.
  2. Working capital in days. Receivables are the money customers owe: if they pay after 60 days on average, receivables are revenue × 60 ÷ 365. Inventory (spare parts and packaging) and payables (what Meridia owes its suppliers) are driven by the operating costs, revenue minus EBITDA: inventory 6 days, payables 34 days. FY2025's balance sheet gives about these numbers (receivables 29.6 = 59.9 days). When receivables rise, cash falls by the same amount.
  3. Capex against depreciation. Depreciation (D&A) spreads the cost of the fixed assets over their useful life: the base case uses the opening fixed assets ÷ 12 years. Capex is 8 % of revenue. When capex is larger than depreciation, the fixed assets grow; the ratio capex ÷ D&A says by how much the company is investing beyond replacing what wears out.
  4. Blue cells are inputs. In the table below, only the yellow boxes with blue figures can be changed; every other figure is computed from them, as in the model convention of Session 1.
  1. Read the drivers below and the operating forecast they produce.
  2. Answer the five checks under the table from the table. Each must show a green ✓.
  3. Change one driver at a time, watch what moves, then put it back (press Reset to the base case). Then write which driver matters most for FY2030 EBITDA and why.

The same structure in another business: a gym chain
A gym chain's revenue is members × monthly fee × 12, not "last year plus 5 %". Its working capital is the reverse of Meridia's: members pay in advance, so the chain holds deferred revenue (a liability) and grows its cash when it grows its members. Its capex is the fit-out of new gyms, far above depreciation in a year of openings. The model has the same rows; the drivers say what kind of business it is.
✅ Step 3 · the plug

Close the balance sheet with a minimum cash balance and the facility

Read first · why a forecast needs a plug
  1. The forecast decides every line except one. Revenue, costs, working capital, capex, the scheduled debt repayment of €2.0 m a year and the dividend are all decided by drivers. What is left over is the cash. In a forecast the balance sheet balances only if one line takes whatever amount is needed: that line is called the plug.
  2. Cash alone is a bad plug. If cash is the plug, a bad year simply shows a negative cash balance, which no company can have. A real company keeps a minimum cash balance (Meridia: €8.0 m, to pay wages and suppliers on time) and borrows on its revolving credit facility when cash would fall below it.
  3. The cash-and-debt plug. Each year: cash available = opening cash + cash from operations − capex − scheduled repayment − dividend. If that is below the minimum, the facility is drawn by the difference. If it is above the minimum and the facility has a balance, the facility is repaid first. In Excel: =MAX(MinCash − CashAvailable, −OpeningFacility).
  4. For now, interest is charged on the opening debt, the debt at the start of each year. Step 4 explains why that is a choice.
  1. Switch between the two plugs below and look at FY2026 in the lender case: what does "cash only" show?
  2. Switch back to minimum cash + facility and read the balance check: it must be 0.0 in every year.
  3. Answer the four checks, then write in one sentence why the bank prefers the second plug.
In Excel: the plug rows

✅ Step 4 · the circularity

Handle the circularity: interest on average debt

Read first · how the circle closes
  1. Interest is paid all year on a balance that changes during the year. Charging it on the opening balance overstates it when debt falls and understates it when debt rises. The usual fix is interest on the average balance: (opening + closing) ÷ 2 × the rate, 4.6 % for Meridia.
  2. That creates a circle. The closing facility depends on the cash available, which depends on net income, which depends on interest, which now depends on the closing facility. Excel calls this a circular reference and warns about it.
  3. A circle like this one has an answer, and repeating the calculation finds it: start with a guess for the interest, compute the facility, recompute the interest, and so on. Each round moves less, because the interest on a change in the facility (4.6 % of it, after 24 % tax) is much smaller than the change. The table below shows the rounds for FY2026.
  4. There are two legitimate ways to handle it. (1) Switch on iterative calculation in Excel (File → Options → Formulas → Enable iterative calculation, maximum 100 iterations, maximum change 0.001) and add a circuit breaker: an input cell that, set to 1, replaces the average by the opening balance, so a broken model can be reset. (2) Avoid the circle: charge interest on the opening balance, and accept a small error. A model that hides the circle with a copy-paste macro, or leaves Excel's warning on, is not one of the two.

  2. Switch between the two interest methods and compare FY2026 interest and the FY2030 facility.
  3. Answer the four checks, then write which method your team uses for the reply and why.
✅ Step 5 · the bank's questions

Answer the bank: the lender case, the covenants and the dividend

Why this step exists. A bank does not lend on the company's own plan. It asks for a lender case, a forecast it considers cautious, and checks whether the company still pays its debt in it. Banca del Litorale's lender case: pallet positions +1.0 % a year, price +2.0 %, EBITDA margin 14.5 %, capex 7.5 % of revenue, and customers paying after 70 days instead of 60. The dividend is the one cash outflow the owners decide every year, so it is the first thing a bank wants to limit.

  1. Switch between the base case and the lender case and read the covenant rows: net debt ÷ EBITDA (at most 3.0) and EBITDA ÷ interest (at least 4.0). The page uses interest on the average balance.

Terms from today
  • Driver. A business quantity that causes a line of the statements: pallet positions, price, days customers take to pay.
  • Days of working capital. Receivables = revenue × days ÷ 365; inventory and payables on the operating costs.
  • Capex ÷ D&A. Above 1, the company invests beyond replacing what wears out.
  • Plug. The line that takes whatever amount makes the balance sheet balance; in a good model, the revolving facility above a minimum cash balance.
  • Revolving credit facility. A line of credit with a limit that the company draws and repays as its cash needs change.
  • Circular reference. A formula that depends, through other cells, on itself: here interest → net income → cash → facility → interest.
  • Iterative calculation · circuit breaker. Excel repeats the calculation until it stops moving; a switch cell replaces the circle by the opening balance.
  • Covenant · lender case. A promise in a loan contract that the bank tests every year; the cautious forecast the bank tests it on.
