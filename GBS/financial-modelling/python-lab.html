Exercise 1 · lists and arithmetic

Calculate Meridia's revenue growth in FY2024 and FY2025, and the average growth over the two years

Why. The base case assumes 6.7 % growth a year. Before the board accepts that number, it compares it with what Meridia actually achieved. If the history is lower, the forecast needs a reason.

A list holds several values in order: revenue[0] is the first (FY2023), revenue[2] the third (FY2025). Growth in a year is this year ÷ last year − 1. The CAGR (compound annual growth rate) is the single yearly rate that turns the first value into the last: (last ÷ first) to the power 1 ÷ number of years, minus 1. In Python, ** means "to the power".

Your task

  1. Create growth, a list of two numbers: the growth of FY2024 and of FY2025, in % (multiply by 100).
  2. Create cagr, the CAGR from FY2023 to FY2025 (two years), in %.
  3. Print both.

Exercise 3 · free cash flow

Calculate Meridia's free cash flow for FY2026 to FY2028

Why. A buyer pays for the cash the business will produce, not for its profit. Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after tax, after the investment in warehouses (capex) and after the money tied up in working capital: the cash that could be paid to lenders and shareholders. It is the number the valuation discounts in Exercise 4.

The formula of Session 1, for each year: FCF = (EBITDA − D&A) × (1 − 24 %) + D&A − capex − increase in working capital. D&A is added back because it is a cost in the profit but not a payment of cash. The starting code already has revenue and EBITDA from Exercise 2. zip(a, b) walks through two lists side by side.

Your task

  1. Inside the loop, calculate the year's D&A (5 % above last year's, starting from FY2025's 10.6), capex (8 % of the year's revenue), and the increase in working capital (10 % of the year's revenue minus last year's revenue).
  2. Append the year's FCF to fcf.
revenue_fc = [180.4 * 1.067 ** n for n in (1, 2, 3)] # from Exercise 2 ebitda_fc = [r * 0.161 for r in revenue_fc] tax = 0.24 da = 10.6 # FY2025 D&A prev_rev = 180.4 # FY2025 revenue fcf = [] for rev, ebitda in zip(revenue_fc, ebitda_fc): da = da # replace: 5 % more than last year capex = 0 # replace: 8 % of rev nwc = 0 # replace: 10 % of (rev - prev_rev) fcf.append((ebitda - da) * (1 - tax) + da - capex - nwc) prev_rev = rev print("FCF FY2026–28, € million:", [round(x, 2) for x in fcf])
Exercise 4 · discounting

Value Meridia with a discounted cash flow, and turn the value into a price per share

Why. A euro in 2028 is worth less than a euro today, because today's euro could be invested in the meantime. A discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation divides each future cash flow by (1 + WACC) to the power of the number of years until it arrives. This gives its present value. The cash flows after FY2028 are summed in one terminal value, which assumes they grow at 2 % a year forever. Their sum is the enterprise value (EV), the value of the whole business; the shareholders get what is left after the net debt.
  • Present value of the FCF of year t (t = 1 for FY2026, 2, 3): FCF ÷ (1 + 0.085) ** t.
  • Terminal value at the end of FY2028 (the Gordon growth formula): FCF of FY2028 × (1 + g) ÷ (WACC − g). Its present value divides it by (1.085) ** 3.
  • EV = the sum of the three present values + the present value of the terminal value. Equity = EV − net debt of 60.0. Price per share = equity ÷ 40.0 million shares.

Your task

  1. Create pv_fcf (the sum of the three present values), pv_tv, ev and price, all in € million except the price, which is in € per share.

Exercise 5 · a table with pandas

Build a sensitivity table of the price per share for five WACCs and five growth rates

Why. Nobody knows the WACC or the long-term growth exactly, and Exercise 4 showed that 84 % of the value depends on them. A sensitivity table shows the price for every combination, so the board sees the range instead of one number. In Excel this is a data table; in Python it is a loop and a pandas DataFrame, a table with labelled rows and columns.

The page has already loaded a function value(wacc, g) that runs Exercises 2 to 4 and returns the price per share; value(8.5, 2.0) gives 2.48. Both arguments are in %. [f(x) for x in list] builds a list in one line.

Your task
