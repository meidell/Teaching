Where we start. The base-case forecast of Session 4: revenue grows from €180.4 m in FY2025 to €249.6 m in FY2030 (pallet positions +3.5 % and price +3.1 % a year), EBITDA stays at 16.1 % of revenue and reaches €40.2 m in FY2030, capex is 8 % of revenue, and working capital is driven by days. At the end of FY2025 Meridia has term debt of €69.0 m and cash of €9.0 m, so its net debt (debt minus cash) is €60.0 m. It has 40.0 million shares. The valuation date is 31 December 2025, the date of the last balance sheet: every cash flow is valued as of that day.
What a DCF is. A discounted cash flow valuation (DCF) values a business as the cash it will produce, with each future euro converted into today's euros at a rate that pays investors for waiting and for the risk. The result is the enterprise value: the value of the whole business, before the debt is paid off. The shareholders own what is left after the net debt.
What the terminal value is. A forecast stops after five years, but Meridia does not. The terminal value is one number, at the end of FY2030, that stands for all the cash flows from FY2031 onwards. Step 4 computes it two ways.
✅ Step 2 · free cash flow

Compute Meridia's free cash flow from the Session 4 forecast

Read first · what free cash flow counts, and what it leaves out
  1. Free cash flow to the firm (FCFF) is the cash the business produces in a year after tax, after paying for new fixed assets (capex) and after the money tied up in working capital, before any payment to lenders or owners. It is the cash that the lenders and the owners, together, could take out of the company. This page uses FCFF throughout.
  2. The formula. FCFF = EBIT × (1 − tax rate) + D&A − capex − increase in working capital. EBIT (earnings before interest and tax) is EBITDA minus depreciation and amortisation (D&A). EBIT × (1 − tax rate) is called NOPAT, net operating profit after tax: the profit the business would make if it had no debt. D&A is added back because it is a cost in the income statement but no cash leaves the company.
  3. Why not net income. Net income has the interest already subtracted, and interest is a payment to the lenders, who are paid out of FCFF. Net income also ignores capex and working capital, which are real cash.
  4. Why not EBITDA. EBITDA ignores tax, capex and working capital. In Meridia's forecast capex alone takes about half of EBITDA, so FCFF is only about 30 % of EBITDA.
  5. Tax on EBIT, not the tax Meridia actually pays. Meridia pays less tax than 24 % of EBIT because interest is deductible. That saving exists because of the debt, so it is counted in the cost of debt in Step 3 (the cost of debt after tax). Counting it in the cash flow as well would count it twice.
  6. FCFF does not change with the dividend or with the interest method of Session 4. Neither of them changes EBIT, capex or working capital. Session 1's simpler model gave FY2026 free cash flow of €9.62 m, because it set D&A at 5 % of revenue and working capital at 10 % of new revenue; tonight's forecast computes both from the balance sheet.

The same structure in another business: a software company sold by annual subscription
A software company has almost no capex (offices and laptops), and its customers pay the year in advance, so its working capital is negative and grows more negative as it grows. Its FCFF is therefore often higher than its net income, the opposite of Meridia. The formula is the same; capex and working capital say what kind of business it is.
✅ Step 3 · the cost of capital

Build the WACC from market data

Read first · where each part of the WACC comes from
  1. The WACC (weighted average cost of capital) is the yearly return that lenders and owners, together, require for financing Meridia. Each one's required return is weighted by its share of the financing: WACC = E ÷ (D + E) × cost of equity + D ÷ (D + E) × cost of debt × (1 − tax rate). D is debt and E is equity, both at market value. FCFF goes to lenders and owners together, so it is discounted at their combined rate.
  2. The cost of equity comes from the capital asset pricing model (CAPM): risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium. The risk-free rate is the yield of a government bond that will certainly be repaid, in the currency of the cash flows: the 10-year German government bond (Bund) yielded 2.85 % on 31 December 2025. The equity risk premium (ERP) is the extra yearly return investors expect from shares above that bond. Aswath Damodaran (New York University) publishes it every January for each country; it is higher in countries with more risk.
  3. Beta measures how much a company's share price moves when the whole market moves: 1.0 moves with the market, 0.5 half as much. Meridia is not listed, so it has no share price and no beta. You borrow the beta of listed companies in the same business, the comparables. Their betas include the effect of their debt, so you first remove it (unlevered beta) and then put back Meridia's: levered beta = unlevered beta × (1 + (1 − tax rate) × D ÷ E).
  4. A premium for size and for being unlisted. The CAPM is estimated on large listed companies. Meridia is small, and its shares cannot be sold on a stock exchange, so buyers usually ask for a few points more. There is no market price for this premium: it is a judgement. Boreal's investment committee adds 3.0 points to the cost of equity for unlisted companies of Meridia's size. Your reply must state it.
  5. The cost of debt is what Meridia pays to borrow today, 4.6 % a year (Session 4), which is 1.75 points above the Bund. Interest is deductible, so the cost after tax is 4.6 % × (1 − 24 %). The weights use the debt-to-equity ratio of the comparables, 0.77, because Meridia's own market value is what we are trying to find.

✅ Step 4 · discounting and the terminal value

Discount the cash flows and compute the terminal value two ways

Read first · from cash flows to a value
  1. The discount factor converts a euro received later into today's euros: 1 ÷ (1 + WACC)t, where t is the number of years from the valuation date (31 December 2025) to the end of the year of the cash flow: 1 for FY2026, 5 for FY2030. The page assumes each year's cash arrives on its last day, as in Session 1.
  2. The terminal value by perpetuity growth (also called the Gordon growth formula): if FCFF grows by g every year for ever, its value at the end of FY2030 is FCFF of FY2031 ÷ (WACC − g), with FCFF of FY2031 = FCFF of FY2030 × (1 + g). The page uses g = 2.0 % a year, which is the European Central Bank's inflation target: Meridia then grows only as fast as prices.
  3. The terminal value by exit multiple: the price a buyer would pay for Meridia at the end of FY2030, if companies then sell for the same multiple of their EBITDA as listed companies do today. TV = multiple × FY2030 EBITDA. Damodaran's European data of 5 January 2026 give an enterprise value of 12.6 times EBITDA for transportation companies (56 companies) and 9.1 times for trucking companies (7 companies). The page uses 9.1, the lower of the two.
  4. Both terminal values are values at the end of FY2030, so both are multiplied by the FY2030 discount factor. Enterprise value = the sum of the discounted FCFF + the discounted terminal value. Equity value = enterprise value − net debt of €60.0 m. Price per share = equity value ÷ 40.0 million shares.

  2. Read the table from the top: each year's FCFF, its discount factor and its value today; then the two terminal values and the two prices per share.

✅ Step 5 · testing the terminal value

Test the terminal value four ways, then write reply parts 3 and 4

Why this step exists. About four fifths of Meridia's value sits in one number, the terminal value, which rests on two inputs nobody can observe: the growth rate for ever and the multiple a buyer will pay in five years. A valuer cannot prove these inputs, but can test whether they are consistent with each other and with the forecast. These four tests are what Boreal's committee will ask about first.

Read first · four tests of a terminal value
  1. Test 1 · the share of the value in the terminal value. The higher it is, the more the value depends on g and the WACC instead of on the forecast your team checked. Report it in the reply, always.
  2. Test 2 · the multiple hidden in the perpetuity value. Divide the perpetuity terminal value by FY2030 EBITDA: this is the EV ÷ EBITDA multiple that the perpetuity method assumes a buyer would pay. Compare it with the 9.1 and 12.6 of listed companies. A large gap means one of the two methods is wrong for Meridia, and the reply must say which.
  3. Test 3 · the growth hidden in the exit multiple. Turn the formula around: the growth rate for ever that the exit multiple assumes is g = (TV × WACC − FCFF of FY2030) ÷ (TV + FCFF of FY2030). Compare it with 2 %. A company cannot grow faster than the whole economy for ever, because it would end up larger than the economy.
  4. Test 4 · the investment in the terminal year. In FY2030 Meridia still invests for growth of 6.7 % a year (capex 8 % of revenue). After FY2030 it grows only 2 %, so it needs less: enough capex to replace what wears out (D&A) plus 2 % more fixed assets, and 2 % more working capital. The normalised FCFF of FY2031 uses those amounts: EBITDA FY2030 × 1.02, D&A = fixed assets at the end of FY2030 ÷ 12, capex = fixed assets × (1 ÷ 12 + 2 %), working capital increase = working capital at the end of FY2030 × 2 %.

Terms from today
  • Free cash flow to the firm (FCFF). The cash the business produces after tax, capex and working capital, before paying lenders or owners: EBIT × (1 − tax rate) + D&A − capex − increase in working capital.
  • EBIT · NOPAT. Earnings before interest and tax (EBITDA − D&A); net operating profit after tax, EBIT × (1 − tax rate).
  • WACC. The weighted average cost of capital: the return lenders and owners together require, each weighted by its market-value share of the financing.
  • CAPM · risk-free rate · equity risk premium · beta. Cost of equity = risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium; the yield of a safe government bond; the extra return expected from shares; how much a share moves with the market.
  • Unlevered and levered beta. The comparables' beta with the effect of their debt removed; the same beta with Meridia's debt put back.
  • Discount factor. 1 ÷ (1 + WACC)t: what one euro at the end of year t is worth on the valuation date.
  • Terminal value · perpetuity growth · exit multiple. One value at the end of the forecast for all later years; FCFF of the next year ÷ (WACC − g); a multiple of EBITDA that a buyer would pay.
  • Normalised terminal year. The first year after the forecast, with capex and working capital set for the growth rate of the terminal value instead of the growth rate of the forecast.
