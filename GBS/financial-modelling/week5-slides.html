Part 1 · drivers

A driver is a business quantity that causes a line of the statements, so the forecast can answer "what if"

"Last year plus 6.7 %"

Revenue FY2026 = 180.4 × 1.067. Correct arithmetic, but nobody can ask what happens if Meridia loses a customer, or raises prices less.

From drivers

Revenue = pallet positions × price per position. FY2025: 220,000 × €820 = €180.4 m. Growth: positions +3.5 %, price +3.1 %, so revenue grows by 1.035 × 1.031 − 1 = 6.7 %.

The two forecasts give the same number. Only the second one tells a manager which quantity to check, and tells the bank which assumption to doubt.

Ask: who in the company can confirm 3.5 % more pallet positions? The sales director, from signed contracts. Who can confirm 3.1 % on price? The contracts' indexation clauses. Nobody can confirm "+6.7 %".

Part 1 · working capital in days

Working capital is money tied up in the business: what customers still owe, plus stock, minus what you still owe suppliers

Receivables = revenue × days ÷ 365  ·  Inventory, payables = operating costs × days ÷ 365
  • Receivables: sales already counted as revenue, but not yet paid in cash. 30 days means customers pay one month after the sale.
  • Inventory: goods bought but not yet used. Payables: goods received but not yet paid for.
  • Net working capital = receivables + inventory − payables.
Meridia FY2025: receivables 29.6, revenue 180.4. How many days do its customers take to pay?
Answer: 29.6 ÷ 180.4 × 365 = 59.9 days. The page rounds this to 60 days as the base-case driver.
The reason for days: days are a policy a manager controls (payment terms), and they stay roughly stable while revenue grows, so the balance grows with the business automatically.
Part 1 · working capital · worked example

When working capital grows, cash falls by the same amount, even though profit is unchanged

Forno Lumen, € thousandFY2025FY2026Change
Receivables: 2,600 × 30 ÷ 365164.4213.7+49.3
Inventory: 2,288 × 5 ÷ 36524.131.3+7.2
Payables: 2,288 × 20 ÷ 36596.4125.4+29.0
Net working capital92.1119.7+27.6

The increase of 27.6 is cash the bakery spends on growing: hotels owe it more, and it holds more flour. Payables work the other way: owing suppliers more keeps cash in the business.

The other direction exists too: a gym chain is paid in advance by its members, so its working capital is negative and growth brings cash in.
The rule to repeat: an increase in an asset (receivables, inventory) uses cash; an increase in a liability (payables) provides cash. This is exactly the sign error planted in Session 3 (+1.4 instead of −1.4), revisited in Part 5.
Part 1 · capex and depreciation

Capex is cash spent on fixed assets now; depreciation spreads that cost over the years the assets are used

D&A = opening fixed assets ÷ useful life  ·  closing fixed assets = opening + capex − D&A
Forno Lumen FY2026, € thousand
D&A = 1,200 ÷ 10 years120.0
Capex = new shop 400 + replacement 100500.0
Capex ÷ D&A4.2
Closing fixed assets = 1,200 + 500 − 1201,580.0

A ratio above 1 means the company invests more than what wears out: it is growing its assets. Meridia FY2025: capex 14.2 ÷ D&A 10.6 = 1.34. The bakery's 4.2 is a year of expansion, and it will need financing.

Two things to stress: (1) capex is cash and never goes through the income statement; D&A goes through the income statement and is not cash. (2) Meridia's base case sets capex at 8 % of revenue; the page asks for the FY2026 ratio.

Quick and mechanical; the class has seen this structure since Session 3. EBITDA = earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation.
Part 1 · from net income to cash

Cash from operations adds back depreciation, which costs no cash, and subtracts the increase in working capital

Cash from operations = net income + D&A − increase in working capital
Forno Lumen FY2026: net income 129.6, D&A 120.0, working capital +27.6. What is cash from operations?
Answer: 129.6 + 120.0 − 27.6 = 222.0. The bakery earns 129.6 of profit but generates 222.0 of cash from running its shops, mainly because 120.0 of the cost was spent years ago.
Ask before revealing. If someone subtracts D&A, ask: did the bakery pay anyone 120 this year? No: the money left when the ovens were bought.
Part 2 · the plug

In a forecast, every line is decided by a driver except one, and that line must take whatever amount makes the balance sheet balance

Decided by driversRevenue, costs, working capital, capex, the scheduled loan repayment, the dividend
Left overCash. It is whatever remains after all the decisions.
The plugThe one line that takes the leftover, so assets = liabilities + equity
Cash available = opening cash + cash from operations − capex − repayment − dividend

A model without a plug does not balance, or balances only because somebody typed a number. In Session 1 the broken file had exactly that: a hidden plug row.

The word "plug" is the modellers' word. Insist that a plug is a formula, never a typed number.

Part 2 · minimum cash and a credit facility

A real company keeps a minimum cash balance and borrows on a revolving credit facility when cash would fall below it

A revolving credit facility is a line of credit with a limit, like an overdraft: the company draws on it when cash runs short and repays it when cash comes back.

Draw (+) or repay (−) = MAX( minimum cash − cash available , − opening facility )
Minimum cash 100Cash availableOpening facilityDraw / repayClosing cash
Short of cash600+40100

The MAX stops the facility going below zero: you cannot repay more than you owe.

Walk the three rows with the formula: MAX(100−60, −0) = 40; MAX(100−130, −50) = −30; MAX(100−180, −50) = −50, so the extra 30 stays as cash (closing 180 − 50 = 130). In Excel on the page: =MAX(MinCash − CashAvailable, −OpeningFacility).

Part 2 · the balance check

The balance check is one formula per year that must read zero, and when it does not, it says where to look

Balance check = ROUND( total assets − total liabilities and equity , 3 )
  • Put it under the balance sheet, one cell per year, and feed it to the master check on the cover (Session 1).
  • If it is not zero, the cause is almost always one of the four you corrected in Session 3: a working-capital sign, a dividend missing from the cash flow, retained earnings built from profit before tax, or a figure carried forward instead of rolled forward.
  • A plug hides none of these: the facility takes only what the cash flow says is missing.
On the page, Step 3: switch between "cash only" and "minimum cash + facility", and look at the lender case.
Do not say what "cash only" shows for Meridia: that is a homework check.
Part 3 · interest on the average balance

Interest is paid all year on a balance that changes during the year, so the average balance is closer to the truth than the opening one

Interest = rate × ( opening debt + closing debt ) ÷ 2

Opening balance

Forno Lumen: 5 % × 600 = 30.0. But the loan falls to 500 during the year, and the facility rises from 0 to 366.9. The bakery owes far more than 600 for most of the year.

Average balance

Term loan (600 + 500) ÷ 2 = 550. Facility (0 + 366.9) ÷ 2 = 183.4. Interest = 5 % × 733.4 = 36.7.

But 366.9 was computed with interest of 30.0. With interest of 36.7, net income is lower, cash is lower, and the facility draw is larger. The number depends on itself.

This is the hinge of the evening: let the room feel the problem before showing the circle.
Part 3 · the circular reference

The circle: interest depends on the facility, and the facility depends on the interest

Intereston the average facility
Net incomefalls when interest rises
Cash availablefalls with net income
Facility drawrises to keep minimum cash
Interestrises again, a little

Excel calls a formula that depends on itself, through other cells, a circular reference, and shows a warning. This circle has an answer, because each turn is much smaller than the last: one more euro of facility adds 5 % × ½ = 2.5 cents of interest, of which 80 % remains after tax.

The convergence argument in one line: the feedback factor is about 0.025 × 0.8 × 0.7 (the share of net income kept after the 30 % dividend), about 1.4 %. Each round moves about one seventieth of the round before.

Part 3 · two correct ways

There are two correct ways to handle the circle, and one way that is never acceptable

1 · Let Excel iterate

File → Options → Formulas → Enable iterative calculation, 100 iterations, maximum change 0.001. Add a circuit breaker: one input cell that, set to 1, replaces the average by the opening balance, so a model that blows up can be reset.

2 · Avoid the circle

Charge interest on the opening balance and accept the small error. Simple and stable; say in the model that you did it.

Never

Leaving Excel's circular-reference warning on and ignoring it, or a macro that copies and pastes values until it stops, hidden from the reader.

On the page, Step 4: read Meridia's rounds, switch between the two methods, and write which one your team uses and why.
On a Mac: Excel → Preferences → Calculation → Use iterative calculation. The circuit breaker formula: =IF(Breaker=1, rate × opening, rate × average).
Part 4 · covenants

A covenant is a promise in a loan contract that the bank tests every year; breaking it lets the bank demand its money back

Leverage: net debt ÷ EBITDA, at most 3.0

Net debt = term debt + facility − cash. It says how many years of EBITDA it would take to repay the debt.

Forno Lumen: (500.0 + 370.7 − 100.0) ÷ 312.0 = 2.47.

Interest cover: EBITDA ÷ interest, at least 4.0

It says how many times the year's profit before depreciation covers the interest.

Forno Lumen: 312.0 ÷ 36.8 = 8.5.

Headroom is how far the business can fall before the covenant breaks. Forno Lumen breaks the 3.0 limit if EBITDA falls to 770.7 ÷ 3.0 = 256.9, which is 18 % below plan.

The bakery uses the average-interest figures (facility 370.7, interest 36.8). Headroom in words is what a bank reads: "EBITDA can fall by 18 % before we may call the loan."

Part 4 · the dividend

The dividend is the one cash outflow the owners decide every year, so it is the first thing a bank wants to limit

Forno Lumen draws 370.7 on its facility in FY2026 and pays a dividend of 37.3. Where does the dividend's cash come from?
Answer: from the bank. Even with no dividend, cash available is −233.4, so every euro of the dividend is borrowed. A bank will either forbid the dividend in a year with a draw, or cap the payout at a level that the cash flow pays for.

On the page, Step 5 asks the same question for Meridia: the highest payout at which the base case never draws the facility, and what the lender case says about FY2026.

The bakery dividend with average interest is 30 % × 124.2 = 37.3. Ask: would you, as the bank, accept a dividend paid with our money? The room usually says no; then ask what the owners will say.

Part 5 · review · Session 3

In the cash flow statement, an increase in receivables is a minus, because the sale was counted as profit but no cash came in

The rule

Asset up (receivables, inventory) → cash down. Liability up (payables) → cash up.

Change in working capital in the cash flow = −(this year's NWC − last year's NWC).

The planted error in Session 3

The FY2024 cash flow showed working capital at +1.4. Working capital had grown by 1.4, so the correct line was −1.4. One sign, and the cash in the cash flow no longer matched the balance sheet by 2.8.

Tonight's Forno Lumen example: working capital grew by 27.6, so cash from operations was 129.6 + 120.0 − 27.6.

2.8 = twice 1.4: the wrong sign moves the number by double its size. That doubling is a fingerprint worth teaching: a difference that is exactly twice a line item is almost always a sign error.

Part 5 · review · Session 1

A value is today's worth of the future cash flows: the forecast years plus a terminal value for all the years after

EV = Σ cash flow ÷ (1 + WACC)t + terminal value ÷ (1 + WACC)N
  • WACC, the weighted average cost of capital: the return lenders and owners together require, 8.5 % for Meridia.
  • Terminal value (Gordon growth): next year's cash flow ÷ (WACC − g), with g = 2 % growth for ever.
  • Meridia: present value of the forecast years 26.04 + present value of the terminal value 133.32 = EV 159.36. The terminal value is 84 % of the value.

WACC 9 % instead of 8.5 % gives EV 147.9: half a point of WACC moves the value by 11.5. Most of the value is in the years nobody forecasts.

EV = enterprise value, the value of the whole business before debt. Session 5 (19 Nov) takes free cash flow and the WACC apart; this slide is only the shape.
