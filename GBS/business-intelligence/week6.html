Describing one number: the mean, the median and the percentiles

Descriptive statistics are the numbers that summarise data as it is, before anyone tests a cause or makes a forecast. Three of them describe how a number such as "rides per subscriber in October" is spread over the subscribers:

  • The mean (the average) adds up all the values and divides by how many there are. Five subscribers who ride 0, 2, 7, 15 and 120 times have a mean of 144 ÷ 5 = 28.8 rides. One heavy rider pulls the mean far above what four of the five did.
  • The median is the middle value once the values are sorted: half of the subscribers are below it and half above. For the same five subscribers it is 7 rides. One heavy rider does not move it.
  • A percentile says how many values lie below a point. The 90th percentile (P90) is the value that 90 % of the subscribers do not exceed. The 25th and 75th percentiles (P25 and P75) mark where the middle half of the subscribers lies.

The distribution is the whole picture: how many subscribers rode 0 times, 1 to 4 times, 5 to 9 times, and so on. A distribution is skewed when a long tail of large values sits on one side, as it does for rides and for money. When a distribution is skewed, the mean and the median differ, and the median describes the typical case better.

Describing a rate: segments, cohorts and the mix

A rate such as churn (cancellations ÷ paying accounts on the 1st of the month) can be split in two ways:

  • A segment is a group of accounts that share a property: a city, a plan, a sales channel. Splitting by segment answers "where is churn high?".
  • A cohort is a group of accounts that joined in the same month: the July 2026 cohort is every account that signed up in July. Splitting by cohort answers "do the subscribers we win now stay as long as those we won before?". The tenure of an account is how many months it has been subscribed.

The mix is the share of each group in the total. A company rate is an average of its groups' rates, weighted by the mix. So a company rate can change because the groups' rates change, or only because the mix changes: if a city wins many new subscribers, who always leave more often than old ones, the city's churn rises even if no subscriber behaves differently. When the split and the total point in opposite directions, statisticians call it Simpson's paradox.

The one rule for todayNever report a company-wide number without having looked at how it is spread and how it splits by the groups someone can act on. A number that is the same as last month can hide two movements that cancel out.

Seven terms from today

  • Mean and median. The mean adds the values and divides by their number; the median is the middle value once they are sorted. When the data is skewed, the median describes the typical case.
  • Percentile. The value that a given share of the data does not exceed: 90 % of the accounts rode 33 times or fewer, so P90 is 33 rides.
  • Distribution. How many cases take each value: the whole shape, not one summary.
  • Segment. A group of accounts that share a property, such as a city or a plan.
  • Cohort and tenure. A cohort is the accounts that joined in the same month; tenure is how many months an account has been subscribed.
  • Mix. The share of each group in a total. A total rate can move only because the mix moves.
  • Actionable. A finding that names a group someone can find in the data and act on before the outcome happens.
In Session 6, ÒRBITA asks whether a price change in one city lowered its churn. Before you see the data: what do you expect the test to say?
After today you can explain why the mean of a skewed number such as rides describes nobody, and what the median and the percentiles say instead; how to split a rate by segment and by cohort; how a change in the mix moves a total rate when no group's rate has moved; how to tell a mix effect from a change in every group; and what makes a finding actionable.
