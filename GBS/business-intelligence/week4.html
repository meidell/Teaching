Two databases, two jobs

ÒRBITA's billing system is an operational database (OLTP, online transaction processing). It is built to do one small thing thousands of times a day, safely: charge a card, pause a subscription, change a plan. Each request reads or writes a handful of rows, and the tables are split so that every fact is stored once and an update touches one place. It is very good at that job.

A KPI is a different job. "Monthly churn by city and plan for the last twelve months" reads every subscription for a year, joins it to rides from telematics and tickets from the help desk, and adds it up. That is analytic work (OLAP, online analytical processing): few requests, each one reading millions of rows. Run it on the billing database and three things go wrong. It competes with the card payments for the same machine. It cannot see rides or tickets, which live in other systems with other keys. And its columns carry billing's definitions — the status column from Session 1 — not the ones your contract wrote down. A warehouse is a second database, built for the second job and filled from the first.

Operational or analytic? Six requests

Warehouse, lake, lakehouse

Three names for where analytic data is kept. They are distinguished by when the structure is imposed and who reads the data, not by brand.

Data warehouseData lakeLakehouse
What is storedTables with fixed, documented columnsFiles of any kind, as they arrived: CSV, JSON, logs, imagesFiles in open table formats (Delta Lake, Apache Iceberg, Apache Hudi) on cheap cloud storage
StructureSchema on write: a row that does not fit is rejected when it is loadedSchema on read: the structure is applied by whoever reads the filesSchema enforced on the files by a transaction layer
Who reads itSQL and dashboards: the board's numbersData scientists and engineers, often in PythonBoth, from one copy of the data
Typical failureSlow and expensive to change when a new question arrivesThe "data swamp": nobody knows what a file means or whether it is currentNewer; more parts to run well

The lakehouse is described in Armbrust, Ghodsi, Xin and Zaharia, Lakehouse: A New Generation of Open Platforms that Unify Data Warehousing and Advanced Analytics (CIDR 2021). Whichever ÒRBITA buys, the design question is the same: what one row of each table means. That is Step 2.

✅ Step 2 · 15 min

The grain machine

The grain of a table is what one row means, said in one sentence that starts "one row per…". It is chosen before any column, because it decides which questions the table can ever answer. Below are six questions from last week's contracts and six candidate tables, each with a different grain. Row counts are for one month, from ÒRBITA's sizes: 47,700 accounts on billing's books, about 560,000 rides (roughly 14 per paying subscriber), about 3,800 tickets, six cities, three ad channels.

Four tables, one per business process

No single grain answers all six. Rides, subscriptions, tickets and ad spend are four business processes, each with its own natural grain: one row per ride, one row per account per month, one row per ticket, one row per city, channel and week. The subscriber-per-day table also answers the churn and retention questions, at thirty times the rows; it is the right choice only if a question needs the day. The city-per-day summary answers nothing on the list: once rows are added up, the subscriber, the hour and the fourteen-day window are gone, and no query can bring them back.

This is Kimball's rule, from The Data Warehouse Toolkit (Kimball and Ross, 3rd ed., 2013): one fact table per business process, at the most detailed grain the process produces. The design runs in four steps — choose the process, declare the grain, identify the dimensions, identify the facts — in that order.

✅ Step 3 · 12 min · reading

Read: facts, dimensions, the star

Do this: each member reads this step (about 8 minutes) and sorts the eight columns at the end. The step is done when all eight are sorted correctly. You need it for Step 4.

Once the grain is declared, every column of a fact table is one of two kinds. A fact (or measure) is a number the process produces at that grain, usually one you add up: kilometres ridden, minutes, euros of revenue, hours to resolve. A dimension is the context you filter and group by: when, where, who, which plan, which channel. Dimensions live in their own tables, wide and descriptive, and the fact table holds a key to each. Drawn, the fact table sits in the middle and the dimensions around it: a star schema.

Five terms that come with it:

  • Date dimension. One row per calendar day, with weekday, month, quarter, public holiday, fiscal period. Every fact table has one, so "by month" or "excluding holidays" is a filter, not a calculation.
  • Conformed dimension. A dimension shared by several fact tables: dim_city and dim_date are used by both fact_ride and fact_spend. That is what lets you put rides and ad spend per city per week in one chart (drill across). Two copies of "city" with different spellings make it impossible.
  • Surrogate key. The warehouse gives every dimension row its own number, instead of reusing the source's id. Telematics and billing can then disagree about ids without breaking the join.
  • Slowly changing dimension, type 2. When Anna Kowalski changes her email or her plan, the warehouse does not overwrite her row (that is type 1, and history is lost). It closes the old row (valid_to) and opens a new one (valid_from), so last month's rides stay with last month's plan.
  • Three kinds of fact table. Transaction: one row per event (a ride). Periodic snapshot: one row per thing per period (an account per month: its status on the 1st, cancelled or not). Accumulating snapshot: one row per thing that moves through stages, updated as it goes (a ticket: opened, first reply, resolved).

✅ Step 5 · 18 min · note part 2

The source-to-target map

A source-to-target map says, for every column of the warehouse, where it comes from and what is done to it on the way. It is what next week's load is built from and what an auditor reads when a number moves. The last three columns are the governance line: who answers for the column, how sensitive it is, and the check that stops a bad row.

  1. Read ÒRBITA's example map below: six columns of fact_ride and fact_spend. Notice the rules: they are Session 1's traps, written down.
  2. Press Fill the targets from our star: the first column fills with your star's keys and measures. Add or rename rows as you need.
  3. Fill the six other cells of every row. One member types (cells are shared with the team); another checks each rule against the Session 1 traps: timezones, failed unlocks, the changed email, duplicated exports, currencies, paused accounts.
  4. The map check marks each row. At least six complete rows, then press Save the map. This is part 2 of your note.
Sensitivity, in one lineUnder the GDPR, data that identifies a person is personal, and a pseudonymous id that can be linked back to them (subscriber_id, a hashed email) is still personal data (Recital 26). ÒRBITA operates in the EU, so every such column needs an owner and a stated purpose. Internal: business data that is not about a person, such as ad spend. Public: could be published as it stands, such as the list of cities.

Five terms from today

  • OLTP and OLAP: many small writes that run the business, or few large reads that analyse it. Two jobs, two databases.
  • Schema on write, schema on read: the warehouse checks a row when it is loaded; the lake leaves it to whoever reads.
  • Grain: what one row means, in a sentence starting "one row per". Declared first; it decides which questions the table can answer.
  • Star schema: one fact table per business process, holding measures at the grain, with keys to the dimensions around it.
  • Source-to-target map: for every column, the source, the rule, the owner, the sensitivity and the check.
