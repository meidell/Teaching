What the course is actually about

The lifecycle, in the order it actually happens

Business intelligence is usually taught as a tour of tools. That produces graduates who can build a dashboard and cannot say what it is for. So this course runs the full lifecycle of data analytics in sequence, and each stage inherits the mess the previous one left.

It starts at the business end, because that is where BI succeeds or fails: which strategic priority, which decision, which number, owned by whom. Only then do we ask where that number would come from — which turns out to be five systems that disagree — and build somewhere for it to live. Then we analyse it, and the hard question is not "what is the average?" but "is this difference real, or am I about to recommend a change on noise?"

Then, and only then, visualisation. A chart is an argument, and most BI charts are bad arguments made confidently. Then machine learning, where the interesting skill is not fitting the model but knowing when not to ship it. And last, the module everyone skips: why the previous dashboard was opened four times and abandoned.

The claim this course is built onThe technical part of BI is the easy part. The value is created at the two ends — choosing the question, and getting somebody to act on the answer — and that is where the teaching hours go here, proportionally, even though the middle is what looks like the subject.

How it is taught, and why

The syllabus names interactive lectures, collaborative simulations and project-based learning. Those are real formats and they only work if the room is genuinely doing the work — so the mechanics are fixed and they repeat every week:

  • Twenty minutes of me, at most. Enough to give you the vocabulary and the trap; the rest of the two hours you are building or defending something.
  • Predict, then reveal. You write a number or a guess before the data appears. The gap is the lesson, and it is what makes you want the answer.
  • Teams of three or four, roles rotating. Analyst drives the tool, Skeptic must argue against their own team's conclusion before it leaves the table, Scribe produces the artifact and the assumptions log.
  • Red team. One team builds, another is scored on breaking it, then you swap. Attacking is rewarded, so disagreeing is a job rather than a social risk.
  • Gallery walk, not presentations. Work goes on the wall, everyone votes, and we only discuss what got a red dot. Five sequential presentations means four bored teams.
  • Break my claim. Every session ends with one falsifiable statement from me. Bring a genuine counterexample the following week and it counts.

Cold-calling is normal here and "pass" is always an acceptable answer — I will come back to you later. The point is that everyone is thinking, not that anyone is caught out.

The spine of the term

Sealed in October. Opened in January.

In Session 1 your team writes one sentence: the decision this project will change, who makes it, and how often. It is sealed and you do not get to revise it. Every layer you build afterwards — the KPI, the schema, the pipeline, the test, the dashboard, the model — is built to serve that one sentence.

In presentation week the panel opens it, and the first question is not about your architecture. It is "would the person who makes this decision do anything differently on Monday?" That is the question a real BI steering committee asks, and it is the one most student dashboards cannot survive.

Being wrong in October is fine and expected — plenty of teams discover in November that the data cannot answer the question they sealed. Saying so, with evidence, is a stronger result than quietly changing the question. Writing the sentence and then finding out it was the wrong sentence is exactly what the first three months of a real BI programme feels like.

One company, five sources, all term

ÒRBITA is a Barcelona-based e-bike and scooter subscription operator: about 40,000 subscribers across six European cities, growing fast, and losing them faster than it admits. Its five systems were bought at different times by different people and have never been reconciled. Every session works on this stack, and the incompatibilities below are the reason most of the sessions are hard.

Source 1
Billing & subscriptions
Plans, payments, pauses, cancellations. One row per invoice.
Trap: a pause is not a churn, and nobody wrote down which is which.
Source 2
Fleet telematics
Every ride: start, end, distance, battery, city. Millions of rows.
Trap: UTC timestamps, six local timezones, and rides that end tomorrow.
Source 3
Support desk
Tickets, categories, resolution time, free-text complaints.
Trap: customers are keyed by email, and people change email.
Source 4
Marketing spend
Channel × city × week, exported by hand from four ad platforms.
Trap: three currencies, and two of the exports overlap.
Source 5
The finance spreadsheet
The numbers the board actually sees. Maintained by one person.
Trap: it disagrees with source 1, and it is the one they trust.
Why a fictional company and not a real clientBecause I can plant the faults. A real dataset is either clean enough to be boring or confidential enough that you cannot publish your findings — and the specific disagreements above are the ones that eat BI projects in practice. Every number in ÒRBITA's data is generated, and generated to be wrong in instructive ways. The techniques, the tools and the failure modes are entirely real.

Attendance

School policy: a maximum of two absences. More than two results in a fail or a dropped course, and justified absences go to the Academic department with their documentation (for example a medical certificate), not to me.

Worth being blunt about the arithmetic: there are eleven sessions, so two absences is already nearly a fifth of the course. And because each session builds on the layer the last one produced, a missed session cannot be caught up by borrowing slides — the schema or the pipeline your team built that day is the thing the following week runs on.

If you must miss oneTell me beforehand and take the Scribe role the week after. That is the role that has to reconstruct what happened, which is the fastest way back in.

Generative AI · an instrument here, and still declared

This course uses AI systems as objects of study and as working tools — Module 5 is about building them, and you will use assistants to write SQL and Python throughout. That is the coursework, not a shortcut around it.

That makes the line sharper rather than blurrier. Using a model to help you write a query is the assignment. Using one to write your interpretation of the result is not. Any AI use in work you hand in must be disclosed in full, including your prompts, and whether it was for form or for substance. Unattributed use is plagiarism under the school's standard protocols and is treated as such.

The recommendation for this course specificallyNever ask a model for the finding. Ask it for a method you could run yourself, then run it and check whether the model was right. Half of Session 10 is evidence that these systems are confidently wrong in public; your own analysis is the last place to forget that.
