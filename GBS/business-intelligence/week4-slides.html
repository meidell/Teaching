The mandate · what the client asks for today

"Thank you for the KPI memo. IT says none of those KPIs can be computed from our systems as they are, and asks what to build. Before I sign a warehouse budget I want the design on one page: which tables, at what level of detail, filled from which of our five systems, and who answers for each column."

What your team hands in today is a one-page design note with three parts:

  2. The source-to-target map: for every column, which system and which column it comes from, what is done to it, who is responsible for it, how sensitive it is, and how a bad value is caught.

A KPI is a key performance indicator: the number a manager is judged on (churn, rides per subscriber). Last week each team wrote a "KPI contract": one KPI, defined in eight fields.
Step 1 · why a second database is needed

ÒRBITA cannot calculate your KPIs from its existing systems, so it has to build a second database: a data warehouse

An operational database (OLTP)

OLTP means online transaction processing. ÒRBITA's billing system is one. It does a small job thousands of times a day: it charges a card, pauses a subscription, changes a plan. Each request reads or writes a few rows. It stores every fact once, so an update changes one place.

An analytic database (OLAP)

OLAP means online analytical processing. "Monthly churn by city and plan for twelve months" reads every subscription for a year, joins rides from the telematics system and tickets from the help desk, and adds them up. Few requests, each reading millions of rows.

Three things go wrong if the billing system answers a KPI question. The query slows down the card payments on the same machine. The billing system cannot see rides or tickets, because they are stored in other systems with other customer numbers. And its columns use billing's definitions, not the definitions your team wrote last week. So the company builds a second database for analysis, filled from the first: a data warehouse.

Telematics: the system that records each ride from the vehicle (start time, kilometres, city).
Step 1 · three places to keep data for analysis

A warehouse, a lake and a lakehouse differ in when the structure of the data is checked, and in who reads it

Step 2 · the most important word of today

The grain of a table is what one row of it means, written in one sentence that starts "One row per…"

You decide the grain before any column, because it decides which questions the table can ever answer.

One row per ride

About 560,000 rows a month. It can say at what hour people ride. It cannot say who cancelled, because a customer who never rides has no row.

One row per account per month

47,700 rows a month, one for every account on billing's books. It can say who cancelled. It cannot say at what hour anyone rode.

One row per city per day

180 rows a month: six cities, thirty days. Everything is already added up, so the customer, the hour and the first fourteen days are gone, and no query can bring them back.

A table cannot answer a question about something smaller than its row.
ÒRBITA's sizes, from the page: 47,700 accounts on billing's books, about 560,000 rides a month (about 14 per paying subscriber), about 3,800 tickets, six cities, three ad channels.

Step 2 · the answer

ÒRBITA needs four tables, one for each business process, each at the most detailed level the process produces

A business process is one activity of the company that produces data: a ride, a monthly subscription, a support ticket, an ad campaign. The rule comes from Kimball and Ross, The Data Warehouse Toolkit (3rd ed., 2013): one central table per business process, at the most detailed grain. A table with one row per account per day would also answer churn, but with 1,431,000 rows a month, thirty times more.

The city-per-day summary answers none of the six. That is the lesson: once data is added up, the detail is gone.
Step 3 · facts, dimensions, the star

A star schema puts one table of numbers in the middle and the descriptive tables around it

A fact (also called a measure)

A number the process produces at the table's grain, usually one you add up: kilometres ridden, minutes, euros of revenue, hours to resolve a ticket. Facts are stored in the fact table, in the middle.

A dimension

The context you filter and group by: when, where, who, which plan, which channel. Each dimension has its own table around the fact table (dim_date, dim_city, dim_subscriber). The fact table holds a key that points to each one.

fact_ride · one row per ride · measures: km, duration in minutes, failed unlock (0 or 1) · dimensions: date, time, city, subscriber, vehicle

Drawn, the fact table sits in the middle and the dimension tables around it, like the points of a star. That drawing is a star schema, and it is what your team draws in Step 4.

A failed unlock is when a customer unlocks a scooter and does not ride; Session 1's trap (rides under 0.2 km or 2 minutes).
Step 3 · five more terms you will need in Step 4

Five terms that come with a star schema, each with ÒRBITA's example

  • Date dimension: a table with one row per calendar day, which says the weekday, month, quarter and public holidays. Every fact table points to it, so "by month" or "without holidays" becomes a filter, not a calculation.
  • Conformed dimension: a dimension that several fact tables share. dim_city is used by fact_ride and by fact_spend, so rides and ad spend per city can go in one chart. Two different spellings of "city" would make that impossible.
  • Surrogate key: the warehouse gives every dimension row its own number instead of the source system's customer number. Telematics and billing can then use different numbers for the same customer without breaking the join.
  • Type 2 change: when Anna Kowalski changes her email or plan, the warehouse does not overwrite her row. It closes the old row with an end date and opens a new row with a start date, so last month's rides stay with last month's plan.
  • Three kinds of fact table: one row per event (a ride), one row per thing per period (an account per month), or one row per thing that moves through stages and is updated (a ticket: opened, answered, resolved).
Anna Kowalski is the customer from Session 1 whose email changed.

Sensitivity. Under the GDPR, the EU data-protection law, data that identifies a person is personal. A customer number that can be linked back to a person is still personal (Recital 26), so it needs an owner and a stated purpose. Internal is business data not about a person, such as ad spend. Public could be published as it is, such as the list of cities.

The rules are Session 1's traps written down: time zones, failed unlocks, the changed email, duplicated exports, currencies, paused accounts.
