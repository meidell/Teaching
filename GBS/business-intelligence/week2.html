The autopsy

Below is the operations dashboard a company like ÒRBITA commissioned in spring 2026. It cost four months of a contractor's time. The access log shows it was opened four times by its intended user, the chief operating officer, and never again. Nothing on it is wrong.

Before any discussion, decide from the artifact alone why it was abandoned, and seal the answer. There is no single right reason; the point is to commit to one before the explanation arrives.

Once sealed it cannot be edited. Sealed predictions are timestamped and count as evidence for the participation mark.

What the artifact says

  • There is no question on it. Twelve numbers, and not one of them is framed as an answer to anything the COO decides. A dashboard that does not name a decision is a report, and reports are read once.
  • Two churn figures disagree, 4.1 percent and 2.6 percent, both labelled "churn". The first time a user sees two numbers for the same thing they stop trusting all twelve.
  • "Data as of 14 Jul 2026." The user opened it in September. A number that is older than the decision it is meant to inform is noise.
  • No owner, no definition, no target. Nobody is named as responsible for any tile, nothing says how a tile is computed, and nothing says what good looks like. There is no way to act on a number without those three.

Everything this course does afterwards exists to prevent one of these four. The session's second half writes down the question; next week defines the numbers; Sessions 3 and 4 build the thing that keeps them fresh; Session 11 is about the owner.

Open the five systems

ÒRBITA is a Barcelona-based e-bike and scooter subscription operator: about 40,000 paying subscribers across six European cities, growing fast, losing them faster than it admits. Its five systems were bought at different times by different people and have never been reconciled. The course page lists the trap in each. Here are four real rows from each system; click the cell that proves the trap.

What these faults have in commonNone of them is a bug. Each system is correct for the job it was built to do: billing bills, telematics logs rides in universal time, the help desk identifies people by the address they wrote from. The faults appear only when the systems are joined to answer a question none of them was built for. That join is what a warehouse is, and it is Sessions 3 and 4.

Teams, roles, and the sealed sentence

Teams of three or four, for the term. Roles rotate every session. Analyst drives the tool and keeps the team on the question. Skeptic must argue against the team's own conclusion before it leaves the table, out loud, every session. Scribe produces the artifact and the assumptions log; what the Scribe does not write down did not happen.

The sentence

As a team, write the one decision this term's work will change: who makes it, what they decide, and how often. Not a goal, not an insight, not a dashboard. A decision is something a named person would decide differently because of the number. The detector below counts the ways a sentence fails that test; it is a rule-based check, and its reasons matter more than its score.

One sentence. Example of the form: "Every Monday the Barcelona city manager decides how many bikes to move from the Eixample depot to the beach depot."
Sealed by the Scribe, once per team. It is opened in presentation week; the first question then is whether it changed.
Being wrong is expectedPlenty of teams discover in November that the data cannot answer the sentence they sealed. Saying so, with evidence, is a stronger result than quietly changing the sentence. That is what the first three months of a real BI programme feel like.
✅ 17:15 · 20 min · Step 4

What BI is, and where it fails

Business intelligence is the set of methods and systems that turn data an organisation already generates into decisions somebody in it has to make. Not the tools: the lifecycle. Here it is in the order it happens, which is also the order of this course.

The mechanism: why the billing database cannot answer the question

ÒRBITA's billing system runs on a transactional database. It is built to do one thing fast: find one subscriber's row, change its status, write an invoice. That is OLTP, online transaction processing: rows stored whole, one at a time, locked while they are written, indexed by subscriber id. Ask it "monthly churn by city for the last eighteen months" and it has to read every column of every row of every invoice ever issued, while the payment service is trying to write to the same table. The query runs for minutes and the billing team asks who is slowing down checkout.

A warehouse stores the same data the other way round: by column. "Churn by city by month" touches three columns, status, city and month, and never reads the other forty. Three columns of three million rows is a few hundred megabytes, read in sequence, in seconds. That is OLAP, online analytical processing, and it is the single technical reason a warehouse exists: the same facts, physically rearranged so that aggregation is cheap and single-row lookup is not. The warehouse is filled from the source systems by a pipeline, nightly or hourly, which is why its numbers are always slightly old and always consistent with each other, two properties the live billing system does not have.

source systems → extract, transform, load → warehouse (columnar, one grain per table) → semantic layer (the definitions) → dashboard query → a number on a tile

The semantic layer is where "churn" is written down once, as a formula over named columns, so that every dashboard computes the same 4.1 percent or the same 2.6 percent, and never both. The dead dashboard in Step 1 had no semantic layer; two analysts each wrote their own definition in their own query. Session 2 writes ÒRBITA's definitions; Session 3 designs the tables they will be computed from.

The four places BI projects fail

  • No decision. The project starts from the data available instead of from a choice someone makes. It produces a report. Sessions 1 and 11.
  • No definition. Two systems, two numbers, and the organisation argues about the number instead of acting on it. Session 2.
  • No trust. The pipeline breaks silently, the dashboard shows last month, and the first person who notices stops looking. Sessions 3 and 4.
  • No adoption. The number is right, fresh and defined, and nobody decides anything differently because of it. Session 11, and the reason the sealed sentence exists.

Red team

Each team attacks another team's sealed sentence. Load the wall, pick a team other than your own, and score the sentence on four questions, 0 to 2 each. A sentence that scores 8 is a decision; a sentence that scores 4 is a goal wearing a decision's clothes. The attacked team gets the score and the weakest question, not a rewrite; they revise nothing, because the sentence is sealed, but they will know in Session 2 which KPI has to carry the weight.

Then, out loud · 6 minutes

Each Skeptic reads their team's score and weakest question in one sentence. The room listens for one thing: which sentence could the data in Step 2 not answer at all? That team has the most interesting term ahead.

The close

Five terms from today

  • Business intelligence: methods and systems that turn data an organisation already generates into decisions somebody in it makes. The lifecycle, not the tools.
  • Decision brief: who decides, what, how often, what they would decide differently, and the one number. Sealed in October.
  • OLTP and OLAP: row storage for one record fast; column storage for one aggregate fast. The reason a warehouse exists.
  • Semantic layer: the place a metric is defined once, as a formula over named columns, so two dashboards cannot disagree.
  • Grain: what one row of a table stands for, one invoice, one ride, one ticket. Next week's hardest word, met today.
