Extract, transform, load

ETL stands for extract, transform, load, the three things that happen to data between the systems that run a business and the warehouse that measures it.

  • Extract: copy the rows out of each source system, without changing them. ÒRBITA's IT has done this today: five files, one from billing (with its customer table), telematics, the support desk, marketing and the finance spreadsheet. The copies first go into a staging area, a set of tables that hold the data exactly as it arrived, so that a load can be repeated and checked.
  • Transform: change the rows so that they fit the warehouse: convert the times to one clock, the money to one currency, the names to one spelling, and remove what should not be counted. Every change follows a written rule, and the rule is the source-to-target map's third column from last week.
  • Load: write the changed rows into the warehouse tables (fact_ride, fact_subscription, fact_ticket, fact_spend and the dimension tables). A good load can be run twice without creating duplicates: data engineers call this idempotent.

Many companies today load first and transform inside the warehouse, with SQL. This order is called ELT. The rules are the same; only the place where they run changes.

The checks that refuse a wrong row

A check (data engineers say a data test) is a rule that every loaded row must pass. A row that fails is refused, or flagged, and written in the log. The four most common checks are the four that the open-source tool dbt ships as its "generic data tests":

  • Not null: the column is never empty. Example: every ride has a date.
  • Unique: no value appears twice. Example: one row per city, channel and week in fact_spend.
  • Accepted values: the value is one of a fixed list. Example: the city is one of ÒRBITA's six cities: Barcelona, Madrid, Lisbon, Milan, Zürich, London.
  • Relationships: a key points to a row that exists. Example: every ride's subscriber exists in billing.

A fifth kind checks a range: a counted ride is longer than 0.2 km and 2 minutes. These are the checks from the last column of your map.

What no check catchesA check can only refuse a value that looks wrong. A ride dated 4 October in UTC, the world clock, looks perfectly right, even when it happened at 00:40 on 5 October in Barcelona. Such errors pass every check and change the numbers anyway. Only a rule written before the load prevents them, which is why Step 3 has rules that no check would have asked for.

✅ Step 4 · each member · part 3 of the report

Build the bridge from billing's 47,700 subscribers to finance's 40,100

Why this step exists. Since Session 1 the board has seen two numbers of subscribers for September: finance says 40,100, billing has 47,700 on its books. Both are right about their own definition. A bridge (accountants also say a reconciliation) is a table that starts from one number and subtracts, line by line, each group of accounts that the other number leaves out, until it arrives at the other number. When the bridge closes, nobody needs to ask which system is "wrong". Session 1 found the shares of a random sample of 1,000 accounts: 840 active (paying), 95 paused, 40 in trial and 25 unpaid.

  1. Fill each line of the bridge with a number of accounts. Use the shares of the sample: for example, trial accounts are 40 of 1,000, so 4.0 % of 47,700.
  2. The page marks each line ✓ when the number is right (rounded to whole accounts).

Six terms from today

  • ETL and ELT. Extract, transform, load: the data is copied out of each system, changed by written rules, and written into the warehouse. In ELT the changes run inside the warehouse, after the load.
  • Staging area. Tables that hold the extracts exactly as they arrived, so a load can be repeated and checked.
  • Data test (check). A rule every loaded row must pass: not null, unique, accepted values, relationships, or a range.
  • Quarantine. A table for refused rows, kept out of the warehouse and listed in the log, so nothing disappears without a trace.
  • Transformation log. The written record of each rule: the problem, the rule, the rows it changed, its effect on the numbers, and why.
  • Bridge (reconciliation). A table that goes from one system's number to another's, one group of accounts at a time.
In Session 5 the class splits ÒRBITA's churn by city for the whole company, not the sample. How far apart will the city with the highest churn and the city with the lowest churn be, in percentage points?
After today you can explain what extract, transform and load each do; what the four common data tests check; why some errors pass every check; why a refused row goes to quarantine and into the log; how a bridge reconciles two systems' numbers; and why the transformation log, not the loaded table, is what makes a KPI trustworthy a year later.
