✅ Step 2 · 20 min

How a new page gets quoted

A page the engines have never seen goes through four things before it can appear in an answer. It has to be found. It has to match a question someone asks. One of its paragraphs has to be picked over everyone else's. And for the result to mean anything, everything except the writing has to be held still. Each part below has one question, something to click, and the line to write down.

AWill the robots find it?
A robot finds a page in one of three ways: it follows a link from a page it already knows, it reads a sitemap (a list of addresses the site publishes for robots), or the site owner tells the engine directly. Then the page waits for the robot's next visit. Google's own documentation: "Crawling can take anywhere from a few days to a few weeks." Each engine has its own robot and its own list, so being found by one says nothing about the others.
Do this: tick what will be done for your page and read which engines receive a signal. The table credits a signal only where the operator documents it (sources at the foot of the page); a robot that follows links finds the lab through its index page.

BWhich question will it answer?
An engine does not search for your question once. It splits it into several smaller searches — Google calls this query fan-out — and builds the answer from whatever those searches return. The main question ("which headphones under 200 francs?") is held by sites with tens of thousands of links; a new page with none will not beat rtings there. A narrower sub-question that nobody answers exactly is a different contest: the engine has to pick something for it, and a page that answers it precisely, with numbers, can be the best match available.
Do this: click the sub-question your team could answer best and most truthfully. It goes into Step 3. The list is an illustration of what a fan-out can produce, not a capture of a real one; your own sub-question is welcome.

CWill its paragraph be picked?
Last week's chunker showed that a piece is quoted on its own, without its neighbours. The one study that tested rewriting for AI engines — Aggarwal and colleagues, the GEO paper (2024) — rewrote the same pages several ways and measured how much of each page the engine's answer used. Adding statistics, adding quotations and citing sources raised visibility the most, up to about 40 %; stuffing in keywords did little. The rewrite below makes those moves one at a time, on an example that is not about headphones.
Do this: the question is "How much does it cost to replace an e-bike battery in Geneva?". Switch the five fixes on one by one and watch the three tests from last week, plus a fourth: does the piece say where its number comes from.

Write down: which single fix changed the most tests.
DWhat makes this an experiment, and not a hope?
A page that gets quoted in January proves nothing on its own: the engine might have changed, the question might have become popular, another site might have linked to it. Three things turn it into evidence. A baseline — who is quoted for the question today, recorded before the page exists (Step 3). One change — the writing; the links stay at zero, the same for every team on the lab. A prediction written before the result — sealed with a fingerprint so nobody can quietly edit it later (Step 7).

The rule for the pagesEvery number on a page has a source line, and every source is real and was opened today. A page that invents a measurement in order to be quoted is a page that teaches an AI engine something false, and each page carries a line saying it was written by students as part of a course experiment. ✅ Step 7 · 12 min

Seal the prediction

Each person seals their own. In Week 10 and in Week 11 the class asks the same three phrasings in the same three engines — nine answers — and counts how many quote the page. Write what you expect now, while you cannot know.

One sentence a classmate could check. "If it is quoted by Google but not Perplexity, then …"
What the fingerprint isThe five answers above, joined into one line, are run through SHA-256, a function that turns any text into 64 characters. Change one character of the text and the fingerprint changes completely; nobody can work backwards from the fingerprint to the text. The fingerprint goes on the class wall tonight with the date. In Week 11 the page recomputes it from your saved answers: if they were edited, the two no longer match. The wall itself is a class notice board, not a vault — the fingerprint shows whether the text changed, not who changed it.

Words to keep

Sitemap · a file listing a site's addresses for robots to read.
IndexNow · a way for a site to tell Bing and a few other engines "this address changed" at once. Google does not take part.
URL Inspection · Request indexing · the site owner's way to ask Google to crawl one address, in Search Console. It asks; it does not guarantee.
Query fan-out · the engine splitting one question into several searches and answering from all of them.
Sub-question · one of those searches. Narrower, less contested.
GEO · generative engine optimization: writing so that AI answers use the page. One study, up to about 40 % more visibility from statistics, quotations and cited sources.
Baseline · the measurement taken before the change. Without it, "after" has nothing to be compared with.
Held constant · what the experiment deliberately does not change. Here: links.
Fingerprint (hash) · a short code computed from a text; any edit to the text changes it.
