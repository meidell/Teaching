Last week you checked six things on the quoted websites. Some of them you can control just by how you write your page; others you cannot control at all:

✅ You control these by how you write
  • the text is plain text on the page, so the AI tools' robots can read it
  • each paragraph says what it is about (names the product)
  • each paragraph gives a number or a comparison
  • the page uses the same words people type in their question
  • the page carries hidden labels that tell robots what it is (the course's website adds these for you)
  • the date on the page is visible and correct
❌ You do not control these
  • how many other websites link to your page
  • what the rest of the internet says about the topic
  • what the AI tool already learned before your page existed
  • whether a website lets the AI robots in (on the course's website it does)

Your team changes only the ✅ things. So if your page is quoted by January, the way it was written is the reason. If it is not quoted, the ❌ things are probably why — and that is a useful result too.

✅ Step 2 · 20 min

How a new page gets quoted

The instructor goes through this step with the whole class (20 minutes). For a brand-new page to appear in an AI answer, four things must be true: A the AI tool's robot must find the page; B the page must answer a question people actually ask; C one of its paragraphs must be chosen over other websites' paragraphs; and D to learn anything from it, the team must change only the writing and nothing else. Each part has a short explanation, something to click, and one line to note in your notebook. Nothing in this step is graded.

AWill the robots find it?
A robot finds a page in one of three ways: it follows a link from a page it already knows, it reads a sitemap (a list of addresses the site publishes for robots), or the site owner tells the engine directly. Then the page waits for the robot's next visit. Google's own documentation: "Crawling can take anywhere from a few days to a few weeks." Each engine has its own robot and its own list, so being found by one says nothing about the others.

Untick and tick them, and the table shows which AI tool hears about your page through which route. BWhich question will it answer?
An engine does not search for your question once. It splits it into several smaller searches — Google calls this query fan-out — and builds the answer from whatever those searches return. The main question ("which headphones under 200 francs?") is held by sites with tens of thousands of links; a new page with none will not beat rtings there. A narrower sub-question that nobody answers exactly is a different contest: the engine has to pick something for it, and a page that answers it precisely, with numbers, can be the best match available.

CWill its paragraph be picked?
Last week's chunker showed that a piece is quoted on its own, without its neighbours. The one study that tested rewriting for AI engines — Aggarwal and colleagues, the GEO paper (2024) — rewrote the same pages several ways and measured how much of each page the engine's answer used. Adding statistics, adding quotations and citing sources raised visibility the most, up to about 40 %; stuffing in keywords did little. DHow will we know the writing made the difference?
If your page is quoted in January, that alone does not prove much: the AI tool may have changed, the question may have become popular, or another website may have linked to your page. Three things make the result trustworthy. 1. A "before" record — who the AI tools quote for your question today, written down before your page exists (Step 3). 2. Only one change — the writing. Nobody adds links to the pages; every team on the course's website is treated the same. 3. A guess written down before the result — locked in Step 7 so it cannot be edited later.

The rule for every pageEvery number on your page needs a source, and you must have opened that source yourself today. Never make up a number to get quoted: an AI tool that quotes it would then tell other people something false. Every page will also say, at the bottom, that students wrote it for a course experiment. Your own experience counts as a source only if it really happened: "tried by a team member on the Geneva–Lausanne train, 28 Oct" is fine; "we measured 32 dB" is not, unless you really used a sound meter.

✅ Step 7 · 12 min

Make your guess, and lock it

Everyone does this alone, on their own device. In Week 10 and again in Week 11 the class will ask your three questions in the same three AI tools — nine answers each time — and count how many of the nine quote your page. Guess the numbers now, before anyone can know. Then press Seal it: your guess is locked, so nobody (you included) can change it later without it showing.

One sentence that a classmate could check in January, e.g. "If Perplexity quotes it before Google does, I was wrong about which tool is first."
How the lock worksYour five answers are turned into a 64-character code (a "fingerprint", made with a method called SHA-256). Change even one letter of the answers and the code comes out completely different, and nobody can turn the code back into your answers. The code is posted on the class board tonight with the date. In Week 11 the page makes the code again from your saved answers: if anything was edited, the two codes no longer match. The board shows whether the guess changed, not who changed it.

Words to keep

Sitemap · a file listing a site's addresses for robots to read.
IndexNow · a way for a site to tell Bing and a few other engines "this address changed" at once. Google does not take part.
URL Inspection · Request indexing · the site owner's way to ask Google to crawl one address, in Search Console. It asks; it does not guarantee.
Query fan-out · the engine splitting one question into several searches and answering from all of them.
Sub-question · one of those searches. Narrower, less contested.
GEO · generative engine optimization: writing so that AI answers use the page. One study, up to about 40 % more visibility from statistics, quotations and cited sources.
Baseline · the measurement taken before the change. Without it, "after" has nothing to be compared with.
Held constant · what the experiment deliberately does not change. Here: links.
Fingerprint (hash) · a short code computed from a text; any edit to the text changes it.
