What the course is actually about

One argument, in two halves

Brands can still buy attention. A paid ad will put you in front of whoever you like tomorrow morning. What nobody sells is a place in the systems that now do most of the deciding — the organic feed, the Reddit thread that ranks for your category, the review someone actually watches, the paragraph an AI returns when a customer asks it what to buy. Each of those is allocated by a third party running its own objective, and you can only reach them through somebody: an algorithm optimising for retention, a creator protecting their own credibility, a community that will notice if it is being played.

The first half of the term is that somebody: platform economics, why reach collapses when a platform rebalances its two sides, how creators are chosen and on what terms, and why what a brand buys from a creator is a lease — non-transferable, degrading with use, and it walks out of the door when the deal ends.

The second half is what the machines make of it. Search stopped being one box: people ask TikTok, Reddit, YouTube, Amazon and increasingly an answer engine. The AI ones do not send you traffic — they answer, and at best cite you. So the target moves from clicks to inclusion, which means being extractable and being independently vouched for.

Why these are one course and not twoWhat an answer engine summarises about a product is largely what creators and communities already said about it. Community work is the raw material of machine citation. That claim is the spine of the term — and in Week 03 you will try to falsify it on a live page.

How it is taught, and why

The syllabus for this course names live-client sprints, reverse-engineering labs and case defence. Those are real formats, and they only work if the room is genuinely doing the work — so the mechanics are fixed and they repeat every week:

  • Predict, then reveal. You write a guess before any data appears. The gap is the lesson, and it is what makes you want the answer.
  • Teams of three, roles rotating. Operator drives the tool, Skeptic must argue against their own team's conclusion before it leaves the table, Scribe produces the artifact. Three is small enough that nobody can hide.
  • Red team. One team builds, another is scored on breaking it, then you swap. Attacking is rewarded, so disagreeing is a job rather than a social risk.
  • Gallery walk, not presentations. Work goes on the wall, everyone votes with stickers, and we only discuss what got a red dot. Five sequential presentations means four bored teams.
  • Silent start. Two minutes of writing alone before discussion opens, so fifteen opinions exist before the first one is spoken.
  • Break my claim. Every session ends with one falsifiable statement from me. Bring a genuine counterexample the following week and it counts.

Cold-calling is normal here and "pass" is always an acceptable answer — I will come back to you later. The point is that everyone is thinking, not that anyone is caught out.

The standing experiment

Published in October. Read in January.

In Week 03 each team takes a page that is currently invisible to the machines — for a real business, with its permission — and rewrites it to be extractable and citable. It goes live, and each team seals a written prediction of what will happen to it.

Then we wait, because that is what the subject requires: crawling, indexing and retrieval work on a scale of weeks, not days. From Week 04 onward the first five minutes of every session re-measures the pages, and the class watches a scoreboard it cannot argue with. The winter break is not lost time — it is the experiment running.

Between publication and the verdict there are 77 days. Very little in a bachelor course gets to be a real longitudinal experiment; this calendar happens to allow one, so the term is built around it.

Four equal components, set by the school. What is not set by the school is what evidences them — and in a workshop course that has to be spelled out, or "participation" becomes a quarter of your grade decided on a feeling.

Attendance

School policy: a maximum of two absences. More than two results in a fail or a dropped course, and justified absences go to the Academic department with their documentation, not to me.

Worth being blunt about the arithmetic: there are eleven sessions, so two absences is already a fifth of the course. And because the material is generated in the room rather than delivered from a deck, a missed session cannot be caught up by borrowing notes — the dataset your team built that day is the thing the following week argues about.

If you must miss oneTell me beforehand and take the Scribe role the week after. That is the role that has to reconstruct what happened, which is the fastest way back in.

Generative AI · an instrument here, and still declared

This course uses AI systems as objects of study and as working tools — you will spend whole sessions querying answer engines, and that is the coursework, not a shortcut around it.

That makes the line sharper rather than blurrier. Using an answer engine as an instrument is the assignment. Using one to write your analysis of it is not. Any AI use in work you hand in must be disclosed in full, including your prompts, and whether it was for form or for substance. Unattributed use is plagiarism under the school's standard protocols and is treated as such.

The recommendation for this course specificallyNever ask a model for the finding. Ask it for a method you could run yourself, then run it and see whether the model was right. Half the term is evidence that these systems are confidently wrong in public; your own analysis is the last place to forget that.
