How to handle a content creator crisis: what to publish, what the law requires, and when to end the contract

Bring: one laptop per team is enough for Steps 3 and 4; for the press round in Step 5 every student needs a laptop or a phone. Teams as in Sessions 3, 4 and 6. Online students work in their team's online room (the "Team room" button in the blue bar).

Why this session exists. A brand that pays a content creator lends that person its name. When the content creator says something unacceptable in public, people tag the brand, journalists ask the brand, and the brand's own advertising may still be showing that person. What the brand does in the first hours decides whether the story is about one person's words or about the brand. Today you learn what to check first, what a public statement must contain and must not, what the law means for the brand, and what the contract you negotiated in Session 5 allows.

Today in one sentence: your team is Sonnaz Audio, the brand of Sessions 4 to 6; @leman.sound, the content creator you signed in Session 5, has mocked people with hearing loss in a live stream; four facts arrive one after another, your team records each decision and the reason in a decision log, publishes a statement, and then the rest of the class plays the press and questions it.

You leave today with: the statement your team published, the decision log (each fact that arrived, what you decided, and why), and one rule for handling a crisis that your team writes into the January playbook.

✅ Step 2 · on the big screen, then on your laptop

What a brand does in the first hours after its content creator says something unacceptable

A crisis, in this course, is an event that many people see at once and that makes them doubt whether a brand can be trusted. A crisis caused by a content creator has a special shape: the words are not the brand's, but the brand chose the person, paid them, and often still shows them in its own advertising. Read the four parts below with one question in mind: what is in the brand's control in the first hours?

AWhy the clip spreads faster than the brand can meet

A short clip of the worst sentence travels further than the full video. Three mechanisms you know from Sessions 1 and 4 explain why.

  • The ranking systems reward reactions. Instagram's and TikTok's ranking systems show a post to more people when the first viewers comment, share and watch to the end (Session 4, Step 2). An angry clip produces exactly those reactions. A study of 563,312 posts on Twitter about political topics found that each moral-emotional word in a post (words such as "shameful" or "disgusting") raised the number of reposts by about 20 % (Brady and colleagues, PNAS, 2017).
  • Tagging puts the brand inside the story. When viewers write "@sonnaz.audio, is this who you pay?", the clip appears in the brand's own notifications and in the "tagged" tab of its profile. Journalists follow these tags.
  • The brand's own ad keeps showing the person. A partnership ad is a paid advertisement that the brand runs from the content creator's post, under both names. It keeps running until someone in the brand's advertising account presses Pause, so the brand's own money keeps showing the content creator while people are angry about her.

The numbers are illustrative. Before you move anything, write down: if the brand reacts after 8 hours instead of 2, how many more people have seen the clip without any word from the brand: twice as many, or more?

BFacts before words: the four things to check in the first hour

The first hour is for checking facts, not for writing. A statement built on a wrong fact has to be corrected later, and a correction makes a second story. Check four things, in this order:

  1. What exactly was said, and where. Find the original post or the full recording, not only the clip. Save a copy with the date and time, because the content creator may delete it.
  2. Is the clip complete and authentic? A clip can be cut so that a sentence means the opposite, or be made with a voice generator. Compare it with the full recording, second by second.
  3. How is the brand connected, in public? Is the content creator paid by the brand right now? Is the brand's logo or product visible in the clip? Was the paid post labelled as advertising?
  4. What is still running in the brand's name? Ads with the content creator, posts scheduled for the coming days, a newsletter, a discount code. Anything still running speaks for the brand while the brand is silent.

If the checks take longer than about an hour, a brand publishes a holding statement: two or three sentences saying that it has seen the post, that it is checking the facts, and when it will say more. A holding statement buys time without saying anything that may later be wrong.

CWhat a statement contains: deny, diminish or rebuild

W. Timothy Coombs's situational crisis communication theory (Corporate Reputation Review, 2007) is the framework most used to choose a response. It starts from one question: how much responsibility do people give the organisation for the crisis? Coombs groups crises into three types: victim crises (a rumour, a natural disaster: little responsibility), accidental crises (a technical failure: some responsibility) and preventable crises (the organisation knowingly took a risk or broke a rule: strong responsibility). The response should match the type:

  • Deny: say there is no crisis, attack the accuser, or blame someone else. Fits only a rumour that is false.
  • Diminish: an excuse ("we did not intend it") or a justification ("it was not that bad"). Fits an accident.
  • Rebuild: an apology that accepts responsibility, and an action that repairs (compensation, a change, a meeting with those hurt). Recommended for a preventable crisis.
  • Bolster: remind people of the good the organisation has done. Only as an addition to one of the others, never alone.

How this applies to a content creator's words. The brand did not say them, so it does not apologise for her words as if they were its own. But people do give the brand responsibility for what it controls: it chose her, it pays her, its ad may still show her, and its paid post may not have been labelled. For that part, the brand rebuilds: it says what it did wrong or what it stopped, and what it does next. A useful test for every sentence: is it a fact the brand can prove, or an action the brand has taken or will take by a stated date? Sentences that are neither (feelings, guesses, attacks) usually make things worse.

Three sentences that turn a statement into a second crisis: "We are sorry if anyone was offended" (the conditional "if" puts the problem on the people who were hurt; this is called a non-apology); "no comment" (it reads as having something to hide); and any guess about the content creator's private life or motives.

DWhat the law and the contract say

1 · The disclosure rule (Session 5, Part D). A paid post must be recognisable as advertising before the viewer reads on. The Swiss Commission for Fairness puts the responsibility on the advertiser, the brand (Principle A.4), and names the person who runs the account as well (rule B.15). If a journalist finds that the brand's paid post had no clear label, the brand cannot say "that was her job": it shares the duty. The right answer is to say so, and to have the label added the same day.

2 · Defamation and personality rights. In Switzerland, accusing a person in public of dishonourable conduct is a criminal offence unless the accuser can prove the statement true or had serious reasons to believe it (Swiss Criminal Code, art. 173, defamation; art. 174 covers a statement the author knows is false). The Swiss Civil Code protects a person's personality, including their private messages, against unlawful use (art. 28). For the brand this means: quote or describe what she said, with the date and the place; do not describe what kind of person she is; never publish her private messages to the brand.

3 · The contract you signed in Session 5. The termination clause for conduct (Session 5, Part E) answers four questions: which conduct allows the brand to end the deal, who decides, what happens to the content, and what happens to the money. Three facts hold whatever the clause says:

  • The brand controls its own ads. Usage rights give the brand the right to run her video as an ad; they do not oblige it to. It can pause the partnership ad at once.
  • Only she can delete her own post. The brand can ask her to remove the sponsored post from her account; the contract decides whether she must.
  • Ending a contract without a clause that allows it is a breach. If nothing was agreed, the brand that stops paying her may owe her the rest of the fee, unless both sides agree how to end it.

✅ Step 3 · team work

The crisis: four facts, one decision each

Why. In a real crisis, facts arrive one at a time and each one can change what the right decision is. A brand that writes down what it decided, when, and why can explain itself later to its board, to a journalist and, if needed, to a court. That written record is the decision log.

What to do. Your team is the marketing team of Sonnaz Audio SA (fictional; Lausanne; it sells the Sonnaz Q1 noise-cancelling headphones for CHF 199). Fact 1 is below. The instructor releases Facts 2, 3 and 4 one after another; each appears on every laptop by itself within a few seconds. For each fact, discuss as a team, choose one decision, write the reason in one or two sentences, and press Log this decision. A logged decision cannot be changed afterwards: if a later fact changes your mind, say so under the later fact.

✅ Step 5 · the whole class

The press round: ask one team a question, answer the questions to your own team

Why. A statement is tested by the questions it does not answer. A journalist reads it looking for the gap: a fact left out, a promise without a date, a sentence that contradicts the decision log.

What to do, in this order.

  1. Every student reads the other teams' statements below (press Refresh to get the newest).
  2. Every student chooses one other team and writes one question a journalist would ask it, at most 120 characters. Aim at the gap: "You say you paused the ad. When exactly, and how much did it spend after the clip appeared?" is a good question; "Why did you do that?" is not. Sending again replaces your question.
  3. When the instructor calls your team, one person reads your statement aloud and answers the questions your team received, on the spot. Online teams answer from their team room on the screen.
  4. Afterwards, write down the question your team found hardest and the answer you would give now.
