How to negotiate a content creator contract: the fee, usage rights, exclusivity and disclosure

Bring: one laptop per person today, not per team. Same teams as Session 4.

Why this session exists. In Session 4 your team chose up to three content creators at the fee printed on their card. A real brand never pays the card price for nothing more than a post: it signs a contract, and the contract decides what the brand may do with the video, whether the content creator may also work for a competitor, how the post is marked as paid, and what happens if the content creator says something the brand cannot stand behind. Those terms are worth as much money as the fee. Sessions 6 and 7 use the contract you write today.

Today in one sentence: in pairs, one of you is Sonnaz Audio and the other is one of the content creators your team chose in Session 4; each of you gets a private brief that says what each term is worth to your side in Swiss francs; you negotiate one contract, and then both briefs are shown side by side.

You leave today with: a one-page term sheet (the fee, how it is paid, the usage rights, the exclusivity, the disclosure, the approval rounds and the clause for ending the contract), the value of that deal to each side in Swiss francs, and the one clause that cost your side the most.

✅ Step 2 · on the big screen

What a content creator contract settles

A contract between a brand and a content creator answers five questions. A: what the brand pays, for which work, and when. B: what the brand may do with the video afterwards. C: whether the content creator may also work for a competitor. D: how the post is marked as paid, which the law decides more than the two sides do. E: what happens if one side wants to end the deal. Below each one is explained, with a calculator where there is a number to work out. The last part, F, shows how two sides who want different things can both come out better by trading terms instead of meeting in the middle on each one.

Why this matters for Step 4. In Step 4 you negotiate all of these terms at once, as the brand or as the content creator. The person who argues only about the fee usually ends with a worse deal than the person who knows what each of the other terms is worth to both sides.

AThe fee, the work it pays for, and when the money is paid

The fee is the fixed amount the brand pays. It is always tied to a list of deliverables: the exact pieces of content, written into the contract. In Session 4 the deliverable was one video or post plus two stories. A good contract also writes down the date each piece goes online, how long it stays online (the content creator could otherwise delete it after a day), and which link and discount code it carries.

When the money is paid is a separate term. A brand prefers to pay after the post is online, often 30 days later, because then it pays only for work it has seen. A content creator prefers money before the work, because the content creator then carries no risk that the brand pays late or not at all. A common middle is half at signature and half on the day the post goes online.

A performance bonus is money on top of the fixed fee, paid per sale that is counted through the content creator's own code or tagged link (Session 4, mechanism C). It moves part of the risk from the brand to the content creator: the brand pays more only when the post sells.

BUsage rights: what the brand may do with the video after it is posted

The video belongs to the content creator, who made it and whose face is in it. Without a written permission, the brand may share the post, but it may not cut it into its own advertisement. Usage rights are that written permission. They are defined by four things, and each one is negotiated: where the brand may use the video (only on its own Instagram and website, or also in paid advertising), for how long (one month, three months, a year), in which countries, and whether the brand may edit it.

The most valuable kind for a brand today is a paid advertisement that runs from the content creator's own account. The viewer sees the content creator's name at the top, not the brand's, and such ads are often cheaper per click than the brand's own studio ads. The platforms have a tool for this: on Instagram and Facebook it is called partnership ads, on TikTok Spark Ads. In both cases the content creator has to give the brand a permission inside the app, for a limited time. Meta describes partnership ads as ads that "allow you to run Facebook and Instagram ads from a creator or other partner's handle", and states that "a creator must give permission to a brand to use individual posts as partnership ads" (Meta for Developers). TikTok describes Spark Ads as an ad format that uses organic TikTok posts, other creators' posts "(with their authorization)": the content creator generates an authorization code, and the length of the authorization can be customised; in TikTok's Content Suite a request defaults to 365 days (TikTok Business Help Center). So the content creator controls the permission in the app, and a contract that says "3 months" must match the period the content creator sets there.

For the content creator, usage rights have a cost. Their face appears in an advertisement that their followers see again and again, which wears out the audience's attention, and while the ad runs, a competing brand is less likely to book them. That is why usage rights are paid for on top of the fee, and why a longer period costs more. There is no official price list and no survey of what brands pay. One influencer-marketing software company writes that "most influencers charge an additional 20 to 50 percent of their base rate for usage rights" (impact.com); it cites no data, so treat it as a rule of thumb.

CExclusivity: whether the content creator may work for a competitor

Exclusivity is a promise by the content creator not to promote a competing brand for a stated period. It is defined by two things: which brands count as competitors (only other headphone brands, or every audio product: headphones, earbuds and speakers), and for how long (one month after the post, three months, a year).

For the brand, exclusivity protects the launch: a viewer who sees the content creator praise Sonnaz headphones on Monday and another brand's headphones on Friday believes neither. For the content creator, exclusivity costs the deals they must refuse during that period. That cost can be worked out, and it is the content creator's strongest argument for a higher fee or a narrower clause. The same company's rule of thumb for exclusivity is 20 to 100 percent of the base rate, again without data; the calculator below works it out from the deals the content creator would really refuse.

Try it · what an exclusivity clause costs the content creator

Before you change anything, write down your guess: is a 12-month clause on all audio products about four times as expensive for the content creator as a 3-month clause on headphones only, or more?

The calculation: deals per year ÷ 12 × months × average fee. With the defaults, a 3-month clause on headphones costs 2 ÷ 12 × 3 × 800 = CHF 400. A 12-month clause on all audio costs 5 ÷ 12 × 12 × 800 = CHF 4,000: ten times as much, not four, because both the period and the list of competitors grow.
DDisclosure: the legal duty to mark a post as paid

The question. How must a paid post be marked, who is responsible if it is not, and can the two sides agree to leave the mark out?

Why it matters. A viewer judges a recommendation differently when they know the person was paid for it. That is why the law treats an unmarked paid post as unfair to the viewer and to competitors. It is the one term in the contract that the two sides are not free to negotiate: whatever they sign, the rule applies.

  • The Swiss rule. Switzerland's Federal Act against Unfair Competition (UWG, SR 241) forbids, in its general clause, art. 2, any commercial practice that is misleading or "contravenes in any other way the rules of good faith". The Swiss Fairness Commission (Schweizerische Lauterkeitskommission), the body that hears complaints about advertising, has written that general clause out in its Principles. Principle B.15 (version of January 2025) says that commercial communication, whatever its form or medium, is unfair "if it is not clearly recognisable as such and clearly separated from the rest of the content". Principle A.3 names "influencing" as a form of commercial communication. B.15 paragraph 2 covers social media accounts directly: when a post is not clearly recognisable as commercial, the relationship with the brand must be disclosed, "in particular when the third party pays sponsoring or comparable fees or gives goods". A product sent free is therefore enough.
  • Real cases. In 2019 the Commission ruled that a Swiss snowboarder whose post showed a sponsor's shop "should have labelled his post as commercial communication". In another case it found no label was needed, because the sponsors' logos on a professional athlete were obvious to everyone: whether a label is needed "must be judged case by case". In 2021 it accepted "Sponsored" as a word "familiar and generally understood by the public".
  • Who is responsible. The Commission's Principle A.4 puts the responsibility on the advertiser, the brand. B.15 paragraph 2 names the person who uses the account, the content creator, and the 2019 complaints were decided against the content creators themselves. So neither side can push the duty onto the other in the contract.
  • What happens after a complaint. The Commission does not fine. It recommends that the brand or the content creator stop, and if the recommendation is ignored it may publish its decision with full names, or bring the case to court itself. Under the UWG, a court can order the practice to stop (art. 9).
  • In the EU, the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive lists as always unfair the promotion of a product "where a trader has paid for the promotion without making that clear" (Annex I, point 11). On 14 February 2024 the European Commission published a check of 576 influencers' posts by the consumer authorities of 22 member states, Norway and Iceland: 97 % had published commercial content, but only 20 % systematically disclosed it as advertising, and 38 % did not use the platforms' labels, such as Instagram's "paid partnership" switch. In the USA, the FTC's Endorsement Guides (2023) require a disclosure that is "difficult to miss" and "easily understandable by ordinary consumers".
  • The platforms' own labels. Instagram's branded content rules require the "paid partnership" label tagging the brand. TikTok asks content creators to turn on the content disclosure setting, which adds a "Paid partnership" label. YouTube asks the uploader to tick "my video includes branded content", which shows a disclosure at the start of the video.

Before you tick anything, write down your guess: is "#ad" written at the very end of the caption, after ten other hashtags, clear enough?

The check applies the reading the rules give: the viewer must recognise the post as paid before reading on, without opening "more". The platform label and a word at the start of the caption do that; a tag at the end, among other tags, is seen last or not at all; "thanks @brand" does not say that money or goods were given. The Fairness Commission decides real cases one by one, so this is a guide, not a ruling.
EEnding the contract: the clause for the day something goes wrong

A contract says how it can end before its term. For a content creator contract the important case is this one: the content creator says or does something in public that the brand cannot stand behind, while the brand's video and ads are still running. A termination clause for conduct (often called a morality clause) answers four questions in writing: which conduct allows the brand to end the deal (stated as precisely as possible, because "anything that harms the brand" lets the brand decide alone); who decides; what happens to the content (the brand stops its ads at once, the content creator removes the post or not); and what happens to the money (the content creator keeps the fee for the work already done, or pays some or all of it back).

For the brand, the clause is insurance. For the content creator, a clause that lets the brand take back the whole fee on its own judgement is a serious risk. Session 7 puts your team in exactly that situation, and the clause you sign today is the one you will have to work with.

FHow both sides can gain: trading terms instead of meeting in the middle

The fee is a pure transfer: every franc one side gains, the other loses. The other terms are different, because the two sides do not value them equally. Usage rights may be worth a lot to the brand and cost the content creator little, if the period is short. A broad exclusivity clause may be worth little to the brand and cost the content creator a lot. When each side gives way on the term that matters less to it and gets the term that matters more, both end up better off than if they had met in the middle on every term. This is called trading (in negotiation books: logrolling, or integrative negotiation).

To trade, each side has to find out what the other side values, which means asking questions ("what would a 12-month clause cost you?") instead of only stating positions. And each side needs its walk-away point: the deal below which it is better to sign nothing. In negotiation books this alternative is called the BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. For the brand it is booking another content creator from its list; for the content creator, it is the other offers they have.

Try it · meeting in the middle, or trading

Two terms. For each, the brand writes what getting it is worth to the brand, and the content creator writes what giving it costs them, in Swiss francs. Before you look at the result, write down your guess: which deal gives the two sides together more money, meeting in the middle on both terms, or trading?

"Meeting in the middle" here means each term is granted halfway: half the usage period, half the exclusivity period, so each side gets half of each value. The trade means the brand gets the full usage rights and gives up the exclusivity. The fee can then move to share the gain between the two sides.

✅ Step 4 · the pair talks; each fills in the same term sheet

Negotiate the term sheet

A term sheet is a short list of the terms two sides have agreed, written before a lawyer turns them into a full contract. Below is the term sheet for your pair. Every term has to have a value before you can lock it.

  1. Talk. Start with the first offers you wrote in Step 3. Ask questions about what each term costs or is worth to the other side. Your brief's table tells you what each option is worth to you; the total at the bottom of your brief updates as you fill in the term sheet.
  2. When you agree on a term, both of you select it on your own laptop, so the two term sheets are identical.
  3. If your total falls below CHF 0, the deal is worse for you than your walk-away. You may refuse to sign: tick "No deal" and lock.
  4. When all terms are agreed, compare the two laptops line by line, then both press Lock the term sheet. A locked term sheet cannot be changed.

The term sheet

Terms from today
Deliverable — one piece of content the contract lists: a video, a post, a story, with its date and how long it stays online.
