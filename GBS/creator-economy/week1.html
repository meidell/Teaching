One question, fifteen phones, and the pipeline behind the answer

Bring a laptop and a phone; both are used in the session.

This session measures which sources three answer engines cite for one buying question, then explains the mechanism that produced the result: how a web search engine crawls, indexes and ranks pages, and how an answer engine rewrites the question, retrieves passages, reranks them, generates an answer and attributes citations. Every later session in the search half of the course refers back to this pipeline.

✅ 14:21 · 12 min · Step 2

The query round

The same question is asked fifteen ways: three engines × five phrasings. Each student is assigned one cell of this grid, so the class covers all fifteen combinations. The design separates two variables: whether the result depends on the engine, and whether it depends on the phrasing. The five phrasings differ in the shape of the intent (keyword, conversational, community-seeking, comparison, situational), which is the variable most likely to change what is retrieved.

What each engine does with the text you submit

The three engines are three different systems, and the differences explain part of what appears on the wall. The descriptions below are taken from the operators' own documentation; the pipeline stages they refer to are explained in Step 4.

Google · ordinary results and AI OverviewThe query is matched against Google's own web index, which Googlebot has built by crawling. Ordinary results are ranked pages. An AI Overview, when shown, is generated by a Gemini model from passages retrieved through the same index and ranking systems, with links to the pages used. Google's AI Mode goes further and uses what Google calls query fan-out: the question is broken into sub-topics and many searches are issued at once, so the text a user typed is not the text that reaches the index.
ChatGPTThe model first decides whether the question needs a web search at all; a question it can answer from training data may get no retrieval and therefore no citations. When it searches, it rewrites the question into search queries, obtains results from third-party search providers (Microsoft's Bing index is the one OpenAI has named) and pages fetched by its own crawler, OAI-SearchBot, then writes an answer and attaches source links. OpenAI documents two separate robots.txt tokens: OAI-SearchBot for appearing in search answers and GPTBot for training data; a site can allow one and refuse the other.
PerplexityAlways retrieves. It maintains its own index, built by PerplexityBot, which its documentation says is used for search results and not for training models. Every answer carries numbered citations, so the retrieval step is visible. Perplexity's answers are sensitive to phrasing because the retrieved set changes with the query; this is the engine on which the phrasing variable is easiest to observe.
Consequence for the measurementA source can only be cited if the engine's crawler was allowed to fetch it, its index contains it, and the retrieval stage selected it for this query. Each engine has a different crawler, a different index and a different retrieval policy, so the same phrasing can produce different sources on different engines without either being wrong.

Three categories of source

Marketing distinguishes three kinds of media by who controls them. Owned: the brand's own sites and channels (sony.ch, a brand's YouTube channel). Earned: independent third parties that chose to cover the product (review sites such as rtings.com, press, expert blogs, retailers' editorial pages). Social: user-generated platforms (Reddit, YouTube creators, TikTok). The wall marks owned domains; the room classifies the rest.

A 2025 study that ran this comparison at scale, across verticals, languages and query paraphrases, found that AI search engines show a "systematic and overwhelming bias towards earned media" over brand-owned and social content, whereas Google's ordinary results have a more balanced mix (Chen, Wang, Chen & Koudas, 2025). The class data can be compared with that finding: the share of owned domains on the wall, and whether Google's rows look different from the ChatGPT and Perplexity rows.

Discussion

Three questions: Did the engines give the same answer? What share of the sources is owned, earned and social? Does the share differ between Google's ordinary results and the two answer engines, as the 2025 study predicts?

The pipeline: how a page becomes a citation

Two systems are involved. A web search engine builds an index of the web and ranks pages against a query. An answer engine adds a second pipeline on top: it retrieves passages from such an index and has a language model write an answer from them. The wall is the output of both. This step goes through each stage, what it computes, and where a page can be eliminated.

A · The search engine: crawl, index, rank

Google describes its own system in three stages: crawling, indexing and serving. Not every page passes each stage.

Crawling: finding and fetching pages

A crawler (Googlebot, Bingbot, PerplexityBot, OAI-SearchBot) starts from URLs it already knows and from sitemaps, fetches each page, and extracts the links on it to find more. There is no central registry of the web; a page nobody links to and no sitemap lists is not found. Before fetching, the crawler reads the site's robots.txt, which can exclude it. Each AI operator uses a different crawler token, so a site can be present in Google's index and absent from Perplexity's. Pages that build their content with JavaScript are queued for rendering (execution in a headless browser) and may be indexed later, or with less of their content.

Eliminated here: pages that block the engine's crawler, pages with no inbound links, content that only exists after JavaScript runs.
Indexing: turning pages into a searchable structure

The fetched text is tokenised (split into words or sub-word units), normalised, and stored in an inverted index: for each term, the list of documents that contain it, with positions and counts. Duplicate pages are merged under one canonical URL. Structured data on the page (schema.org markup for products, reviews, prices, FAQs) is parsed into fields the engine can use directly. A simplified inverted index for three pages:

term → documents (position) noise → D1 (3, 41) D2 (7) D3 (2) cancelling → D1 (4, 42) D2 (8) D3 (3) commute → D2 (19) "under 200" → D2 (12) D3 (9) industry-leading→ D1 (1)

A query is answered by looking up its terms and intersecting the lists, which is why this structure answers a query over billions of pages in milliseconds. It also explains the first mechanism on the wall: a page that never uses the words of the query (commute, under 200) is not in the candidate set for that query, however good it is.

Eliminated here: pages that do not contain the query's vocabulary; duplicates folded into another URL.
Ranking: scoring the candidates

The candidate pages are scored by several families of signals, combined by a machine-learned ranking model.

Lexical relevance. The classical scores are TF-IDF and BM25 (Robertson & Walker, 1994). A term counts more if it appears often in the document (term frequency, tf), less if it appears in many documents (inverse document frequency, idf: "headphones" is in every candidate, "commute" in few), and long documents are normalised so they do not win by volume. BM25 for a query q and document d:

score(d,q) = Σ over terms t in q of idf(t) · tf(t,d)·(k₁+1) / ( tf(t,d) + k₁·(1 − b + b·|d|/avgdl) ) with idf(t) = ln( (N − n(t) + 0.5)/(n(t) + 0.5) + 1 ), k₁ ≈ 1.2, b ≈ 0.75

Link analysis. PageRank (Brin & Page, 1998) treats a link as a vote: a page's score is the sum of the scores of the pages linking to it, each divided by the number of links that page gives out, with a damping factor d ≈ 0.85 that models a reader who sometimes jumps to a random page. It is computed over the whole link graph, offline, and is one signal among many today; the idea that other sites' behaviour towards a page is evidence about the page survives in every modern ranker.

PR(A) = (1 − d)/N + d · Σ over pages T linking to A of PR(T) / outlinks(T)

Semantic matching. Since 2019 Google has used transformer language models (BERT, later MUM) in ranking to match the meaning of a query to a passage rather than its exact words, and since 2021 individual passages of a page can be ranked, not only the page as a whole. This is why the conversational phrasing can retrieve a page that never uses the word "commute".

Quality signals and raters. Google employs external Search Quality Raters who grade result pages against a published guideline document (the Search Quality Rater Guidelines, around 170 pages). Their grades do not change a page's rank directly; they are used to evaluate and train changes to the ranking systems. The guidelines' central concept is E-E-A-T: Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness. Three of the four are assessed from evidence outside the page (reputation, what other sources say, who links), not from the page's own claims. Since 2023 Google has also shown a dedicated Discussions and forums block for many product queries, a deliberate decision to surface first-hand user experience.

Eliminated here: pages with weak term and meaning overlap; pages with no independent evidence of authority; pages whose passages do not answer the question.

B · The answer engine: retrieval-augmented generation

An answer engine is a language model connected to a retriever, an architecture named retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) by Lewis et al. (2020). Instead of answering from what the model learned in training (its parametric knowledge, which has a cut-off date and no sources), it fetches text at query time and conditions the answer on it. Five stages, each of which can drop a source:

Query understanding and rewriting

The model classifies the request (does it need fresh information? is it a product question?), and rewrites it into one or more search queries. Google's AI Mode calls this query fan-out: the question is split into sub-topics and many queries are issued simultaneously. A user's phrasing therefore reaches the index as several machine-written queries; the "situational" phrasing on the grid may become "best headphones airplane engine noise", "ANC headphones under 200 CHF" and "headphone battery life long flights".

Effect: phrasing matters less than at the lexical stage, but which sub-queries are generated is invisible and differs between engines.
Retrieval: lexical and dense

Each query is run against an index, and modern retrieval is hybrid. The lexical half is the inverted index with BM25, as above. The dense half represents each passage as an embedding: a vector of several hundred to a few thousand numbers produced by a neural model, such that texts with similar meaning have vectors that point in similar directions. The query is embedded the same way and the nearest vectors are found by cosine similarity. Searching billions of vectors exactly is too slow, so engines use approximate nearest-neighbour structures (graph indexes such as HNSW) that trade a little recall for speed. The unit retrieved is a chunk, typically a passage of a few hundred tokens, not a whole page.

cos(q, p) = (q · p) / (‖q‖ ‖p‖) q, p ∈ ℝⁿ, n ≈ 768 … 3072 retrieve: top-k chunks by α·BM25(q,p) + (1−α)·cos(q,p)
Eliminated here: chunks that neither share vocabulary with the query nor sit near it in embedding space; pages whose relevant content is spread thin across the page rather than concentrated in one passage.
Reranking

Retrieval returns a few hundred candidate chunks cheaply. A cross-encoder, a transformer that reads the query and one chunk together and outputs a relevance score, rescores them. It is far more accurate than vector similarity and far more expensive, which is why it runs only on the shortlist. The top k chunks, often between 5 and 20, go forward. Authority signals from the search engine's ranking can enter here as features.

Eliminated here: chunks that match the topic but do not answer the question; weakly supported sources when stronger ones cover the same claim.
Generation

The surviving chunks are placed in the model's context window together with the question and a system instruction of the form "answer using only the sources provided and cite them". The model then generates the answer token by token. Because the answer is conditioned on retrieved text, it is said to be grounded; grounding reduces fabricated claims but does not eliminate them, since the model may still misread or over-generalise a source. What the model can extract from a chunk is what is stated in it: a chunk that says "the XM5 is quieter than the QC45 on aircraft noise" supplies a usable comparative claim; a chunk that says "industry-leading noise cancellation" supplies none.

Eliminated here: sources whose content cannot be turned into a statement that answers the question.
Citation attribution

The answer's sentences are linked back to the chunks that support them, either because the model emits citation markers as it writes or through a separate attribution step that matches each sentence to its best-supporting chunk. A chunk used in the context window but not matched to any sentence is not cited, even though it influenced the answer. The domains on the wall are the sources that survived all five stages and were attributed.

Eliminated here: sources that shaped the answer but were not attributed.

D · What the evidence supports, and what remains open

Established. AI search engines cite earned media far more than brand-owned or social content, and the engines differ from each other in domain diversity, freshness and sensitivity to phrasing (Chen et al., 2025). Adding citations, quotations and statistics to a page raised its visibility in generative answers by up to about 40% on a benchmark of queries, with the size of the effect depending on the domain (Aggarwal et al., 2024, the paper that introduced the term generative engine optimization). Both results are consistent with the pipeline: extractable, well-supported statements survive the generation and attribution stages.

Hypotheses, each tied to a stage. The following are plausible mechanisms for the pattern on the wall. None has been tested on this category, and Session 2 tests them.

  • Vocabulary match → retrieval. Forum titles are written in the user's own words ("best ANC headphones under 200?"); manufacturer copy is not. Testable by comparing query terms with page text.
  • Comparative content → generation. A discussion among users produces statements of the form "A is better than B at X", which an answer needs; a brand page compares itself with nobody. Testable by counting comparative claims per source.
  • Independent authority → ranking and reranking. Links, mentions and reputation outside the page (the E-E-A-T evidence) favour independent sources. Testable from inbound-link data.
  • Training data → generation prior. Forum text is heavily represented in the corpora the models were trained on, and both Google and OpenAI announced content agreements with Reddit in 2024. A model may prefer to cite what it already "knows". Hard to test from outside; a reason for caution rather than a conclusion.
  • Chunk structure → retrieval and attribution. A question followed by self-contained answers yields chunks that stand alone; a marketing page yields chunks that depend on their context. Testable by inspecting how a page splits into passages.
Two or three sentences. Name the stage (crawl, index, rank, rewrite, retrieve, rerank, generate, attribute) and the evidence from your row.

Teams and roles

Five teams of three, fixed for the term. In a team of three, each member's contribution is visible.

In each session every member takes one of three roles; the roles rotate:

  • Operator: operates the tools and is responsible for the team producing its output.
  • Skeptic: argues against the team's conclusion before it is handed in. The role makes critical review a defined task rather than a personal matter.
  • Scribe: writes the team's output; only what the Scribe records is handed in.

Terms introduced today

Crawler / robots.txt · the program that fetches pages, and the file that tells it what it may fetch. Googlebot, Bingbot, PerplexityBot, OAI-SearchBot, GPTBot.
Render queue · where JavaScript-built pages wait to be executed before indexing.
Inverted index · term → list of documents containing it. The structure that makes web-scale lookup fast.
Canonical URL · the one address an engine keeps for a set of duplicate pages.
TF-IDF / BM25 · lexical relevance scores: term frequency, discounted for common terms, normalised for length.
PageRank · link-based authority: a page's score derives from the scores of pages linking to it.
Passage ranking · ranking a section of a page, not only the page.
Quality raters / E-E-A-T · human evaluators grading results by Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness; used to train ranking, not to rank pages directly.
RAG · retrieval-augmented generation: retrieve text, then generate from it.
Query fan-out · one question rewritten into many machine-issued queries.
Embedding / dense retrieval · text as a vector; similar meaning, similar direction; found by cosine similarity.
ANN (HNSW) · approximate nearest-neighbour search, fast at the cost of a little recall.
Chunk · the passage-sized unit that is retrieved and cited.
Cross-encoder / reranking · rescoring a shortlist by reading query and chunk together.
Context window · the text a model can see while generating; retrieved chunks are placed in it.
Grounding · conditioning the answer on retrieved text.
Citation attribution · linking answer sentences to supporting chunks; unattributed chunks are not cited.
Owned / earned / social media · brand-controlled, independent third-party, and user-generated sources.
GEO · generative engine optimization: changing a page to raise its visibility in generated answers.
