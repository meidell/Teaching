How answer engines select sources

This section explains the mechanism behind the results on the wall.

Retrieval and summarisation

An answer engine works in two stages. It first retrieves documents that appear to answer the question, then summarises them, extracting the statements it can make with confidence. These are two separate selections. A page can be retrieved but not quoted, if none of its content stands on its own out of context. A page can be well suited to quotation but never retrieved, if few other sources link to or reference it.

Why forum threads are frequently cited

  • Phrasing that matches the query. A thread titled "best ANC headphones under 200?" uses the user's own words; a manufacturer page states "industry-leading noise cancellation", which matches no actual query.
  • Comparative content. A discussion among many users produces the comparative statements a summary needs; a single company's page does not compare itself unfavourably with its competitors.
  • Perceived independence. Sources are weighted partly on how the rest of the web treats them; user forums, including negative reviews, signal independent opinion.
  • Structure. A question followed by self-contained answers is close to the format a retrieval system extracts most easily.

Why brand pages are cited less

A brand page typically answers a different question ("who we are"), is written to persuade rather than to inform, has an evident interest in the outcome, and is not confirmed by independent sources. Google's Search Quality Rater Guidelines describe the relevant criteria as E-E-A-T: Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness and Trustworthiness. Three of these four are assessed by other sources, not claimed by the brand itself.

Two conditions for being cited

Extractability: the claim is stated close to the question, in a form that remains meaningful when quoted on its own. Independent confirmation: other sources that the system already trusts make the same claim. Advertising, where most marketing budgets go, provides neither.

Open questionToday's data shows which sources are cited, not whether citation can be influenced. It is unclear whether structure alone is sufficient, or whether independent references built up over time are required. In Session 3 each team publishes a real page to test this; the result is measured in January.

✅ 15:08 · 8 min · Step 5

Teams and roles

Five teams of three, fixed for the term. In a team of three, each member's contribution is visible.

In each session every member takes one of three roles; the roles rotate:

  • Operator: operates the tools and is responsible for the team producing its output.
  • Skeptic: argues against the team's conclusion before it is handed in. The role makes critical review a defined task rather than a personal matter.
  • Scribe: writes the team's output; only what the Scribe records is handed in.
✅ 15:16 · 22 min · Step 6

Three candidate reasons

Using the wall, explain why these sources were cited and not others. Write three candidate reasons. Each must be a mechanism, not an observation: a single sentence that could be shown to be false.

Observation versus mechanismObservation: "Reddit came up a lot." It cannot be wrong, and therefore explains nothing. Mechanism: "It is cited because the thread's title is phrased as the user's question." This makes a prediction (a thread with a vague title should be cited less) and can be tested. The teams' mechanisms are tested against each other in Session 2.
The most testable reason is the most useful one; it is tested first.

"A page can be made quotable by structure alone. Who links to it does not matter."
The claim is probably false. Look for a real counterexample (a page, a query, a screenshot), or for evidence that it is true, and bring it to Session 2; either counts toward participation. Evidence that the claim holds would change the approach taken in Session 3.
