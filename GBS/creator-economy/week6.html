How to read a campaign report and find the content creator with bought followers

Bring: one laptop per team is enough today. Teams as in Sessions 3 and 4.

Why this session exists. After a campaign, the brand receives a report from its agency, or builds one from the platforms' statistics. The report decides whether the content creators are paid again next year and whether the marketing budget grows. Many of the numbers in such reports measure attention that cannot be checked, or that was bought. Today you learn which numbers mean what, how fake followers and fake likes are made and the traces they leave, and which numbers a finance director accepts. Your January playbook is marked against the standard the class writes at the end of today.

Today in one sentence: your team receives the agency's report on a November campaign by Sonnaz Audio (the brand from Sessions 4 and 5) with five content creators; one of them bought followers and likes, and the report does not say which; you find that content creator from the numbers alone, then you rewrite the report so that it shows only numbers a finance director would accept.

You leave today with: your team's verdict and the evidence for it, your team's version of the report for the finance director, and the class's measurement standard: the numbers every campaign report in this course must show, and how each is counted.

✅ Step 2 · on the big screen, then on your laptop

What the campaign numbers mean

A campaign report is a table of numbers about one or more sponsored posts. Each number answers a different question, and some are counted by the platform, some by the brand and some estimated by the agency. Read each one below with three questions in mind: who counted it, what one unit of it is, and could someone have bought it.

AReach, impressions and views: how many people, how many times

Reach is the number of different accounts that saw the post at least once. Impressions is the number of times the post was shown on a screen, counting the same account again each time it scrolls past. Views is the platform's count of plays or displays; Instagram now reports "views" for posts, reels and stories in its Insights, TikTok counts a view when a video starts to play, YouTube counts a view only after its own checks against automated plays. All three are counted by the platform, on its own servers, and shown to the account owner in the app's statistics (Instagram Insights, TikTok Analytics, YouTube Studio). The brand never sees the raw data: it sees a screenshot the content creator sends, or a number the agency copied.

By hand. A post is shown 4,100 times to 3,300 different accounts. Impressions ÷ reach = 4,100 ÷ 3,300 = 1.24: on average each person who saw it saw it 1.24 times. That ratio is called the frequency.

Reach compared with followers. A platform does not show a post to all followers. Its ranking system decides, post by post, whom to show it to (Session 4, Step 2). On Instagram a post typically reaches a minority of the followers; on TikTok a video is shown mainly to people who do not follow the account, so reach can be larger than the follower count. A low reach-to-followers ratio is therefore normal on Instagram for a large account, and normal on neither platform for an account whose followers are real people who chose to follow it recently.

BEngagement rate: the same likes give two very different figures

Engagement is the sum of the actions people took on the post: likes, comments, shares and saves. The engagement rate divides that sum by a base, and there are two bases in use. By followers: engagement ÷ followers. By reach (or by views): engagement ÷ the number of people who saw the post. Agencies and content creators' media kits often quote the first, because it is easy to compute from public numbers; the second is the one that says how the people who actually saw the post reacted. The report must say which one it uses, or the figure cannot be compared with any other.

Change any number. Before you change the followers, write down: if the bakery buys 10,000 followers who never look at its posts, which of the two rates changes, and in which direction?

CEarned media value: an estimate of what the attention would have cost as advertising

Earned media value (EMV) is a figure computed by the agency, not counted by anyone. The usual recipe: take the impressions, multiply by a price per 1,000 impressions that an advertiser would have paid for an ad (the CPM, cost per mille), and add a value per like, per comment and per share taken from a table the agency chose. The result is presented in francs, next to the fees paid, so that it looks like a return. Finance departments do not accept it, for three reasons: the CPM and the values per action are assumptions the agency picks itself; a franc of EMV is not a franc anyone received; and every bought like and every fake impression increases it.

DClicks, sales and cost per sale: the numbers the brand counts itself

A link click is counted twice: by the platform (a tap on the link sticker or the link in bio) and by the brand's website, through the tracking code added to the link (a UTM parameter, for example ?utm_source=instagram&utm_campaign=leman_nov). The website's analytics then follows that visitor and counts a sale if the visitor buys within a chosen time, the attribution window (often 7 or 30 days). A discount code given only to one content creator counts the sales of buyers who typed it, whether they clicked or not. Attributed sales = sales counted through the link within the window, plus sales with the code, minus those counted twice and minus returns. The cost per sale (CPA, Session 4) = fee ÷ attributed sales. These are the only numbers in a campaign report that are counted by the brand from money that arrived.

Two ways the sales are overstated: a buyer who saw the post, then searched the brand's name and bought without the code, is not counted (the report understates); a buyer who would have bought anyway but found the code on a coupon website is counted (the report overstates). The report must say which window and which counting rule it used.
✅ Step 3 · on the big screen, then on your laptop

How followers and likes are bought, and the six signs they leave

A follower, a like or a comment can be bought online for a few francs per thousand. The seller runs large numbers of accounts that are not used by the people they claim to be. Knowing how they are made tells you where to look.

AHow it is done
  • Follower farms. A seller controls thousands of accounts, created in bulk or taken over after their owners stopped using them, often registered in countries where phone numbers for verification are cheap. On order, the accounts follow the customer's account within hours. They do not watch anything afterwards.
  • Bought likes and comments. The same accounts, or scripts, like a chosen post and leave short comments that fit any post ("🔥🔥", "Love this ❤️", "Great content!"), because the script does not know what the post shows.
  • Engagement pods. Groups of accounts agree to like and comment on each other's posts within minutes of publishing, so the platform's ranking system reads early interest. The accounts are real; the interest is arranged.
  • Follow-unfollow. The account follows thousands of others, keeps those who follow back, then unfollows them. It leaves an account that follows unusually many others.
  • What the platforms do. Instagram, TikTok and YouTube remove accounts they identify as inauthentic and remove likes and follows that came from third-party apps; a purged account loses followers in one sudden drop. Their rules forbid buying followers or engagement, so an account that bought them risks losing reach as well.
BThe six signs, and the innocent explanation each one can have
SignWhy bought followers or likes produce itAn innocent explanation to rule out first
1 · A jump in followers with no jump in viewsBought followers arrive in one batch; they do not watch, so the views of the posts that week stay flat.One video went viral: the followers jumped AND the views of that video jumped in the same week.
2 · Reach far below the followers, for a recent follower baseThe platform shows posts to followers who interact; fake followers never do, so the post reaches a small share of them.A large, older Instagram account: a reach of 5–10 % of the followers is common there.
3 · More likes than plausible among the people reachedBought likes arrive from accounts the platform did not count as reached by the post.Rarely innocent. A share of 10–15 % of the reached accounts liking a post is already a strong result.
4 · Comments that would fit any postScripts and pods write short, generic comments that do not mention the product.A few emoji comments are normal on every account; look at the share of comments that say something specific.
5 · An audience in countries the content is not aboutFarm accounts sit where phone numbers are cheap, not where the content creator lives.An international niche (tech, travel, English-language content) has a genuinely international audience.
6 · Clicks and sales near zero for the reach reportedAccounts that are not people do not tap links and do not buy.A poor fit between the product and the audience (Session 4: a gym audience and over-ear headphones) also gives few sales.
The rule: one sign proves nothing, because each has an innocent explanation. Bought followers show several signs at once, and the innocent explanation of one sign does not fit the others.

The campaign report: find the content creator with bought followers

The situation. In November, Sonnaz Audio (the fictional Swiss brand from Sessions 4 and 5) paid five content creators from the Session 4 pool for one sponsored post each about its CHF 199 headphones, CHF 7,800 in fees in total. The agency sent the report below. Each content creator also sent a screenshot of their own statistics; the figures from those screenshots are in each content creator's card. One of the five bought followers and likes; the agency did not notice. Your team finds which one, using the numbers alone.

The agency's report · Sonnaz Audio · November campaign · five sponsored posts

The agency's conclusion, as written: "The campaign generated CHF 13,809 in earned media value for CHF 7,800 in fees, a return of 1.8 times. @zug.commute, @leman.sound and @objets.zurich delivered the strongest engagement. We recommend renewing all five partnerships."

Engagement rate here = (likes + comments + shares + saves) ÷ followers. EMV = impressions × CHF 30 per 1,000 + likes × CHF 0.80 + comments × CHF 5 + shares × CHF 8 + saves × CHF 2: the agency's own values.

Do this. Open each content creator's card. Compute what the agency did not: reach ÷ followers, engagement ÷ reach, likes ÷ reach, clicks ÷ reach. Look at the follower curve and the views curve, the audience countries and the comments. Tick the signs you see on each card. Then decide.

Rewrite the report for the finance director

Why. The finance director decides whether the content creator budget is renewed, and accepts a number only if three conditions hold: it was counted, not estimated; the counting rule is written down, so that next year's report counts the same way; and it is hard to buy, so that it cannot be inflated by the content creator or the agency. The class's measurement standard

The table below counts every team's choice for each number. A number becomes part of the class standard when most teams keep it in the report. This standard is what every campaign report in your January playbook is checked against: if the playbook quotes a number the class dropped, it must say why.

What a finance director accepts, in one paragraph. A campaign report shows, for each content creator: the fee; the reach, with the share of it in the country where the brand sells, both from the platform's own screenshot; the link clicks counted by the brand's website; the attributed sales, with the counting rule written at the top; and the cost per sale. Views and engagement rate by reach may appear as context. Followers, likes, engagement rate by followers and earned media value are left out, because they are not money, cannot be checked by the brand, or can be bought.
