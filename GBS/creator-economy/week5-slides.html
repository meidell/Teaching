Step 2 · why a contract

A brand never pays only for a post. It signs a contract that answers six questions.

A · The fee

What the brand pays, for which content, and when the money is paid.

B · Usage rights

What the brand may do with the video after it is posted.

C · Exclusivity

Whether the content creator may also work for a competitor.

D · Disclosure

How the post is marked as paid. The law decides this one.

E · Ending the contract

What happens if the content creator does something the brand cannot stand behind.

F · Trading

How both sides can gain by giving way on different terms.

The person who argues only about the fee usually ends with a worse deal than the person who knows what each other term is worth to both sides.

Point at Session 4: the card price was CHF 450 for @leman.sound and the brand got nothing beyond one post. Today the same money buys a contract.
Step 2 · A · the fee and a bonus

A bonus per sale moves part of the risk from the brand to the content creator

A performance bonus is money on top of the fixed fee, paid for each sale counted through the content creator's own code or tagged link (Session 4).

The post sells…Contract 1 · CHF 900 fixedContract 2 · CHF 600 + CHF 15 per sale
5 pairs (it goes badly)CHF 900 · CHF 180 a saleCHF 675 · CHF 135 a sale
20 pairs (as expected)CHF 900 · CHF 45 a saleCHF 900 · CHF 45 a sale

On the page, Step 2 A: write your guess first, then change the numbers. The two contracts cost the same at 20 sales: (900 − 600) ÷ 15.
These are the page’s default numbers, recomputed. Ask: who prefers Contract 2, the brand or the content creator? The brand when it is unsure the post will sell; the content creator when she trusts her audience. A bonus is also a signal: a content creator who refuses any bonus does not believe in her own conversion.
Step 2 · B · usage rights

Usage rights: the written permission to reuse the video, and four things define it

What defines them

Where: organic reposts, the brand's website, paid ads. How long: 1, 3, 6 or 12 months. Which countries. Whether the video may be edited.

The most valuable kind

A paid ad that runs from the content creator's own account: Instagram's "partnership ads", TikTok's "Spark Ads". The viewer sees the content creator's name, not the brand's.

For the content creator, usage rights cost something: her face in an ad her followers see again and again, and fewer bookings from competitors while it runs. A rule of thumb from a vendor (impact.com) adds 30 to 50 % of the fee for every three months; it cites no data.

Ask the room: if you were Sonnaz, would you rather have three more Stories or three months of ads from her account? This matters in the negotiation: usage rights are worth a lot to the brand.
Step 2 · C · exclusivity

Exclusivity costs the content creator the deals she must refuse, and the cost grows with both scope and time

cost = deals per year ÷ 12 × months × average fee
ClauseCalculationCost to the content creator
Headphones only, 3 months2 ÷ 12 × 3 × CHF 800CHF 400
All audio products, 12 months5 ÷ 12 × 12 × CHF 800CHF 4,000

Ten times as much, not four: the wider list of competitors and the longer period multiply each other.

The page’s defaults: 2 headphone deals a year, 3 other audio deals, CHF 800 average. For the brand, exclusivity protects the launch: a viewer who sees her praise two headphone brands in one week believes neither. Ask: which is worth more to Sonnaz, December only or 12 months?
Step 2 · D · disclosure

A paid post must be recognisable as advertising before the viewer reads on, and the two sides cannot agree otherwise

  • Switzerland: the Federal Act against Unfair Competition (UWG) forbids misleading commercial practice; the Swiss Fairness Commission's Principle B.15 requires commercial communication to be clearly recognisable.
  • Who is responsible: the brand (Principle A.4) and the person who runs the account (B.15, paragraph 2). The 2019 complaints were decided against the content creators themselves.
  • The EU: paying for a promotion "without making that clear" is always unfair (Directive 2005/29/EC, Annex I, point 11).
What counts as clear: the platform's "Paid partnership" label, or "Ad" at the very start of the caption, or saying it in the first seconds. "#ad" at the end, after ten other tags, does not.
Do the checkbox exercise on the page together: tick only "#ad at the end" and show the verdict. This is the one term that is not negotiable: a deal without a clear label is not a valid deal.
Step 2 · E · ending the contract

The clause for the day something goes wrong: who may end the contract, and who keeps the money

For the brand

Insurance: if she says or does something in public that Sonnaz cannot stand behind, Sonnaz can stop the ads and end the contract.

For the content creator

A risk: a clause that lets the brand take back the whole fee on its own judgement can cost her everything she earned.

The usual middle: the brand may end the contract for conduct that harms it, and the content creator keeps what she has earned so far. Session 7 puts your team in exactly that situation, with the clause you sign today.

Keep it short. Students tend to skip this term in the negotiation; remind them it is on the outcome form and that Session 7 uses it.
Step 2 · F · trading

Meeting in the middle gives both sides less than trading terms they value differently

Two terms. The brand values usage rights at CHF 400; they cost the content creator CHF 100. Long exclusivity is worth CHF 100 to the brand and costs her CHF 500.

DealThe brand gainsShe gainsTogether
Meet in the middle on both terms+CHF 250−CHF 300−CHF 50
Trade: full usage rights, no exclusivity+CHF 400−CHF 100+CHF 300
The brand wins both+CHF 500−CHF 600−CHF 100

After the trade, a higher fee of anything between CHF 100 and CHF 400 leaves both sides better off.

The page’s defaults (400, 100, 100, 500), recomputed. The point: to trade you must find out what the other side values, which means asking questions, not stating positions. Then ask everyone to write the Step 2 sentence (which term will be hardest to agree on, and why) and press the Step 2 button.
Part 2 · the negotiation

Today you do not negotiate as yourself. You negotiate for someone else, and you know only your own side.

Sonnaz Audio's influencer marketing manager

Negotiates for the brand. Has a boss, the chief marketing officer, and a limit on what she or he may sign.

@leman.sound's talent manager

An agent: negotiates the content creator's deals for her and keeps a share of the fee. Has a limit on what the client allows.

Each of you gets confidential instructions: a page that only your role sees. It says what your side knows, what it wants, what it will do if there is no deal, and what you may promise.

Why agents: in the creator economy most deals above a few hundred francs are negotiated by managers and agencies, not by the content creator. An agent has a limit on authority, which is both a tactic and a trap. The method: two sides, each with information the other lacks, the same case for every pair, then all the results compared.
Part 2 · the case

What both sides know: Sonnaz wants @leman.sound for its Christmas sales

  • Sonnaz Audio (fictional) sells the Sonnaz Q1 headphones at CHF 199 a pair, in Switzerland only, and keeps CHF 90 per pair sold.
  • @leman.sound (Léa Morel, fictional): headphone reviews on Instagram, in French, from Lausanne. 8,200 followers; a typical Reel is seen by about 3,100 people; 78 % of her viewers live in Switzerland. In Session 4 one Reel by her sold 12 pairs for CHF 450.
  • The package: one review Reel, three Stories and a link in her profile for four weeks, online between 1 and 24 December.
  • Her rate card, the price list in her media kit: one Reel CHF 450, one Story CHF 150, one week of link CHF 50.
  • Open: the fee, a bonus per pair, usage rights, exclusivity, what Sonnaz may check before posting, ending the contract, and anything you invent.
This is the "What both sides know" box on every student’s page. Do not add anything from either confidential page.
Part 2 · five words for preparing

Before you talk, write down five things

WordWhat it means
BATNAYour best alternative to a negotiated agreement: what you will actually do if there is no deal.
Walk-away pointThe worst deal you would still sign, because anything worse is less good than your BATNA.
Interest · positionA position is what you ask for ("CHF 2,000"). An interest is why you want it ("I need proof that my audience buys").
StandardA fact outside the two of you that supports a number: a past fee, a rate card, what others were paid.
OptionsTerms beyond the fee that cost one side little and help the other a lot.
Read each line once. These are the boxes of Step 4 (the last box is the first offer).
Part 2 · the five words in one example outside the case

You sell your used bike before you move abroad on Friday

BATNA

A bike shop buys it for CHF 200 today. So your walk-away point is CHF 200.

Standard

Three bikes like yours are listed online at CHF 280 to CHF 350. That supports asking about CHF 300.

Interests

Yours: cash before Friday. The buyer's: a bike that works on Monday, and a lock.

Option

You add your lock and helmet, worth little to you after Friday. The buyer pays CHF 320 instead of CHF 280.

The buyer never needs to know about the shop's CHF 200. You decide what you tell.

An example away from the case, so nobody copies it into the negotiation (the numbers are illustrative). Ask: what is the buyer’s BATNA? Another bike from the listings, at about CHF 300. So the zone where both prefer a deal runs from CHF 200 to about CHF 300, plus whatever the lock and helmet are worth to the buyer.
Part 2 · agents and authority

An agent may only promise what the client allows, and saying so is part of the negotiation

  • Both of you have a limit in your instructions: a maximum, or a period, beyond which your boss or your client must say yes.
  • "I have to check with my client" can be true, or it can be a way to stop giving way. Both happen.
  • If you agree something beyond your limit, record the deal as "subject to my client's approval": there is a box for it on the outcome form.
Do not say what the limits are. The debrief asks whether each side stated its limit or whether the other side assumed it.

Steps 3 and 4 · alone

Read your instructions, then write your preparation memo

Now: find your pair number on the screen. Read "What both sides know" and then your confidential instructions, alone. Then write Step 4: at least box 1 (your BATNA), box 3 (your walk-away point), box 5 (two standards) and box 6 (three options beyond the fee).

If you have a question about the facts, ask the instructor quietly. Your partner cannot help you with your own instructions.

Twenty minutes: ten to read, ten to write. Walk around. Common mistake: writing a position in the BATNA box ("I want CHF 2,000"). The BATNA is what happens without a deal.
Step 5 · the pair

Negotiate. Then each of you records exactly what was agreed.

  • Start by asking what matters to the other side, not with a number.
  • Every term on the outcome form needs an answer: fee, bonus, usage rights, exclusivity, approval, ending the contract.
  • The disclosure is fixed by law: a clear label, at the start.
  • "No deal" is an allowed outcome, if it is better for your side than the deal on the table.
  • At the end, each of you fills in Step 5 on your own laptop and presses Send our outcome.
About 22 minutes of talk. Warn at 15:37 ("five minutes left"), stop at 15:42. Switch the screen to deal.html tab 2 at 15:43 so pairs see their row appear; "records differ" is a debrief point, not a mistake to fix quietly.

Step 6 · question 1 of 4

Your BATNA

What would your side have done without a deal, and was your BATNA stronger or weaker than the other side’s? Does a weak BATNA mean you must ask for less?
On the page, Step 6: write your answer to question 1 in one or two sentences.
Draw out: her BATNA (CHF 600) was weak in money, but she did not have to say so; a weak BATNA justifies caution, not a low first offer. Sonnaz’s BATNA (@beatsbasel, about 14 pairs) was also weak, because his audience buys studio gear.
Step 6 · question 2 of 4

Where the number came from

You settled on CHF X. Which fact produced that number? Were the standards close to this deal (her own fees, this brand, this year) or general ones?
On the page, Step 6: write your answer to question 2 in one or two sentences.
Standards close to the case persuade more: her rate card (CHF 1,100), what Sonnaz paid her before (CHF 380 for one Story, known only to Sonnaz). A general rule of thumb (usage +30–50 %) is weaker. Pairs that agreed a standard first and the number second usually found it easier.
Step 6 · question 3 of 4

Interests and options

What did each side want besides money, and which terms did you invent that cost one side little and helped the other a lot?
On the page, Step 6: write your answer to question 3 in one or two sentences.
Look for: the results letter (Voltaplex), a bonus per pair (her conversion is good, so she should want one), usage rights from her account (followers for her, cheaper clicks for Sonnaz), exclusivity limited to headphones in December.
Step 6 · question 4 of 4

Agents and information

What could you promise without asking your boss or your client, and did you say so? Would knowing the other side’s instructions have made the deal easier or harder?
On the page, Step 6: write your answer to question 4 in one or two sentences.
People who negotiate to solve a problem together tend to say more information would have made it easier; people who negotiate to win tend to say harder. If time allows: "Round 2" on deal.html opens both sets of instructions; pairs renegotiate and record "Round 2".
