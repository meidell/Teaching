Technology in education · a demonstration

One course, front and back

Every course on janerikmeidell.com is a set of hand-written web pages — lessons with labs, homework that checks itself, a glossary that grows every week — and one small database behind them that remembers what each student did. This demo opens both doors on one course: what the student sees, and what the instructor sees the next morning.

The course is OMBAFR455 · Sustainable Financial Markets (SUMAS Online MBA, ten weeks), in a demonstration copy. The cohort of twenty is fictional, generated for the talk, and frozen on Tuesday 8 September 2026 — the morning the final exam opens.

Front end

What the student sees

A course hub, one lesson page a week, homework, a glossary — and a few things that only work because there is a database behind the page.

  • The hub — the course as a map: week cards, key dates, the grade weights, a progress bar that fills as the weeks are done.
  • A week page — six screens: the hook, the ideas, labs that compute (WACI, the step-up, the greenium), a checkpoint quiz with explanations, and "where this goes next". Every section done is remembered.
  • Homework — 20-odd problems a week; the page checks the answer, gives a hint, and unlocks the worked solution after an honest attempt.
  • One sign-in, any device — a name and a password; the phone and the laptop show the same progress.
  • Messages — the 💬 button at the bottom right of every page: the instructor, a group, the whole class.
  • Weekly feedback new — four ratings and two sentences, two minutes a week. Where the board on the right gets its material.

Back end

What the instructor sees

One password opens every course. The dashboard answers three questions in order: what do I do today, what shape is the class in, who needs a name.

  • Today — the ranked list: who has not handed in, who is waiting for an answer, who went quiet, the question most of the class got wrong.
  • The cohort — when each student was last here, how far through they are (a distribution, not an average), where the course loses them, the worst-answered checkpoint questions.
  • The roster — one table, a drawer per student: every module, every quiz score, their feedback, their messages.
  • Feedback board new — the weekly ratings as statistics with a trend, and the comments as cards, anonymous by default; a room-screen version to discuss with the class; notes for the next session.
  • Messages · Handed in — threads waiting for an answer; the Apple note, who handed it in, who was late, who sent the draft.

Two things built for this demonstration
Feedback board

Discussing feedback with the class

Students rate each week on four scales — clear, pace, workload, useful — and write two sentences. The dashboard turns that into statistics (Week 5 is the low point on pace and workload — the week of the online test) and the room screen turns it into a conversation: the numbers first, then the comments behind a curtain, revealed when the class is ready, never a name.

Final exam

A suggested exam, run on the site

The real course sits its final on Moodle. This is the same exam on the site: a cover with the rules, a 90-minute clock, 20 multiple-choice questions and three lab-style calculations marked on submit, and one case memo on Rhône Cement marked by hand against a rubric. Open book; one attempt; autosave. The instructor's marking scheme is on the same page.

How to run the demo in a talk · 8 minutes
Start on the hub as a student.Scroll the week cards and the grade tiles; open Week 5, step through two sections and the WACI-style lab; answer one checkpoint question and read the explanation.
Give feedback for Week 5.Four taps and one sentence — show how little it asks. (On this copy it is saved under the demo course, never the real cohort.)
Open the dashboard with the one password.Read "Today" aloud: six missing hand-ins and three late ones, three messages waiting, three gone quiet, the worst-answered question, and the feedback reading for Week 5.
Show the cohort views, then one student.The last-seen bars, the progress distribution, the stall points — then click a name and show the drawer: modules, quiz scores, feedback, messages.
Put the feedback board on the screen.Week 5: the four meters, then Reveal — the comments appear, anonymous. This is the moment to discuss what students said and what changes next year.
End on the exam.Start it, answer two questions, submit: Parts A and B are marked instantly; Part C goes to the instructor with the rubric. Then the question for the room: what should a platform do, and what should it leave to the teacher?
The ideas the site works with — and which ones this demo shows
Pages, not a platformHand-written HTML, no framework, no build step: every course is deliberately bespoke; what is shared is shared once.
Progress follows the studentSaved on the device first, sent to the database once they say who they are; nothing is lost by signing in late.
Relative flags, not thresholds"Behind" means well under this cohort's median at this point in the course — a fixed cut-off once flagged two thirds of a class.
Distributions, not averagesAn average hides the two who never started. Every cohort view is a histogram.
The question, not the scorePer-question quiz statistics tell the teacher what to re-explain; the score alone does not.
Anonymous on the screen, named in the drawerThe class sees its own answers without names; the instructor can still help one person.
Presence registerCampus courses: a button that turns red for a few minutes in the room, 20 % of the grade pro rata. Not in this online course.
Solutions released by the instructorCampus courses: worked solutions unlock in the room, per exercise, so nobody races ahead. Not in this demo.
Walls, forums, team reportsOther courses: sealed predictions on a shared wall, forums with deadlines, one report written by a team on the site.
Bilingual from one sourceFrench courses generate their English twin from a translation table; one engine, two editions.
Each day as a PDF · a native appEvery session page prints to a PDF; the Insights app wraps the live site with push notifications.
What is real and what is not. The course pages are the real OMBAFR455 material, in a copy with its own database namespace (demo) — try the sign-in, the quiz, the feedback page; nothing reaches the real cohort. The dashboard, the board and the exam read a generated dataset (demo/data.js): twenty invented students, their progress, quiz picks, feedback and messages. The real dashboards read the database and open only with the instructor's password.
