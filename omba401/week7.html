Week 7 · 17–23 August · Syllabus topic 9 · Doane ch. 12–13

Regression: from which group, to how much

Every test so far has taken a category — this branch or that one — and asked whether it moves the outcome. This week the input becomes a number, and the question becomes the one management actually asks: how much? Café Léman is looking at a thirteenth site with 3,000 people a day walking past the door, and the board wants a revenue figure in the paper. You are about to give them one — and, more importantly, to give them the right size of uncertainty around it. Plan 4–5 hours: about two for this lesson and its labs, then 2–3 for the homework problems linked below.

By the end of Week 7 you will be able to

  • Fit a least-squares line by hand from five sums, and say why "least squares" means what it says.
  • Interpret a slope and an intercept in francs — and know when the intercept is meaningless.
  • Tell r from R², and read the standard error of the estimate as a typical miss.
  • Read a residual plot for the structure your model has not captured.
  • Test whether a slope is real with a t-test, and put a confidence interval on it.
  • Distinguish a confidence interval for the mean from a prediction interval for one new café — and know which one the board needs.
  • Fit a multiple regression, use dummy variables, and explain why adjusted R² exists.

Section 7.1 · The Hook

A number for the board paper

Your decision

Café Léman is looking at a thirteenth site.

The property team has found a unit with 3,000 people a day walking past the door. The board meets on Thursday and wants one line in the paper: what daily revenue should we expect?

You have the twelve existing cafés — footfall past the door, and mean daily revenue, for the same month. The relationship is obvious to the eye. The question is what you are entitled to promise.

Place your bet — then lock it

Which of these belongs in the board paper?

Pick your answer.
Locked. The point estimate really is about CHF 7,418 — you will compute it in §7.2 — and the model is a good one: footfall explains 95% of the variation in revenue across these twelve cafés. But the interval depends entirely on what you are predicting. If the board asks "what do cafés at 3,000 footfall average?", the answer is ± CHF 448. If it asks "what will this one café make?" — which is what it is actually asking — the honest answer is ± CHF 1,313, nearly three times wider. Both come out of the same regression, and mixing them up is the most expensive mistake in this week. §7.4 shows you why they differ.
The shape of the week

It is the same machine as last week.

Week 6 ended with a promise: ANOVA and regression are the same engine. Both split total variation into a part your model explains and a part it does not, and both compare the two with an F. The only thing changing is what goes in the input column.

WeekInputQuestionAnswer looks like
5two groupsDo they differ?yes / no, plus a gap
6k groupsDo any of them differ?yes / no, plus which pairs

Four moves this week:

  • Fit the line — least squares, and what the two coefficients mean in money (§7.2).
  • Judge the fit — r, R², the typical miss, and the residual plot that tells you what you left out (§7.3).
  • Test it — is the slope real, and how wide is the honest interval around a prediction (§7.4).
  • Add drivers — multiple regression, dummy variables, and why R² alone will lie to you (§7.5).

A regression that predicts well and a regression you understand are different achievements. The board needs the first. Your job needs the second.

Section 7.2 · The Line

Least squares, and what the coefficients buy you

The data

Twelve cafés, one month

Footfall is people counted past the door on an average day. Revenue is mean daily takings. Everything this week comes out of these twenty-four numbers.

Five sums are all you need for the line — and you should compute them once, by hand, because the exam will ask you to:

SSxx = Σx² − (Σx)²/n   ·   SSxy = Σxy − (Σx)(Σy)/n   ·   SSyy = Σy² − (Σy)²/n
b1 = SSxy / SSxx   ·   b0 = ȳ − b1x̄
Lab · why "least squares"

Move the line and watch the penalty

Drag the two sliders to place any line you like. The vertical grey sticks are the residuals — what the line misses at each café. The number that matters is SSE, the sum of those misses squared. Least squares is nothing more mysterious than the line that makes SSE as small as it will go. Try to beat it.

Reading the answer

Two numbers, two very different sentences.

revenuê = 1,106.54 + 2.1038 × footfall

The slope is the whole point. b1 = 2.1038 means: one extra person past the door per day is associated with about CHF 2.10 more daily revenue. Not CHF 2.10 of profit, and not "causes" — associated with. But it is a sentence a property director can act on: a site with 500 more passers-by is worth roughly CHF 1,050 a day, or about CHF 380,000 a year, in extra revenue. That is how you decide what rent is worth paying.

The intercept is usually a trap. b0 = 1,106.54 is, literally, predicted revenue at zero footfall — a café nobody walks past, taking CHF 1,107 a day. That is nonsense, and it is nonsense for a specific reason: x = 0 is far outside the range of the data, which runs from 950 to 4,200. The intercept is doing an arithmetic job, positioning the line vertically. Interpret it only when zero is a real, observed possibility.

Say which is which. "Associated with" is not pedantry; it is the difference between a defensible sentence and one a colleague can demolish. These twelve cafés were not randomly assigned their footfall — busy sites cost more rent, attract different formats and open longer hours. Any of that could be doing the work.

Section 7.3 · The Fit

r, R², and the misses

Three numbers for "how good"

They answer different questions. Do not swap them.

StatisticHereWhat it tells you
r — correlation+0.9749Direction and tightness, on a −1 to +1 scale. Unit-free. Says nothing about steepness.
R² — determination0.9503The share of the variation in revenue the line accounts for: 95.0%. In simple regression it is just r squared.

That is the Week 6 table with different row labels. SSR is last week's "between", SSE is last week's "within". Same split, same F, same logic — the input just happens to be continuous.

R² = 95% is unusually high, and you should be suspicious of your own delight. Footfall is very close to a direct driver of café revenue, so this is close to the best case. Most business regressions land between 0.2 and 0.6, and a model with R² = 0.35 can still be extremely useful. Next week's time-series data will bring you back down to earth.
Lab · the residual plot

The graph that tells you what you left out

A residual is what the line missed: e = y − ŷ. Plotted against the fitted value, a good model shows a shapeless band around zero. Anything else is information you have not used yet. Switch the colouring on and see what these residuals are hiding.

What to look for, always, in this order: a curve (your relationship is not linear) · a funnel (the spread grows with the fitted value — heteroscedasticity) · an outlier (one point dominating the fit) · and any pattern that lines up with a variable you left out. The fourth is the one you just found, and it is what §7.5 is for.

Section 7.4 · Inference

Is the slope real, and how wide is the promise?

Testing the slope

The same t-test you have run since Week 5.

Your twelve cafés are a sample. The slope you computed, b1 = 2.1038, is an estimate of a true slope β1 you cannot see. The question is whether the true slope could be zero — that is, whether footfall has any relationship with revenue at all.

H0: β1 = 0   ·   H1: β1 ≠ 0
t = b1 / sb1   on   df = n − 2
QuantityValue
b12.103846
sb1 — standard error of the slope0.152077
t = 2.103846 / 0.15207713.834
df = 12 − 210
t critical, two-tail, α = 0.052.228
p-value0.00000008

13.83 against a critical value of 2.23. Reject H0 comfortably. And a 95% confidence interval for the true slope:

2.1038 ± 2.228 × 0.1521  =  CHF 1.765 to CHF 2.443 per passer-by

Notice how much more useful the interval is than the verdict. "Significant" tells the property director nothing she can price. "Between CHF 1.77 and CHF 2.44 per daily passer-by, with 95% confidence" tells her what a site is worth, and how much she is guessing.

The F-test in the ANOVA table asks the identical question. F = 191.38 on (1, 10), p = 0.00000008 — and 13.834² = 191.38. In simple regression the t-test on the slope and the F-test on the model are the same test written twice. They part company in §7.5, when there is more than one slope to test.
Lab · the two intervals

Predicting the average, and predicting one café

Move the target footfall and watch two bands. The inner one is the confidence interval for the mean — where the average café at that footfall sits. The outer is the prediction interval for one specific new café. The gap between them is the individual café's own randomness, and it never shrinks, however much data you collect.

"Cafés with 3,000 passers-by average between CHF 6,970 and CHF 7,866 a day." Narrow, because averages are stable. Answers a question about a population of sites.

Prediction interval · one café
± CHF 1,313

"This café will take between CHF 6,106 and CHF 8,731 a day." Nearly three times wider, and it is the one the board is actually asking for.

Quoting the confidence interval when the board asked about one new café understates your uncertainty by a factor of three. It is the single most common misuse of a regression output in business.

Watch the bands flare as you slide toward either end. Precision is best at the mean of x (2,200 here) and decays as you move away — which is the mathematical reason extrapolation is dangerous. Push the slider to 4,300 and the interval is already wide; at 8,000 the model would be guessing entirely, and it would do so with a perfectly straight face.

Section 7.5 · Multiple Regression

More than one driver — and the number that lies

The extension

Same idea, more columns.

§7.3's residual plot showed the misses were not random: the cafés the model under-predicted were the ones with the most seats. That is a variable sitting in your spreadsheet doing nothing. Put it in the model.

ŷ = b0 + b1x1 + b2x2 + … + bkxk

Each slope is now a partial effect: the change in revenue for one more unit of that predictor, holding the others constant. That phrase is the whole of multiple regression, and it is what lets you separate two drivers that move together.

Station is a dummy variable — 1 for the three station cafés, 0 for the rest. A dummy's coefficient is the average difference between the two categories, holding everything else fixed. This is also, exactly, how Week 6's ANOVA can be written as a regression.

Three findings, in order of importance

What the full model tells you that the simple one could not.

1 · Seats matter, and they are worth CHF 42.54 a day each. With footfall held constant, an extra seat adds about CHF 42.50 of daily revenue (t = 5.22, p = 0.0008). Twenty more seats is roughly CHF 850 a day, or CHF 310,000 a year. That is a fit-out decision the simple model was silent about — and it was hiding in the residuals all along.

2 · "Station" stops mattering. On its own you would swear a station location drives revenue. In the full model its coefficient is −290.87 with p = 0.32 — indistinguishable from zero. Why? Because station location works through footfall. Once you know how many people walk past, knowing it is a station tells you nothing more. The variable was never a driver; it was a proxy for one.

3 · R² will never tell you to stop. Add "metres to the lake", a variable with no business logic whatsoever, and R² rises from 0.9916 to 0.9919. It always does — a new column can only ever explain more, even if it is noise. Adjusted R² falls, from 0.9884 to 0.9873, because it charges you for the extra parameter. That is what it is for.

R̄² = 1 − (1 − R²) · (n − 1) / (n − k − 1)
The discipline this implies. Never justify a variable by what it does to R². Justify it by a mechanism you can state in a sentence, then check whether its coefficient is significant and its sign is the one you expected. A predictor that improves the fit while pointing the wrong way is telling you something is wrong with the model, not with reality.

Take it outside Café Léman

Two columns of numbers you have always assumed are related.

Not graded, nobody sees it. Regression is the easiest technique in this course to find a real use for, because almost every job has a pair of variables somebody talks about as if the relationship were established.

Pick whichever one you already have the data for

  1. Spend and result at work. Marketing spend against leads, headcount against output, discount against volume, training hours against error rate. Fit the line, and state the slope in your own units — "each extra CHF 1,000 of spend is associated with 4.2 more leads". That sentence is the deliverable.
  2. Something about you. Sleep against a next-day measure, commute time against departure time, steps against anything. Twenty-odd observations is plenty. The lesson is usually how much scatter there is around a relationship you were certain about.
  3. A published claim. Find a chart in the press asserting a relationship. Get the underlying numbers if you can, and ask: what is R²? What would the prediction interval be? Most published trend lines would embarrass their authors if a prediction interval were drawn on them.

Then the four questions that matter more than the arithmetic: what does the residual plot look like? · what is se in your units, and is a typical miss of that size tolerable? · is the intercept meaningful, or is x = 0 outside your data? · and the one that will save you at some point: would you defend this as causal, and if so, on what grounds other than the regression?

Bring whatever you find to Forum 2, which closes Sunday 6 September — including the ones where the slope turned out not to be significant. "I was sure these were related and they are not" is a better post than a tidy result.

Your Excel toolkit

The whole week, as a formula.

Two routes again: single functions for one number at a time, or the ToolPak for the entire output at once. Learn both — the exam asks for the first, work will want the second.

Everything at once, as an array=LINEST(y_rng, x_rng, TRUE, TRUE) — coefficients, standard errors, R², F
Multiple regression=LINEST(y_rng, x1:xk_rng, TRUE, TRUE) — predictors in adjacent columns
t critical for the slope=T.INV.2T(0.05, n−2)
p-value from t=T.DIST.2T(ABS(t), n−2)
Adjusted R²=1−(1−R2)*(n−1)/(n−k−1)

Watch out for three things. LINEST returns its coefficients backwards — the last predictor's slope comes first and the intercept comes last. The ToolPak's "Significance F" is the model p-value, and in simple regression it equals the slope's p-value exactly. And the ToolPak gives you a confidence interval for each coefficient, never a prediction interval for a new observation — that one you build yourself, from se and the leverage term in §7.4.

Where this goes next

From cross-section to time.

Every regression this week compared twelve different cafés at one moment. Nothing in it knew about yesterday. But most business data arrives as one thing measured repeatedly — monthly revenue, quarterly demand, daily footfall — and that changes the problem in two ways.

Week 8 is time-series analysis. First, the observations are no longer independent: this month looks like last month, which breaks an assumption you have relied on since Week 4. Second, the variation splits into pieces with names — trend, season and noise — and separating them is most of the work. You will find that a trend fitted to raw monthly data can say almost nothing, while the same data deseasonalised says a great deal.

The tool is still a line fitted by least squares. What changes is what you fit it to, and how much you are allowed to believe it.
