The brief

Your situation

You are still Head of Operations at Café Léman — twelve cafés around Lake Geneva, about 21,000 loyalty members, and a sustainability promise the board takes seriously.

Eighteen months ago the board approved a reusable-cup programme: members who sign up get a branded cup and a 50-rappen discount on every drink they bring it for. It has been running since November 2025 in four branches — Nyon, Vevey, Morges and Rolle — while the other eight carry on as before.

The board meets in September to decide whether to roll the programme out to all twelve cafés. Rollout costs roughly CHF 340,000 in year one (cups, point-of-sale changes, staff training, the discount itself). The CFO is unconvinced. The Head of Marketing is certain it works. Neither has looked properly at the numbers.

You have. Write the paper the board will read.

The question you must answer, in one sentence: on the evidence available, should Café Léman extend the reusable-cup programme to all twelve branches — and how confident can the board be in that answer?

What to write

Five parts — and the weeks each one needs

Write it as a board report, not an exam answer: numbered sections, a short executive summary at the front, tables and charts where they carry the argument. The word counts are guidance, not rules — the total is what is marked.

1 · The question and the data~250 wordsWeeks 1–2

State the decision, then describe what you are working with before you do anything to it.

  • Which variables are nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio — and what that permits you to compute.
  • The sampling frame, and who it misses. Name the bias.
  • A compact descriptive summary of both datasets. Where a distribution is skewed, say which measure of centre you will use and why.
2 · Who joins, and who doesn't~400 wordsWeeks 4 & 9

The board's first question will be "how many people actually want this?"

  • A confidence interval for the proportion of members who have joined. State the interval, not just the point estimate, and say in plain words what the interval means.
  • A chi-square test of independence for segment against joined_cup. Check the expected-count condition before you report the statistic.
  • Report an effect size, and turn it into a sentence about who the programme is really for.
3 · Does joining change what people spend?~500 wordsWeeks 5, 6 & 7

This is the section that decides your grade. Do it in the order below and show your working at each step.

  • First, the obvious comparison: a two-sample test of average spend, joiners against non-joiners. Report it honestly, whatever it says.
  • Then ask who joins. Use ANOVA across the four segments to establish that segments differ in spend, and a post-hoc test to say which.
  • Then a multiple regression of spend on visit frequency, segment and joining. Compare the joining coefficient with the raw difference from your first test.
  • Explain the discrepancy to a board member who does not know what a confounder is.
4 · Is the pilot visible in the branch numbers?~450 wordsWeeks 5 & 7

The survey asks members what they do. The panel shows what the tills recorded. They must be reconciled.

  • Compare the four pilot branches before and after November 2025.
  • Then do the same for the eight branches that never ran the pilot, over the same months — and explain why that comparison changes what the first one means.
  • Estimate the effect once more with a regression that controls for what else moved. Report the three estimates side by side and say which you would put in front of the board, and why they differ.
Three defensible methods will give you three different numbers. That is not a failure of the analysis — it is the analysis. A board report that gives one number and hides the other two is worse than one that gives a range and explains it.
5 · The forecast and the recommendation~450 wordsWeek 8

Close the loop: what happens next, and what should the board do.

  • Take chain-wide monthly revenue. Fit a trend to the raw series, then decompose it — seasonal indices from a centred moving average — and fit the trend again. Report both, and explain why they disagree.
  • Forecast September, October and November 2026.
  • Recommend: roll out, don't, or extend the pilot — with the CHF 340,000 in view. Give the board an expected annual effect in francs, a range around it, and the one piece of evidence that would change your mind.
Timing note. Parts 1–3 use material through Week 7. Part 4 needs Week 7, Part 5 needs Week 8. You can write Parts 1 and 2 as soon as Week 4 is behind you — and you should. Nothing in this essay requires Week 9 except the chi-square in Part 2, which Week 9 covers.
Marking

How this is graded

100 points, scaled to 25% of the course.

CriterionPtsWhat earns the marks
Method choice & execution30 The right test for the question, correctly computed. Statistics, degrees of freedom, critical values and p-values all reported. Arithmetic that reproduces from your workbook.
Assumptions & limitations20 Conditions checked before conclusions drawn — independence, normality or sample size, expected counts. Sampling bias named. What the data cannot tell you, said out loud.
Business interpretation25 Every finding converted into francs, customers or risk. A board member who skipped statistics can follow the argument. No sentence ends at "p < 0.05".
Evidence & reproducibility15 Tables and charts that carry the argument rather than decorate it, each labelled and referenced in the text. A workbook where every number in the report can be found.
Structure & clarity10 Executive summary, numbered sections, within the word count, sources cited.
Total100—

What separates a distinction

  • The confounding in Part 3 is found, quantified and explained — not just mentioned.
  • The three estimates in Part 4 are reconciled rather than averaged.
  • The recommendation carries a number and an honest uncertainty, and names the evidence that would overturn it.
  • Somewhere in the essay you say clearly what you would have measured instead, had you designed the pilot yourself.

What loses marks every time

  • Reporting a p-value with no effect size, or an effect size with no units.
  • Concluding causation from the survey. It is observational data and nothing in it was randomised.
  • Charts pasted without a sentence saying what to look at.
  • A conclusion that does not answer the board's question with a yes, a no, or a specific condition.
