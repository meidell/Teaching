Week 8 · 24–30 August · Syllabus topic 10 · Doane ch. 14

Time series: trend, season and noise

Every technique so far has assumed your observations are independent — that knowing one tells you nothing about the next. Monthly business data breaks that assumption on the first row: this August looks like last August, and every August looks like nothing else. The Nyon manager has just seen revenue fall 20% since May and wants to know what has gone wrong. Nothing has gone wrong. This week is about being able to prove that — and then about forecasting what happens next, with an honest account of how wrong you are likely to be. Plan 4–5 hours: about two for this lesson and its labs, then 2–3 for the homework problems linked below.

By the end of Week 8 you will be able to

  • Name the four components of a time series and say which of them you can actually measure.
  • Write the multiplicative model and explain why business data usually needs it rather than the additive one.
  • Compute a centred moving average and say why a 12-month window is the one that removes seasonality.
  • Build seasonal indices from the ratio-to-moving-average, and normalise them so they average 1.
  • Deseasonalise a series, fit a trend to it, and explain why the trend on the raw data was almost meaningless.
  • Reseasonalise a forecast, and produce a monthly figure someone can put in a budget.
  • Measure forecast error with MAD, MSE and MAPE, and beat a naive benchmark — or admit you did not.

Section 8.1 · The Hook

"Revenue is collapsing"

Your decision

The Nyon manager wants to know what has gone wrong.

She has pulled her own monthly revenue for the last three years and sent you the last four months of it, with a one-line message: "We have lost a fifth of the business since May. What is happening?"

She is right about the arithmetic. From May 2026 to August 2026 Nyon's monthly revenue fell from CHF 211,500 to CHF 168,900 — a drop of 20.1%. Four months, one fifth of the business, and no obvious cause.

Place your bet — then lock it

Before you look at anything else. Is the Nyon café shrinking?

Pick your answer.
Locked. Compare the same month a year apart instead of four months in a row: August 2025 was CHF 161,600 and August 2026 is CHF 168,900 — up 4.5%. The café is growing steadily at about CHF 900 a month, and it does so every year while appearing to collapse each summer. Nyon simply empties in July and August: the seasonal index for August is 0.862, meaning a typical August runs 14% below the underlying level, while May runs 8% above it. The manager compared a peak with a trough and read the difference as a trend. This is the single most common misreading of business data, and by §8.4 you will be able to hand her a chart that settles it — and a forecast for the autumn.
The shape of the week

Independence was the assumption. It has just gone.

Weeks 4 to 7 all leaned on the same assumption: each observation is independent of the others. In a time series that is simply false — this month resembles last month, and this August resembles every August. Two consequences follow, and the week deals with them in order.

  • Reading the past. The variation has named parts, and separating them is most of the work (§8.2, §8.3).
  • Predicting the future. Once separated, you can fit a trend that means something, then put the season back to get a usable monthly number (§8.4).
  • Being honest about it. Every forecast is wrong; the question is by how much, and whether you beat a benchmark a five-year-old could produce (§8.5).
What this week does not cover. Exponential smoothing, ARIMA and the rest of modern forecasting sit on top of exactly this decomposition. If you understand trend, season and error here, the machinery in a forecasting package will make sense; if you skip it, that software will produce numbers you cannot defend.

A trend fitted to raw seasonal data is not a small error. On this series it gets the growth rate wrong by 22% and looks like it explains almost nothing.

Section 8.2 · The Components

Four parts, and only two you can pin down

The decomposition

Any business series is treated as a product of four things.

Measurable
T · Trend

The long-run direction, over years. Nyon's is about +CHF 900 a month. Usually fitted with a straight line — the same least squares as Week 7.

Measurable
S · Seasonal

A pattern that repeats on a fixed, known period — 12 months, 4 quarters, 7 days. Nyon's August index is 0.862. This is the part the manager mistook for a trend.

Rarely separable
C · Cyclical

Multi-year swings with no fixed length — the business cycle. You need decades to see one, so with three years of data it is folded into the trend and you say so.

Whatever is left
I · Irregular

Noise: a heatwave, a strike, a road closure. Not forecastable by definition. Its size is your forecast error, which is why §8.5 measures it.

multiplicative:   yt = T × S × C × I    ·    additive:   yt = T + S + C + I

Business data almost always wants the multiplicative model, and for a reason you can see: the seasonal swing at Nyon is proportionally the same each year but absolutely bigger as the café grows. August 2024 was about CHF 25,000 below trend; August 2026 is about CHF 31,000 below it. The percentage is the same both times — 14% — which is exactly what a multiplicative season means.

Use the additive model when the seasonal swing is a constant number of units regardless of level. That is common in physical processes and rare in revenue.

The practical test. Plot the series. If the seasonal peaks and troughs fan out as the level rises, the season is multiplicative. If the band stays the same width, it is additive. Look at the chart in §8.3 and you will see the fan.

Section 8.3 · The Season

The moving average that erases a year

The trick

Average exactly one full cycle and the season cancels.

A 12-month moving average contains one of every month — one January, one August, one December. Whatever the season does, it does all of it inside the window, so the average comes out with the seasonality removed. What is left is trend plus a little noise.

One complication: an even-numbered window of 12 sits between two months rather than on one. The fix is to average two consecutive 12-month averages, which recentres it on a month. That is the centred moving average, and it is why the first and last six months of any monthly series have no CMA.

CMAt = ½ ( MAt−½ + MAt+½ )   ·   ratiot = yt ÷ CMAt
SImonth = average of that month's ratios, then normalised so the twelve average to 1
Lab · watch the window work

Three windows on the same data

The green line is the raw series; the gold line is a moving average of the length you choose. Watch what happens at 12: the seasonal wobble disappears completely and the underlying growth appears. That is not a coincidence — it is the whole reason the window has to match the cycle.

Lab · build the indices

Ratio to moving average, month by month

Each usable month gives a ratio of actual to centred moving average. Average the ratios for each calendar month, normalise so the twelve average to exactly 1, and you have the seasonal profile of the business.

Why normalise? The raw averaged ratios will not quite sum to 12, because of noise and because the ends of the series are missing. Multiplying them all by 12/(their sum) forces the seasonal component to be neutral over a full year — it redistributes revenue between months without inventing or destroying any. Skip this step and your annual forecast is systematically too high or too low.

Section 8.4 · The Forecast

Divide, fit, multiply back

Three steps, in this order

Deseasonalise, fit the trend, then put the season back.

1.   dt = yt ÷ SImonth   (deseasonalise)
2.   d̂t = b0 + b1t   (least squares on the deseasonalised series — Week 7)
3.   Ft = d̂t × SImonth   (reseasonalise)

Step 2 is just Week 7 with time on the x-axis. The whole novelty of the week is steps 1 and 3 — and the reason they matter is what happens if you skip them.

Section 8.5 · The Error

Every forecast is wrong. By how much?

Three measures

They disagree on purpose.

MAD = Σ|et| ÷ n   ·   MSE = Σet² ÷ n   ·   MAPE = ( Σ |et| ÷ yt ) ÷ n × 100
MeasureUnitsUse it when
MADthe same as your data (CHF)You want a plain answer: "we are typically out by this much." Treats a big miss and two half-sized misses the same.
MSEsquared units — uninterpretableBig misses are disproportionately costly. Its square root, RMSE, is back in CHF and is what most software reports.
MAPEpercentYou need to compare across series of different sizes — branches, products, regions. The only one of the three that travels.
MAPE's two traps. It is undefined when an actual is zero, and it punishes under-forecasting more heavily than over-forecasting — a forecast of 50 against an actual of 100 gives 50%, while 100 against 50 gives 100%. If your series has near-zero values or you care symmetrically, use MAD.

Be suspicious of this result. A MAPE of 0.5% is far better than real forecasting ever achieves — this is a teaching series built with very little noise so the decomposition is visible. In practice a monthly revenue MAPE of 5% to 15% is respectable, and anything under 3% should make you check for a leak between your training data and your test data. What is realistic here is the comparison: a decomposition model routinely beats the naive benchmark by a factor of several, and if yours does not, the extra complexity is not paying for itself.
The limits

Three things this method cannot do.

  1. It cannot see a turning point. A straight-line trend extrapolated forward assumes the future continues the past. Every recession in history was missed by a trend line, and the method gives no warning — the arithmetic is just as confident either way.
  2. It assumes the seasonal pattern is stable. Nyon's August index of 0.862 comes from two Augusts. If the town changes — a new employer, a festival, remote work — the profile shifts and your indices are describing a business that no longer exists. Recompute them every year.
  3. It has no confidence interval attached. Week 7 gave you prediction intervals because the observations were independent; here they are not, so the honest way to express uncertainty is to hold out data, measure the error you actually made, and quote that. Which is what §8.5 just did.

The forecast is the easy half. The defensible half is the sentence that comes after it: "and on the last twelve months, this method was out by an average of X."

Take it outside Café Léman

One seasonal series that somebody at work misreads every year.

Not graded, nobody sees it. This is the most immediately useful week of the course, because the mistake it fixes is made in almost every monthly management pack ever written.

Pick whichever one you already have the data for

  1. Monthly revenue, volume or headcount at work. Three years is ideal, two will do. Compute the twelve indices and show them to whoever writes the monthly commentary. The sentence "we are down 12% on last month" is almost always a seasonal index in disguise.
  2. Something with a weekly cycle. Daily orders, tickets, footfall, support calls. Same method, period 7 instead of 12 — a 7-day moving average needs no centring, because 7 is odd. Mondays and Fridays will surprise you.
  3. Your own household. Two or three years of electricity, heating or grocery spend. Deseasonalise it and see whether the efficiency measure you installed actually did anything, or whether you just measured it in a warm month.

Then the three questions that matter more than the arithmetic: do your indices average 1 after normalising? · does the fan test say multiplicative or additive? · and the one that will change a meeting: what does the year-on-year comparison say, and does anyone in your organisation currently look at it that way?

Bring whatever you find to Forum 2, which closes Sunday 30 August. The raw chart and the deseasonalised chart side by side is the single most persuasive thing you can post.

Your Excel toolkit

The whole week, as a formula.

Decomposition is one of the few things Excel has no single button for — you build it in columns, which is a virtue: every step stays visible.

StepExcel
12-month moving average (uncentred)=AVERAGE(B2:B13), dragged down
Centred moving average=AVERAGE(C7:C8) — the mean of two consecutive 12-month averages
Ratio to moving average=B8/D8
Average ratio for one month=AVERAGEIF(month_col, 8, ratio_col)
Normalise the twelve indices=raw_index * 12 / SUM(all_raw_indices)
Deseasonalise=B2/INDEX(SI_range, MONTH(A2))
Trend on the deseasonalised series=SLOPE(deseas, t) and =INTERCEPT(deseas, t)
Reseasonalised forecast=(intercept + slope*t) * INDEX(SI_range, month)
MAD · MSE=AVERAGE(ABS(actual−forecast))  ·  =SUMXMY2(actual,forecast)/n
MAPE=AVERAGE(ABS((actual−forecast)/actual))*100
Quick chart of the seasonPlot the twelve indices as a column chart with a line at 1.0

Two warnings. The MAD and MAPE formulas above are array formulas in older Excel — confirm with Ctrl+Shift+Enter, or use SUMPRODUCT instead. And Excel's own FORECAST.ETS will do exponential smoothing with seasonality in one call: useful at work, useless in the exam, and it will not show you the indices, which are usually the most interesting output.

Where this goes next

From numbers to categories.

Every technique in this course so far has needed a number for its outcome — a mean, a proportion, a slope, a forecast. But a great deal of business data is not numeric at all. Which of four packages did the customer choose? Did the complaint get resolved, escalated or dropped? Is the defect on line A, B or C?

Week 9 is chi-square, the test for counts in categories. It answers two questions: does what I observed match what I expected, and are these two categorical variables related at all? It also completes the final essay — Part 2 of the brief needs exactly this test.
