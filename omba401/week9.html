Week 9 · 31 August – 6 September · Syllabus topic 11 · Doane ch. 15–16

Counts in categories: chi-square

Every technique so far has needed a number for its outcome — a mean, a proportion, a slope, a forecast. But a great deal of what a business records is not numeric at all. Which drink did they order? How did they pay? Which of three outcomes did the complaint reach? This week you test claims about counts in categories, using one statistic that does two quite different jobs — and then, when even that is too demanding, you meet the tests that assume almost nothing at all. It is the last new technique of the course. Plan 4–5 hours: about two for this lesson and its labs, then 2–3 for the homework problems linked below.

By the end of Week 9 you will be able to

  • Run a goodness-of-fit test: does an observed distribution match a claimed one?
  • Run a test of independence on a contingency table, and get the degrees of freedom right.
  • Compute expected counts from row and column totals, and explain why they are what they are.
  • Check the conditions, and know what to do when an expected count falls below 5.
  • Find which cells drive a significant result, instead of stopping at the p-value.
  • Report Cramér's V, and explain why a chi-square of 64 can still be a small effect.
  • Choose a nonparametric test — sign, Wilcoxon, Mann-Whitney, Kruskal-Wallis — and say what it costs you.

Section 9.1 · The Hook

The supply contract

Your decision

Head office is about to buy a year of coffee on the wrong assumption.

Procurement is signing a twelve-month supply contract, and it has built the order on the national Swiss café mix: 30% espresso, 25% latte and cappuccino, 20% filter, 15% cold, 10% other. Nyon's own till data for the last month — 600 drinks — is on the table in front of you.

Cold brew looks high. But tills are noisy, 600 is not a large sample, and "looks high" has been wrong before in this course. Procurement wants an answer today.

Place your bet — then lock it

Is Nyon's drink mix genuinely different from the assumed national mix?

Pick your answer.
Locked. The mix is emphatically different — you will get χ² = 30.96 on 4 degrees of freedom against a critical value of 9.49, p ≈ 0.000003. But the more useful finding is where it comes from: cold brew alone contributes 25.60 of the 30.96, which is 83% of the whole statistic. Nyon sells cold drinks at 23% of volume where the contract assumes 15%. Espresso is also a little low, and the remaining categories are unremarkable. A test that stops at "significant" tells procurement nothing; a test that names the cell tells them exactly which line of the order to change. Reading the cell contributions is the difference between a p-value and a decision, and §9.2 shows you how.
The shape of the week

One statistic, two jobs, and a safety net.

Chi-square measures the same thing in every application: the gap between what you counted and what you would have expected, scaled so that a gap of 10 in a cell expecting 20 counts for far more than a gap of 10 in a cell expecting 2,000.

χ² = Σ (O − E)² ÷ E
  • Goodness of fit — one categorical variable against a claimed distribution. df = k − 1 (§9.2).
  • Test of independence — two categorical variables against each other. df = (r − 1)(c − 1) (§9.3).
  • The conditions and the effect size — when you are allowed to believe it, and how much it matters (§9.4).
  • Nonparametric tests — what to use when even a t-test's assumptions are out of reach (§9.5).
A note on where this sits. Chi-square is always right-tailed: only a large statistic is evidence against H₀, because (O − E)² cannot be negative. There is no such thing as a "significantly small" chi-square — though an unnaturally small one, in the right context, has occasionally been evidence that data was fabricated.

A significant chi-square says "these counts are not what you assumed". It never says which assumption was wrong, or in which direction, or whether it matters. All three of those are your job.

Section 9.2 · Goodness of Fit

Does the mix match the claim?

The test

One variable, one claimed distribution.

The hypotheses are about the whole distribution, not about any single category:

H0: the mix is 30 / 25 / 20 / 15 / 10   ·   H1: it is not
Ei = n × pi   ·   χ² = Σ (Oi − Ei)² ÷ Ei   ·   df = k − 1

Why k − 1? Because once you know four of the five counts and the total, the fifth is forced. One constraint, one degree of freedom gone. That logic recurs all week.

Lab · which cell is doing the work?

The contribution chart

Each bar is one category's (O − E)² ÷ E. Chi-square is nothing more than their sum, so this chart is the test statistic, taken apart. A well-written conclusion names the tallest bar.

How to say it in a report. "The drink mix at Nyon differs significantly from the assumed national mix (χ² = 30.96, df = 4, p < 0.001). The difference is driven almost entirely by cold drinks, which are 23% of volume against an assumed 15%; this single category accounts for 83% of the test statistic. We recommend the cold-drink line of the supply contract be revised upward and the espresso line reviewed." Notice that the statistics occupy one bracket and the rest is business.

Section 9.3 · Independence

Does how they pay depend on where they are?

The decision

One kiosk design, or three?

Café Léman is specifying self-service kiosks. A single design is far cheaper — but only if payment behaviour is the same everywhere. Operations sampled 1,000 transactions across the three branch formats.

H0: payment method and branch format are independent   ·   H1: they are associated
Eij = (row total × column total) ÷ n   ·   df = (r − 1)(c − 1)

Where the expected counts come from. If the two variables really were independent, every format would show the same payment split — the overall one. So the expected count in a cell is just that row's size × the overall proportion for that column, which rearranges to (row × column) ÷ n. Nothing more mysterious than that.

And df = (r − 1)(c − 1) = 4 here: fix two cells in each row and two in each column, and the rest of the table is determined by the margins.

Section 9.4 · Conditions & Effect Size

Significant, and how much does it matter?

The conditions

Three things to check before you quote the p-value.

  1. Counts, not percentages. Chi-square must be computed on raw frequencies. Feeding it percentages is the single commonest error, and it silently rescales the statistic to whatever your denominator happened to be — usually 100, which makes everything non-significant.
  2. Independent observations. One row per transaction, person or item, each appearing once. Ask the same customer twice and the test is invalid in a way no amount of data fixes.
  3. Expected counts large enough. The usual rule: all expected counts ≥ 5, or more leniently, at least 80% of cells ≥ 5 and none below 1. Note that the condition is on expected counts, never observed ones — a cell can legitimately observe zero.

Our table's smallest expected count is 50.00, so we are comfortably inside the rule. When you are not, the fixes are: combine adjacent categories into a sensible larger one, collect more data, or use Fisher's exact test, which computes the probability directly instead of approximating it.

Why the condition exists at all. χ² is a continuous distribution being used to approximate the behaviour of discrete counts. That approximation is good when the counts are reasonably large and poor when they are tiny — with an expected count of 2, a difference of one or two transactions moves the statistic wildly, and the p-value it produces is fiction.

What chi-square answers
Is it real?

"Could this pattern have arisen by chance?" Depends heavily on n. With enough data, almost any departure from independence becomes significant.

What Cramér's V answers
How strong?

"How tightly are these two variables tied together?" Independent of n. Bands: ~0.1 weak, ~0.3 moderate, ~0.5 strong. Ours is 0.179.

V = √( χ² ÷ ( n × min(r − 1, c − 1) ) )

Our association is significant at p ≈ 4 × 10⁻¹³ and weak at V = 0.18. Both statements are true, and only reporting the first one is how a trivial finding gets into a strategy deck.

Section 9.5 · Nonparametric Tests

When you cannot assume a distribution

The idea

Throw away the numbers, keep the order.

Every test from Week 5 onward assumed something about the shape of the data — usually normality, or a large enough sample for the central limit theorem to rescue you. Sometimes you have neither: n is small, the data are badly skewed, there is an extreme outlier, or the measurements are ordinal — a 1-to-5 satisfaction rating, where the gap between 4 and 5 is not the same size as the gap between 1 and 2.

Nonparametric tests solve this by discarding the raw values and working with signs and ranks instead. An outlier of 900 becomes "the largest", worth exactly one rank more than the second largest. That is what makes them robust — and it is also exactly what they cost you.

Paired · simplest
Sign test

Counts how many differences are positive and how many negative, and tests that against a coin flip. Uses only the direction. Replaces the paired t-test.

Paired · stronger
Wilcoxon signed-rank

Ranks the differences by size, then sums the ranks of the positives and negatives. Uses direction and magnitude order. Replaces the paired t-test with more power than the sign test.

Two independent groups
Mann-Whitney U

Pools both groups, ranks everything, and asks whether one group's ranks sit systematically higher. Replaces the two-sample t-test.

Three or more groups
Kruskal-Wallis H

The same idea across k groups. Replaces one-way ANOVA from Week 6, and its statistic is compared against — of all things — a chi-square distribution with k − 1 df.

The price. If the data really are normal, a nonparametric test is roughly 95% as powerful as its parametric twin — you need about 5% more data for the same chance of detecting a real effect. That is a small premium for not having to defend a distributional assumption. If the data are not normal, the nonparametric test is often more powerful, so the premium can be negative.
Lab · did the kiosk speed up service?

Twelve branches, before and after

Mean service time in seconds, before and after the self-service kiosk went in. Twelve paired observations, one clear reversal, and a sample far too small to argue about normality. Toggle between the two tests and see what ranking buys you.

Report the median, not the mean. If you were not willing to assume a distribution for the test, you should not summarise with a mean either. The median difference here is −16 seconds; the mean is −19.6, dragged down by the three big station branches. The median is the honest headline, and it is the one that matches the test you ran.

Take it outside Café Léman

One cross-tabulation from your own work.

Not graded, nobody sees it. Categorical data is the easiest kind to find, because almost every system you use records categories and almost nobody tests them.

Pick whichever one you already have the data for

  1. Any two categorical fields in a system at work. Channel × outcome, region × product, shift × defect type, source × conversion. Build the contingency table, run the test, and — this is the part that matters — report the row percentages and the largest cell contribution, not just the p-value.
  2. A claimed distribution. Somebody in your organisation has asserted a mix: "roughly 60/30/10". Count a month of reality against it with a goodness-of-fit test. These claims are very often years out of date, and nobody has ever checked.
  3. A small before-and-after. Fewer than twenty paired observations, of anything. Too few to assume normality — so run a sign test and a Wilcoxon and compare what each one concludes.

Then the four questions that matter more than the arithmetic: were all your expected counts at least 5? · which cell contributed most to the statistic? · what is Cramér's V, and would you call that a strong relationship in your business? · and the one that should decide whether you act: is the effect big enough to change anything, whatever the p-value says?

Your Excel toolkit

The whole week, as a formula.

Excel's chi-square functions are unusually good — but note the trap in the first row.

TaskExcel
p-value directly from two ranges=CHISQ.TEST(observed_rng, expected_rng) — returns the p-value, not the statistic
Expected count, goodness of fit=n * claimed_proportion
Expected count, contingency table=row_total * col_total / n
One cell's contribution=(O−E)^2/E
The statistic itself=SUMPRODUCT((O_rng−E_rng)^2/E_rng)
p-value from the statistic=CHISQ.DIST.RT(chi2, df)
Critical value=CHISQ.INV.RT(0.05, df)
Cramér's V=SQRT(chi2/(n*MIN(r−1,c−1)))
Building the contingency tableInsert → PivotTable, with counts in the values area
Ranks for a nonparametric test=RANK.AVG(value, range, 1) — AVG, so ties share the average rank
Sign test p-value=2*BINOM.DIST(smaller_count, n, 0.5, TRUE)

Three warnings. CHISQ.TEST returns a p-value, not a statistic — students report it as chi-square every year. It also needs you to have built the expected range yourself, so it saves less work than it appears to. And always use RANK.AVG, never RANK.EQ, for nonparametric work: tied values must share the average of the ranks they span, or your test statistic is wrong.
