Hypothesis tests: is that difference real?

Week 4 gave you an honest range around a single number. This week you answer the question a board actually asks: is this difference real, or is it noise? Café Léman has redesigned its menu and spend is up 80 centimes a head. Rolling it out to all fourteen branches costs CHF 180,000. You have 40 receipts and a decision to make. Plan 4–5 hours: about two for this lesson and its labs, then 2–3 for the homework problems linked below. Online Test 1 also runs this week — see the note below.

By the end of Week 5 you will be able to

  • Turn a business claim into a null and alternative hypothesis, and know which one carries the burden of proof.
  • Compute a test statistic and a p-value for one sample, by hand and in Excel — and say in plain English what that p-value means.
  • Choose one-tailed or two-tailed before you look at the data, and explain why the order matters.
  • Tell a Type I error from a Type II, and use power to say how big a sample a question actually deserved.
  • Separate statistical significance from practical significance — and refuse to confuse a small p-value with a big effect.
  • Run and read a test on two samples: independent groups, paired before-and-after, and two proportions.

Section 5.1 · The Hook

The 80-centime question

Your decision

Do you roll the new menu out to fourteen branches?

Café Léman redesigned its menu at the Nyon branch: bigger photographs, fewer items, the high-margin dishes at eye level. Before the change, average spend per customer ran at CHF 14.80 — you know this well, from six years of till records. Since the change, a sample of 40 customers has averaged CHF 15.60, with a spread of s = CHF 3.50.

An 80-centime lift. Marketing has already written the memo. Rolling the redesign out to all fourteen branches costs CHF 180,000 in printing, photography and staff retraining. The CFO turns to you.

You have seen this movie before. In Week 4, "62% said yes" turned out to mean "somewhere between 50% and 74%". So: is an 80-centime lift, measured on 40 people, evidence that the menu works — or is it the sort of wobble you would get anyway?

Commit — then lock it
Pick your gut answer.
Locked. Here is the uncomfortable arithmetic, which you will do yourself in §5.3: with 40 customers and a spread of CHF 3.50, a sample mean wobbles by about 55 centimes from sample to sample. An 80-centime gap is therefore about 1.4 wobbles — and if the menu had changed nothing at all, you would still see a gap that big or bigger roughly 16% of the time. That is not evidence. It is not evidence of absence either, which is the trap most people fall into next. The fourth answer is the sharpest of the four: the spread is exactly what decides this.
The shape of the week

From an interval to a verdict.

Week 4 asked how big is it? and answered with a range. Week 5 asks is it there at all? and answers yes or no — with a stated risk of being wrong. Four moves:

  • Set up the trial — null and alternative hypotheses, and who carries the burden of proof (§5.2).
  • Weigh the evidence — the test statistic and the p-value, built by simulation before any formula (§5.3).
  • Know how you can be wrong — Type I and Type II errors, α, and power (§5.4).
  • Compare two groups — independent samples, paired samples, and proportions (§5.5).

A hypothesis test never proves anything. It tells you whether your data is surprising enough to act on — and nothing more.

Section 5.2 · The Logic

Innocent until the data says otherwise

The courtroom

Two statements, and an unequal burden of proof.

A hypothesis test is a trial, and the defendant is "nothing is going on". That statement is the null hypothesis, written H₀. It is presumed true. The claim you are hoping to establish — that the menu works, that the branches differ, that the campaign paid off — is the alternative, H₁, and it must earn its place.

H₀ · the null
μ = 14.80
The new menu changed nothing. Average spend is still what it always was. Always contains an equals sign — it is a specific, checkable claim.
H₁ · the alternative
μ ≠ 14.80
Average spend has moved. This is what you would need evidence for. It never contains an equals sign.

The asymmetry is deliberate and it is the whole point. A court does not declare the accused innocent; it fails to convict. In exactly the same way, a test never concludes "H₀ is true" — only reject H₀ or fail to reject H₀. "We found no significant difference" means we could not convict, not we proved there is nothing there.

Why H₀ always has the equals sign. You can only compute "how surprising is my data?" against a specific number. "μ = 14.80" gives you something to simulate. "μ ≠ 14.80" does not — which value would you use? The null is the one you can test because it is precise.
One tail or two — decide before you look

Which way are you willing to be surprised?

If you only care about a rise, you can put all your scepticism in one tail and detect a rise more easily. But that comes at a price: a one-tailed test is blind to a change in the other direction, however large.

Your questionH₀H₁Tails
Has spend changed?μ = 14.80μ ≠ 14.80Two
Has spend risen?μ ≤ 14.80μ > 14.80One (right)
Has waiting time fallen?μ ≥ 8.0μ < 8.0One (left)

The rule that keeps you honest: choose the tails from the question, before the data arrives. Looking at your sample, noticing it went up, and only then deciding to run a one-tailed test is a way of halving your p-value without collecting a single extra observation. It has a name in the literature — p-hacking — and it is the most common quiet fraud in business analytics.

For the menu decision the honest choice is two-tailed. A redesign could plausibly have driven the high-margin dishes down; you would very much want to know that.

Section 5.3 · The Evidence

From a gap to a p-value

Build it before you name it

What would the world look like if the menu did nothing?

Forget formulas for a moment. Suppose H₀ is exactly true: the redesign changed nothing, and average spend is still CHF 14.80. Now sample 40 customers. You will not get 14.80 — you will get 14.72, or 15.11, or 14.53, because 40 people are not the whole population. That wobble is the standard error from Week 4:

SE = s ÷ √n = 3.50 ÷ √40 = CHF 0.553

So in a world where the menu did nothing, sample means of 40 customers pile up around 14.80 with a typical wobble of about 55 centimes. Your job is to find out how unusual 15.60 is in that world. The lab below builds that world, one sample at a time, so you can see the answer before you compute it.

That is the whole idea, and it is worth saying in one sentence: a p-value is the probability of seeing a gap at least as extreme as yours, in a world where H₀ is true. Nothing more mystical than that. The formula below is only a shortcut for the simulation you just ran.

Worked example — the menu decision

Four lines, and a verdict.

Every one-sample test of a mean follows the same four lines. Here they are for Café Léman, with n = 40, x̄ = 15.60, s = 3.50, μ₀ = 14.80, at the conventional α = 0.05:

  1. Hypotheses. H₀: μ = 14.80 · H₁: μ ≠ 14.80 (two-tailed — a redesign could hurt).
  2. Standard error. SE = 3.50 ÷ √40 = 0.553.
  3. Test statistic. t = (15.60 − 14.80) ÷ 0.553 = 1.446, with df = n − 1 = 39.
  4. p-value and decision. =T.DIST.2T(1.446, 39) gives p = 0.156. Since 0.156 > 0.05, we fail to reject H₀.
t = (what you saw − what H₀ claims) ÷ standard error
"how many wobbles away from the null is my result?"

Read the verdict carefully, because this is where careers are made and lost. It does not say the menu doesn't work. It says: with 40 receipts, this data cannot tell the difference between a menu that does nothing and a menu that lifts spend by 80 centimes. The honest memo to the CFO is not "the redesign failed" — it is "we cannot yet tell, and here is what it would take to find out." You will produce that number in §5.4.

Critical-value route, same answer. Some textbooks compare t to a cut-off instead of computing p. At α = 0.05 with df = 39, the two-tailed critical value is =T.INV.2T(0.05,39) = 2.023. Our t of 1.446 does not reach it, so we fail to reject — identically. The p-value simply tells you how far short you fell.

Two things are worth noticing, and they are the source of most bad analytics. Push the mean far enough and any gap becomes significant — obviously. But hold the mean at 15.60 and push n instead: at n = 40 the same 80-centime lift is unconvincing, at n = 150 it is significant, at n = 200 it is overwhelming. The effect never changed. Only the amount of evidence did.

"Significant" is a statement about your sample size at least as much as about the world.

Section 5.4 · The Errors

Four ways to be wrong

The two-by-two everyone should carry

You will be wrong sometimes. The question is which way.

A test makes a binary call about an unknown truth, so there are exactly four outcomes — two right, two wrong. The two wrong ones have unglamorous names and very different business consequences.

H₀ is actually true
(the menu does nothing)
H₀ is actually false
(the menu really works)
You reject H₀
("roll it out")
Type I error · αYou spend CHF 180,000 on a menu that does nothing. A false alarm.
Correct · powerYou caught a real effect. This is the outcome you are paying for.
You fail to reject
("do nothing")
CorrectYou didn't spend money chasing noise.
Type II error · βThe menu genuinely works and you shelved it. A missed opportunity — and nobody ever gets blamed for this one.

α is a choice, not a discovery. Setting α = 0.05 is you declaring, before seeing anything: I accept a 5% chance of a false alarm. It is a business decision about the cost of being wrong in that direction, and it is why medicine uses 0.01 and A/B tests on a button colour happily use 0.10.

β is the one nobody sets — and it is usually the bigger risk in business. Power, defined as 1 − β, is the chance your study finds a real effect if there is one. Most business analyses never compute it, which is how organisations end up concluding "no effect" from studies that could never have detected one.

Set the lift to CHF 0.80 and n to 40 — the study you actually ran. Power: about 30%. Read that plainly: even if the menu genuinely lifts spend by 80 centimes, this study had roughly a seven-in-ten chance of missing it. The failure to reject H₀ in §5.3 tells you almost nothing about the menu, and almost everything about the study.

Now push n to 150. Power crosses 80%, the conventional target. That is the number to put in the memo: not "the redesign failed", but "give me 150 receipts and I can answer this properly."

Note. The curves use the normal approximation, which is what every power calculator does at these sample sizes; with n below about 20 the true t-based power is a percentage point or two lower. The lesson is unaffected.
The four things a p-value is not

Where p-values go to die.

More bad decisions are made by misreading a p-value than by computing one wrongly. All four of these are wrong:

  • "p = 0.03, so there's a 3% chance H₀ is true." No. The p-value is computed assuming H₀ is true. It is the probability of the data given the hypothesis, never the reverse — the same Week 3 trap as P(flagged | stolen) versus P(stolen | flagged).
  • "p = 0.001, so the effect is huge." No. A tiny p-value with a large enough n can accompany an utterly trivial effect. p tells you how confident, never how big. For size, report the effect and its confidence interval.
  • "p = 0.06, so there is no effect." No. 0.06 and 0.04 are almost the same evidence; α = 0.05 is a convention, not a law of nature. "Failed to reject" is not "proved equal".
  • "We tested twenty things and one came out significant." At α = 0.05 you expect one false alarm in twenty tests by construction. Testing until something is significant guarantees a finding and destroys its meaning.

Statistical significance answers "could this be noise?". It never answers "does this matter?" — that one is yours, and it needs the effect size and the CHF 180,000.

Practical significance, in this case. Suppose the lift is real and is exactly 80 centimes. Nyon serves about 190 customers a day, so the lift is worth roughly CHF 152 a day, about CHF 55,000 a year across the branch — and rather more across fourteen. Against CHF 180,000 of one-off cost, that is a decision worth making properly. A p-value cannot tell you any of that.

Section 5.5 · Two Samples

Comparing two groups

Three questions, three tests

Which test? Ask how the two groups are related.

Almost every comparison a business makes is one of three shapes, and picking the wrong one is the single most common error in applied testing. The deciding question is never "what kind of data is it?" — it is "are the two numbers linked to the same unit?"

SituationTestStatistic
Two separate groups. Nyon customers vs Vevey customers — different people.Independent two-sample tt = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) ÷ SE
The same units, twice. The same 40 customers before and after the redesign.Paired tt = d̄ ÷ (s_d ÷ √n)
Two shares. 62% of Nyon regulars vs 48% of Vevey regulars said yes.Two-proportion zz = (p̂₁ − p̂₂) ÷ SE

The paired test is the one people miss, and missing it is expensive. When the same customer appears in both numbers, the difference cancels out everything that makes customers differ from each other — appetite, income, whether they came for lunch or coffee. All that between-customer noise, which is most of the CHF 3.50 spread, simply vanishes. You are left with the change itself.

Worked example — the same lift, tested two ways

Why the design of the study beat the size of the sample.

Here is the most important comparison on this page. The lift is 80 centimes in both cases, and the sample is 40 customers in both cases. Only the design differs.

Same effect. Same number of people. One study is inconclusive and the other is overwhelming — because pairing removed the noise that had nothing to do with the menu. The 95% interval for the paired lift is CHF 0.42 to CHF 1.18: not just "real", but usefully bounded.

The cheapest way to buy statistical power is almost never more data. It is a better design.

In Excel. Paired: =T.TEST(before, after, 2, 1). Independent, not assuming equal variances: =T.TEST(rng1, rng2, 2, 3). The third argument is the number of tails; the fourth is the design. Getting that fourth argument wrong is exactly the mistake above, in reverse.

Slide Vevey's average up towards Nyon's and watch the interval slither across zero. The moment it touches zero, p crosses 0.05 — they are two views of the same arithmetic. That is worth carrying into the exam: a 95% interval for a difference that contains zero is exactly a two-tailed test that fails to reject at α = 0.05.

Two proportions

62% at Nyon, 48% at Vevey. Different, or not?

Same logic, different standard error. When comparing two shares you pool them under H₀ — because if H₀ is true, both branches share one underlying rate:

pooled p̂ = (37.2 + 26.4) ÷ 115 = 0.553
SE = √[ 0.553 × 0.447 × (1/60 + 1/55) ] = 0.0928
z = (0.62 − 0.48) ÷ 0.0928 = 1.508 → p = 0.131

A 14-point gap between the branches, and it still fails to reject at α = 0.05. Proportions are noisy: with 60 and 55 respondents, a 14-point difference is comfortably within what chance produces. Anyone who reports "Nyon is 14 points ahead" without that caveat is reporting the noise.

In Excel there is no built-in two-proportion test. Build it: =(p1-p2)/SQRT(pp*(1-pp)*(1/n1+1/n2)) for z, then =2*(1-NORM.S.DIST(ABS(z),TRUE)) for the two-tailed p.

Take it outside Café Léman

One test, on data that is actually yours.

Several of you have said the same thing: the ideas make sense inside the case, and then evaporate on the way to your own work. So from this week on, every lesson ends with one small thing to try on data you already have. It is not graded and nobody sees it — it exists because the step from "I followed that" to "I can do that" only happens on your own numbers.

Pick whichever one you already have the data for

  1. Your commute. Time your journey for ten mornings. Is your average genuinely different from the number you always tell people ("it's about forty minutes")? That is a one-sample t-test with μ₀ = 40, and it takes one line of Excel.
  2. Something at work that changed. A price, a process, a supplier, a shift pattern. Pull the same measure for the periods before and after. Is it a two-sample test or a paired one? Deciding that correctly is 80% of the skill.
  3. Your own spending. Export three months of card transactions. Test whether your weekend daily spend really differs from your weekday spend — two independent samples, and almost certainly a bigger difference than you expect.

Then answer the three questions that matter more than the arithmetic: what was H₀? · would you have bet money on the result before you ran it? · if p came out above 0.05, was that because there is nothing there, or because you only had ten mornings? That last question is §5.4, and it is the one that separates people who run tests from people who understand them.

Bring whatever you find to the discussion forum if you'd like a second pair of eyes on it — including the ones that came out inconclusive. Those are usually the more interesting posts.

Your Excel toolkit

Every test this week, as a formula.
