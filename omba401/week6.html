Analysis of variance: four branches, one question

Last week you compared one group to a number, and two groups to each other. Now the regional manager wants all four Café Léman branches ranked — and if you do that with a t-test on every pair, you will hand her a finding that is not there. This week: one test, asked once, that covers all four at the same α. Plan 4–5 hours: about two for this lesson and its labs, then 2–3 for the homework problems linked below.

By the end of Week 6 you will be able to

  • Say why running every pairwise t-test inflates the false-alarm rate, and put a number on how badly.
  • Write the ANOVA hypotheses correctly — and know why H₁ is not "all the means differ".
  • Split total variation into between-group and within-group parts, and explain what each one measures.
  • Build a complete ANOVA table by hand — SS, df, MS and F — and reproduce it in Excel.
  • Read F against its critical value and its p-value, and check the three assumptions before you believe either.
  • Report an effect size, and use Tukey's HSD to say which groups actually differ once F is significant.

Section 6.1 · The Hook

Four branches, six comparisons

Your decision

Which Café Léman branch is doing best — and can you prove it?

The regional manager has average spend per customer from four branches, six days each: Nyon, Vevey, Morges and Rolle. She wants to know whether the branches genuinely differ, so she can send the Rolle manager to retrain the others — or not.

You know how to compare two groups. So the obvious move is to compare every pair: Nyon vs Vevey, Nyon vs Morges, Nyon vs Rolle, Vevey vs Morges, Vevey vs Rolle, Morges vs Rolle. That is six t-tests.

Before you run them, notice something. Each test is run at α = 0.05 — a one-in-twenty chance of a false alarm. You are about to take that gamble six times.

Commit — then lock it

Suppose the four branches are in truth completely identical. You run all six pairwise t-tests at α = 0.05. What is the chance that at least one comes back "significant"?

Pick your gut answer.
Locked. The answer is 26.5%. Each test has a 95% chance of behaving; six independent tests all behaving is 0.956 = 0.735, so the chance that at least one misfires is 1 − 0.735 = 0.265. Better than a one-in-four chance of reporting a difference that does not exist — in a study where, by construction, nothing is going on. And it gets worse fast: ten groups means 45 pairs and a 90% chance of a false alarm. This is the multiple-comparisons problem, and it is the entire reason this week exists.
The shape of the week

One test, asked once, at one α.

Analysis of variance replaces the six tests with a single question: is there any difference anywhere among these four means? One test, one α, no inflation. Four moves:

  • One null for all of them — and the surprising place the evidence comes from: variance (§6.2).
  • Build the F ratio — the ANOVA table, line by line, on the real branch data (§6.3).
  • Read it honestly — critical value, p-value, the three assumptions, and effect size (§6.4).
  • Find out which ones — because a significant F does not tell you. Tukey's HSD does (§6.5).

ANOVA is a test about means that works entirely by comparing variances. That sentence sounds wrong the first time. By the end of §6.2 it will sound obvious.

Section 6.2 · The Logic

A test about means, decided by variances

The hypotheses

One null, and an alternative most people get wrong.

ANOVA asks one question of all four branches at once, so it needs only one pair of hypotheses.

H₀ · the null
μ₁ = μ₂ = μ₃ = μ₄
Every branch has the same true average spend. Any differences you see in the six-day samples are sampling wobble.
H₁ · the alternative
at least one μ differs
Not "all four differ". One branch out of line is enough to make H₀ false. This is why a significant F cannot tell you which — it was never asked.
The most common exam mistake is writing H₁ as μ₁ ≠ μ₂ ≠ μ₃ ≠ μ₄. That says all four are mutually different, which is a much stronger claim than the test makes — and it is not even well defined. Write it in words: at least one mean differs from the others.

Watch for the moment the presenter stops talking about means and starts talking about spread. That switch is the whole trick, and the lab below makes it physical.

Where the evidence comes from

Two kinds of variation, and their ratio.

Take all 24 observations and forget which branch they came from. They vary — some days are CHF 12.60, some are CHF 16.90. That is total variation. ANOVA splits it into two pieces that add up exactly:

SSB · between groups
signal
How far the four branch means sit from the grand mean. If the branches really differ, this is large.
SSW · within groups
noise
How far each day sits from its own branch mean. This is ordinary day-to-day variation and has nothing to do with branch differences.
SStotal  =  SSbetween  +  SSwithin

Now the leap. If H₀ is true and all four branches are identical, then the spread between the branch means is caused by nothing but the same sampling noise that produces the spread within each branch. The two would estimate the same thing, and their ratio would be about 1.

If the branches genuinely differ, the between-group piece is inflated by real differences on top of the noise — so the ratio climbs above 1. That ratio is F.

F  =  MSbetween ÷ MSwithin  =  signal + noise ÷ noise

That is why a test about means is decided by variances. The means enter through SSB; the noise yardstick comes from SSW; the verdict is their ratio.

Three checks worth doing every single time, because each one catches a different slip:

  1. SSB + SSW = SST. 20.6467 + 11.2933 = 31.9400. ✓ If this fails, you have an arithmetic error, not a finding.
  2. dfbetween + dfwithin = dftotal. 3 + 20 = 23 = N − 1. ✓
  3. MSW should look like a typical group variance. Take each branch’s own sum of squares from Step 3 and divide by n − 1 = 5: 3.8933 ÷ 5 = 0.779, 3.1133 ÷ 5 = 0.623, 2.0733 ÷ 5 = 0.415, 2.2133 ÷ 5 = 0.443. MSW = 0.5647 sits right among them, as it must — it is their pooled average.
Why dfwithin = 20: each branch contributes n − 1 = 5 degrees of freedom to the noise estimate, and there are four branches: 4 × 5 = 20. Equivalently N − k = 24 − 4. The second form is the one that generalises to unequal group sizes.

F = 12.19 against a critical value of 3.098 — reject H₀. The four branches do not all have the same average spend.
F is always one-tailed to the right. There is no such thing as a suspiciously small F to reject on — F below 1 just means the between-group differences are smaller than the noise, which is exactly what H₀ predicts. Every F table you will ever see is a right-tail table.

Before you believe it

Three assumptions, and how to check them in a minute

ANOVA earns its p-value by assuming three things. Two of them it forgives; one it does not.

AssumptionHow to checkOur data
Independence
observations don't influence each other		Think about the design, not the numbers. Were the days sampled separately? Is one branch's Tuesday linked to another's?Six separate trading days per branch — fine. Not forgiving: violate this and nothing below matters.
Normality
within each group		Histogram or a quick look for wild skew. With n ≥ 30 per group the CLT covers you anyway.n = 6 is small, so look at the numbers: no outliers, no obvious skew. Acceptable. Forgiving when groups are equal sized.
Equal variances
homoscedasticity		Rule of thumb: largest s ÷ smallest s < 2. Formally, Levene's test.0.8824 ÷ 0.6439 = 1.37 ✓ comfortably under 2. Forgiving with equal n, much less so without.
If equal variances fails badly and your groups are different sizes, the F-test's real false-alarm rate can be double what you think. The fix is Welch's ANOVA — the same idea as Welch's t-test from §5.5. Excel has no built-in for it; R and Python do.
How big, not just whether

Effect size: η² = 0.65

A p-value of 0.0001 tells you the differences are real. It says nothing about whether they are large. For that, report the share of total variation the grouping explains:

η²  =  SSB ÷ SST  =  20.6467 ÷ 31.9400  =  0.646

Which branch you are in explains about 65% of all the variation in daily average spend. That is a genuinely large effect — the conventional bands are 0.01 small, 0.06 medium, 0.14 large. It is also the sentence the regional manager actually wanted, and the one a bare p-value would never have given her.

Say it in francs too. η² is a proportion and proportions are slippery in a meeting. The branch means run from CHF 13.53 to CHF 16.07 — a spread of CHF 2.54 per customer. At roughly 300 customers a day, that gap is about CHF 760 a day between the best and worst branch. Statistical significance, effect size, and money: give all three.
Section 6.5 · Post Hoc

A significant F does not name a winner

The awkward gap

You have proved that something differs. She asked which.

F = 12.19, p = 0.0001. You reject H₀. All that entitles you to say is: at least one branch differs from the others. The regional manager's next sentence is inevitable — "So is Rolle better than Nyon?" — and the ANOVA you just ran cannot answer it.

The temptation is to go back and run the six t-tests after all, now that F has "given permission". That is better than running them cold, but it still leaks false alarms. The proper tool is a post hoc test, which compares every pair while holding the family-wise error rate at 5%.

One number, then six comparisons

Tukey's honestly significant difference

Tukey computes a single yardstick — the smallest gap between two group means that counts as real — and then applies it to every pair. One number does all six comparisons.

HSD  =  q × √(MSW ÷ n)  =  3.958 × √(0.5647 ÷ 6)  =  1.214

The q is the studentised range statistic, looked up for k = 4 groups and dfwithin = 20 at α = 0.05. It plays exactly the role a critical t played last week, but it is inflated to account for the fact that you are making six comparisons rather than one — which is precisely why it is honest.

So: any two branch means more than CHF 1.21 apart differ significantly. Any pair closer than that does not.

Six naive t-tests flag four pairs as significant. Tukey confirms only three. Nyon vs Rolle — p = 0.045, comfortably "significant" on its own — does not survive once the other five comparisons are accounted for.

That single row is the whole week in miniature. A gap of CHF 1.03 between Nyon and Rolle looks convincing in isolation, and would have been written into the memo as a finding. Held to the standard that six comparisons demand, it is not distinguishable from noise. The honest answer to "is Rolle better than Nyon?" is: not on this evidence.

When the groups are not the same size

Tukey–Kramer — the same test, done properly for unequal n

Café Léman gave us six days per branch, so one HSD covered all six pairs. Real data almost never arrives that tidy: one branch was closed for a week, one segment of your survey is far larger than the others, one supplier has been on the books three times as long.

The fix is one substitution. The n in the formula was the size of a group; when the two groups differ, use the harmonic mean of the two sizes being compared:

nh  =  2 ÷ (1/na + 1/nb)     then     HSD  =  q × √(MSW ÷ nh)

That is Tukey–Kramer, and the name matters less than this: it is not a different test. It is Tukey's HSD with the one adjustment that unequal groups require, and with equal groups it collapses back to exactly the formula above — the harmonic mean of 6 and 6 is 6. Software offers it as "Tukey–Kramer" or simply as "Tukey HSD" with unequal n handled silently.

One consequence is worth seeing. Suppose Rolle had lost two days to a closure, so we compared Nyon (n = 6) with Rolle (n = 4):

nh = 2 ÷ (1/6 + 1/4) = 4.8   →   HSD = 3.958 × √(0.5647 ÷ 4.8) = 1.36

The yardstick widened from 1.21 to 1.36. So the HSD is no longer one number — each pair gets its own, and the pairs involving your smallest groups get the widest one. That is not a defect; it is the method saying what it should say. Less data on a group means less certainty about where its mean sits, so the gap has to be bigger before you may call it real.

You will need this for the final essay. Part 3 runs an ANOVA across the four customer segments, and those segments are nowhere near equal in size — the survey has 130, 140, 97 and 53 members in them. Use Tukey–Kramer there, and say in the paper that you did. The formula is also on the notation key.
What you can actually report

Three defensible sentences

ClaimSupported?
"The four branches do not all perform the same." Yes — F(3, 20) = 12.19, p < 0.001.
"Rolle and Nyon both beat Vevey."Rolle yes (gap 2.53), Nyon yes (gap 1.50) — both above HSD 1.21.
"Rolle is our best branch."No — Rolle is not separable from Nyon (gap 1.03 < 1.21). It is in the top group, not alone at the top.

Notice what the analysis has actually produced: not a ranking, but a grouping. Vevey sits at the bottom, Rolle at the top, and Nyon and Morges are genuinely hard to separate from their neighbours. If the business decision is "who do we send for retraining", that is a far more useful — and far more defensible — answer than a league table.

Other post hoc tests you will meet. Bonferroni simply divides α by the number of comparisons (0.05 ÷ 6 = 0.0083) — dead simple, and slightly conservative: on this week's numbers it demands a gap about 5% wider than Tukey's, and that penalty grows only slowly as you add comparisons. It is a fine fallback; Tukey is exact for the all-pairs problem, which is why it is the default. Scheffé is even more conservative but allows any comparison you think of afterwards. Dunnett compares every group against one control only, which needs fewer comparisons and so has more power. Tukey is the default when you want all pairs.

Take it outside Café Léman

One comparison of three or more groups, on data that is actually yours.

Same deal as last week: one small thing on your own numbers, not graded, nobody sees it. ANOVA is unusually easy to find real uses for, because "we have several groups and someone wants them ranked" is most of management.

Pick whichever one you already have the data for

  1. Three or more of anything at work. Regions, suppliers, product lines, shift patterns, campaign variants. Pull one number per unit per period. Run ANOVA: Single Factor. Before you look at the p-value, write down which group you expect to win — the gap between your expectation and the result is the interesting part.
  2. Your week. Log something daily for a month — commute time, hours slept, steps, spend. Group by day of week. Is Monday genuinely different, or is that a story you tell yourself? This one is nearly always a lesson in how much within-group noise there is.
  3. Somebody's league table. Find a published ranking in your industry — branches, funds, schools, hospitals. Ask whether the gaps between adjacent positions are bigger than the noise. Most published rankings would not survive a Tukey test, and knowing that changes how you read them.

Then the three questions that matter more than the arithmetic: did the equal-variance check pass? · what is your η², and would you call that big in your business? · after a significant F, how many pairs actually survived post hoc — and did any of your "obvious" differences fail? That last one is the §6.5 lesson, and it is the one that will change a report you write.

Bring whatever you find to Forum 2, which opens this week and closes Sunday 6 September — including the ones where F came out non-significant. A well-explained null result is a better post than a lucky one.

Your Excel toolkit

The whole week, as a formula.

Excel gives you the same two routes as last week: build the table yourself from sums of squares, or let the Analysis ToolPak print the whole thing. Learn both — the first is what the exam asks for, the second is what you will use at work.

TaskExcel
The whole ANOVA table at onceData → Data Analysis → ANOVA: Single Factor
Grand mean=AVERAGE(all_data)
SS within one group=DEVSQ(group_rng)
SSW (sum over groups)=DEVSQ(g1)+DEVSQ(g2)+DEVSQ(g3)+DEVSQ(g4)
SST=DEVSQ(all_data)
SSB=DEVSQ(all_data)−(SSW)  or  =SUMPRODUCT(n_j,(means−grand)^2)
MSB · MSW=SSB/(k−1)  ·  =SSW/(N−k)
F=MSB/MSW
p-value from F=F.DIST.RT(F, df1, df2)
Critical value of F=F.INV.RT(0.05, df1, df2)
Effect size η²=SSB/SST
Equal-variance check=MAX(STDEV.S(g1),…)/MIN(STDEV.S(g1),…) — want < 2
Tukey HSD=q*SQRT(MSW/n) — look q up in a studentised-range table

Watch out: the ToolPak's ANOVA output labels the two rows "Between Groups" and "Within Groups", and calls the p-value "P-value" and the critical value "F crit". It gives you no effect size and no post hoc test — you must add η² and Tukey yourself, which is exactly why so many reports stop at the p-value.

Where this goes next

From groups to drivers.

You can now compare one group to a number, two groups to each other, and any number of groups at once. But every one of those tests takes a categorical input — which branch — and asks whether it moves the outcome.

The obvious next question is what happens when the input is a number. Not "which branch", but "how much does spend rise per extra minute of service time, per franc of advertising, per degree of temperature?" That is regression, and it is Week 7.

The comforting part: it is the same machine. The optional StatQuest video above shows that ANOVA is a regression with categorical predictors — same sums of squares, same F, same table. You are not starting again next week; you are turning the same handle.
