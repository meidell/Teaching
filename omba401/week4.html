Sampling & estimation: from a sample to a statement

Week 3 gave you the grammar of uncertainty. This week you use it to do the thing statistics is actually for: saying something honest about a whole population from a handful of it. At Café Léman, an intern surveys 60 regulars and 62% say a CHF 250,000 refit would bring them back more often — the board wants 55% to greenlight it. You'll see why 62% is not an answer, and how a confidence interval turns one sample into a defensible statement. Section 4.1 · The Hook

The 62% that isn't

Your decision

Do you sign off a CHF 250,000 refit?

Café Léman is deciding whether to refit the Nyon branch — new layout, new seating, CHF 250,000. The board's rule is simple: go ahead only if more than 55% of regulars say they'd visit more often afterwards. An intern surveys 60 regulars. 62% say yes. The CFO reads the number, sees 62 > 55, and reaches for a pen.

You pause, because 62% is not one number — it is one number from one sample of 60 people. Ask a different 60 and you'd get something else. Before we compute anything: how sure can the board actually be that the true figure across all regulars is above 55%?

Commit — then lock it
Pick your gut answer.
Locked. With only 60 responses, the honest 95% range around 62% runs from roughly 50% to 74% — a spread of nearly 25 percentage points. The survey simply cannot tell the board whether true support is 51% or 73%, and both sit on opposite sides of the decision. (And the fourth answer is right too, in a different way: if the 60 weren't chosen properly, no amount of arithmetic repairs them. We'll deal with both.) You'll compute that range yourself in §4.5.
From "what might" to "what is"

Probability → inference.

Week 3 ran forwards: given a known population, what might a sample look like? This week runs backwards — the direction real business questions come in. You have the sample; you want the population. Four moves get you there:

  • Why a sample can speak at all — the sampling distribution and the central limit theorem (§4.2).
  • How wrong it can be — the standard error, and the √n law (§4.3).
  • Saying it honestly — confidence intervals for a mean, with z and with t (§4.4).
  • Proportions and planning — the refit survey, and how many people you actually needed (§4.5).

A single number from a sample is never an answer. An interval, with a confidence level attached, is.

Section 4.2 · The Foundation

Why a sample can speak for a population

First, name the two worlds

Parameter or statistic — which one do you actually have?

Every question this week lives across two worlds. The population is everyone you care about — all 4,300 Café Léman members. The sample is the handful you actually measured. Each world has its own vocabulary, and confusing them is the root of most bad statistics:

QuantityPopulation (parameter)Sample (statistic)
Meanμ ("mu")x̄ ("x-bar")
Standard deviationσ ("sigma")s
Proportionpp̂ ("p-hat")
StatusFixed but unknownKnown but random

That last row is the whole week in two phrases. The parameter you want is a fixed number you'll never see. The statistic you have is a number you can see, but which would have come out differently had you asked different people. Inference is the discipline of getting from the second to the first while being honest about the gap.

The idea that makes it work

A statistic has a distribution of its own.

Here's the move that unlocks everything. Take a sample of 25 customers, compute the mean spend. Now do it again — different 25, different mean. Do it a thousand times and you get a thousand means, and those pile up into a shape of their own. That shape is the sampling distribution of x̄, and the central limit theorem tells us three remarkable things about it:

  • It is centred on μ. Sample means are not systematically too high or too low — they're right on target, on average. (This is what "unbiased" means.)
  • It is narrower than the population — its spread is σ/√n. Averaging cancels out extremes, and more averaging cancels more.
  • It becomes a bell as n grows, no matter what shape the population has. Skewed, lumpy, two-humped — the means still go normal. In practice n ≈ 30 is usually plenty.

The third point is the miracle, and the lab below is built to make you believe it. The population you'll sample from is customer spend — heavily right-skewed, nothing like a bell, a long tail of big spenders. Watch what the averages do.

Section 4.3 · The Wobble

The standard error & the √n law

Two different wobbles

How much the data varies vs how much the average varies.

This is the distinction students most often lose, so slow down here. The standard deviation (σ or s) tells you how spread out individual values are — how different one day, or one customer, is from another. The standard error tells you how spread out your estimate is — how far a sample mean typically lands from the truth.

standard error of the mean  =  SE = σ ÷ √n

Nyon's daily cups have σ = 23: any single day swings around by roughly 23 cups. But average 36 days and the average only wobbles by 23 ÷ √36 = 3.83 cups. The data are as noisy as ever; your estimate of the centre is six times steadier. That is the entire economic case for collecting more data.

When σ is unknown — which is nearly always — you use the sample's own s in its place: SE = s ÷ √n. That substitution has a price, and §4.4 shows what it is.

Notice the shape: the first thirty days buy you an enormous amount, and the second hundred buy you almost nothing. Because the error falls with √n and not n, halving your error costs four times the data. Nine days → ±15 cups. Thirty-six days → ±7.5. One hundred and forty-four days → ±3.8. Each step is 4× the work for 2× the precision.

"Get more data" is good advice exactly twice: when you have very little, and when you can afford to quadruple it.

Worked example — is the café slowing down?

A sample of 36 days.

Head office knows from six years of till records that Nyon's long-run daily average is μ = 193 cups with σ = 23. You pull a sample of the last 36 days and get x̄ = 188. Is the café slowing down, or is 188 just noise?

  • The standard error is 23 ÷ √36 = 23 ÷ 6 = 3.83 cups.
  • 188 sits (188 − 193) ÷ 3.83 = −1.30 standard errors below the long-run mean.
  • A z of −1.30 is an ordinary distance — roughly 10% of 36-day stretches land at least that low with nothing wrong at all.

So: no evidence of a slowdown. Note what made the judgement possible — not the 5-cup gap, but the gap measured in standard errors. Five cups would have been damning with n = 400 and meaningless with n = 4. Week 5 turns this exact reasoning into a formal hypothesis test.

Section 4.4 · The Statement

Confidence intervals for a mean

The shape of every interval you'll ever build

Estimate, plus or minus how wrong you expect to be.

A point estimate like x̄ = 188 is a guess with no honesty attached. A confidence interval attaches it. Every interval in this course, for every quantity, has the same three parts:

estimate  ±  (critical value × standard error)
the middle · how confident you want to be · how noisy your estimate is

The second and third parts together are the margin of error. Only two things can widen an interval: wanting more confidence, or having a noisier estimate. Only one thing narrows it for free — a bigger n.

For the Nyon sample (n = 36, x̄ = 188, σ = 23, SE = 3.83) at 95% confidence, the critical value is 1.96:

188 ± 1.96 × 3.83  =  188 ± 7.51  =  180.5 to 195.5 cups

That is the statement you can defend. Notice it contains 193 — the long-run mean — so this sample is entirely consistent with nothing having changed, which is exactly what §4.3 concluded the long way round.

The bit that costs marks

What "95% confident" actually means.

The tempting reading is "there's a 95% probability the true mean is between 180.5 and 195.5." It is wrong, and it's worth understanding why: μ isn't random — the interval is. The true mean is a fixed number sitting there; your interval jumped to where it jumped because of which 36 days you happened to draw. It either caught μ or it didn't.

What the 95% describes is the reliability of the method: if you repeated this whole exercise many times, about 95% of the intervals you built would contain μ. You get one draw from that process. The lab below runs the process fifty times at once so you can watch the misses happen.

When you don't know σ — which is nearly always

The t-distribution: paying for what you don't know.

The 1.96 above assumed you knew the population's σ. In real work you don't; you have s from the same small sample that gave you x̄. Using a guessed σ makes your interval too optimistic, so you compensate by using a slightly fatter bell: the t-distribution, indexed by degrees of freedom = n − 1.

Read the column downwards: t is always bigger than 1.96 — that's the penalty — and it shrinks towards 1.96 as n grows, because a big sample estimates σ well. By n = 50 the penalty is under 3%. Small samples are where t matters.

Worked example. You sample 25 customers and find a mean spend of CHF 14.80 with s = CHF 3.50. Then SE = 3.50 ÷ √25 = 0.70, df = 24, t = 2.064:

14.80 ± 2.064 × 0.70  =  14.80 ± 1.44  =  CHF 13.36 to CHF 16.24

In Excel that whole margin is one function: =CONFIDENCE.T(0.05, 3.5, 25).

The rule of thumb. σ genuinely known → use z. σ estimated by s → use t with n − 1 df. When n is large the two agree, so if in doubt, use t — it is never wrong, only occasionally over-cautious.

Section 4.5 · The Decision

Proportions, and how big a sample you needed

Same shape, different middle

An interval for a percentage.

"62% said yes" is a proportion, p̂ — and it obeys the same three-part recipe as a mean. Only the standard error changes:

SE of a proportion  =  √( p̂(1−p̂) ÷ n )
interval  =  p̂ ± z × SE

For the refit survey: p̂ = 0.62, n = 60. So SE = √(0.62 × 0.38 ÷ 60) = √0.003927 = 0.0627, and at 95% the margin of error is 1.96 × 0.0627 = 0.123 — 12.3 percentage points:

0.62 ± 0.123  =  49.7% to 74.3%

There it is. The honest statement the intern's survey supports is "somewhere between about half and three-quarters of regulars" — and the board's 55% threshold sits inside that range. The survey does not clear the bar; it doesn't even come close to settling the question.

Two habits worth stealing. First, when you have no prior estimate of p, use p = 0.50 — it maximises p(1−p) and so gives the safest, largest n. Second, notice the E² in the denominator: the sample size you need grows with the square of the precision you demand. Going from ±5 points to ±2.5 points doesn't cost twice as many respondents. It costs four times.

So what do you tell the board?

Not "yes" and not "no" — "not yet, and here's what it costs to know." The intern's 60 responses put true support somewhere between 50% and 74%, which straddles the 55% rule and therefore decides nothing. To be confident the true figure clears 55%, you need the interval's lower edge above it: with p̂ ≈ 0.62 that means a margin of error under 7 points, which takes about 185 respondents.

So the recommendation writes itself: hold the CHF 250,000 decision for two weeks and spend a fraction of it surveying 200 regulars properly. That is a rounding error against the refit budget, and it converts an unanswerable question into an answerable one. And make sure those 200 are drawn across all days and times — remember, a bigger sample fixes noise, never bias.

The value of a statistician in the room is rarely the answer. It's knowing when the data cannot support one — and what it would take.

Your Excel toolkit

When you need the exact number, not just the picture.

The intuition lives in the labs above; these are the functions for a precise answer. CONFIDENCE.* returns the margin of error — add and subtract it from the mean yourself.

TaskExcel
Sample mean, SD, size=AVERAGE(rng) · =STDEV.S(rng) · =COUNT(rng)
Standard error of the mean=STDEV.S(rng)/SQRT(COUNT(rng))
z for a 95% level=NORM.S.INV(0.975) → 1.96
t for 95%, n = 25=T.INV.2T(0.05,24) → 2.064
Margin of error, σ known=CONFIDENCE.NORM(0.05,σ,n)
Margin of error, σ unknown=CONFIDENCE.T(0.05,s,n)
SE of a proportion=SQRT(p*(1−p)/n)
Sample size for a proportion=CEILING(NORM.S.INV(0.975)^2*p*(1−p)/E^2,1)

Tip: alpha is 1 − confidence, so 0.05 for 95% and 0.01 for 99%. The interval itself is always =AVERAGE(rng) minus and plus the margin.
