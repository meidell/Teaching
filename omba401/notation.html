Start here

Eight rules that decode almost any symbol

Statistics notation is not arbitrary. Once these eight habits click, most formulas can be read aloud without looking anything up.

  1. Greek letters describe the population — the truth you're after. μ σ σ² p λ. You almost never know them. That is the entire reason this course exists.
  2. Latin letters describe your sample — the answer you actually measured. x̄ s s² p̂. Known, but only an estimate of the Greek one above it.
  3. A bar means "the mean of". x̄ is said "x-bar" and means the average of all the x values.
  4. A hat means "estimated from a sample". p̂ is said "p-hat": the proportion your sample gave you, standing in for the true p.
  5. A subscript i means "the i-th one". xᵢ is one single observation — the one you happen to be looking at. x₃ is the third.
  6. Σ (capital sigma) means "add up everything after me". Σx = add all the values. It is an instruction, not a quantity.
  7. Big N is the population; little n is your sample. If a formula contains n, it wants the number of things you measured — in one sample. 25 cups is n = 25; 36 days sampled is n = 36; 60 regulars surveyed is n = 60. n is never "how many samples I took": that quantity has no symbol and appears in no formula, because in real work you take one sample. It only shows up in the Week 4 simulations, which repeat the draw so you can see the pattern.
  8. In the Excel column, only the function name is literal. rng means "the range of cells your data sits in" — replace it with the real cells, so =AVERAGE(rng) becomes =AVERAGE(B2:B37). Same for s, n, p, σ and df: type the number, or point at the cell holding it.

One habit worth stealing: when you copy a formula into your notes, write the sentence next to it. "s = the spread of my data, in the data's own units" is worth more in an exam than the square roots.

Centre, spread & position

The busiest week for notation. Everything after this reuses these letters.

SymbolSay itWhat it stands forWhere

xᵢ"x-sub-i"A single observation — one day's cups, one customer's spend.§2.2

The formulas, and the Excel that does them

Mean
x̄ = Σx ÷ n
add up every value, divide by how many there are
Excel: =AVERAGE(A2:A21)
Weighted mean
x̄w = Σ(wᵢ · xᵢ) ÷ Σwᵢ
multiply each value by its weight, add those up, divide by the total weight
Excel: =SUMPRODUCT(x,w)/SUM(w)
Sample standard deviation
s = √[ Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1) ]
distance of each value from the mean, squared, averaged over n − 1, then square-rooted back into the original units
Excel: =STDEV.S(A2:A21)  ·  population version: =STDEV.P(…)
Interquartile range
IQR = Q3 − Q1
the width of the middle 50% of the sorted data
Excel: =QUARTILE.INC(rng,3)−QUARTILE.INC(rng,1)
Coefficient of variation
CV = (s ÷ x̄) × 100%
the spread expressed as a percentage of the average, so two different units can be compared
z-score
z = (x − x̄) ÷ s
how many standard deviations this value is from the mean — positive above, negative below
Excel: =(x−AVERAGE(rng))/STDEV.S(rng)
Outlier fences · the 1.5 × IQR rule
lower = Q1 − 1.5 · IQR  ·  upper = Q3 + 1.5 · IQR
anything outside these two lines is flagged for a look — flagged, not deleted
Chebyshev's rule · any shape at all
at least (1 − 1/k²) of all values lie within k standard deviations of the mean
for any k > 1 — k = 2 gives at least 75%, k = 3 gives at least 89%. Works on any distribution, however ugly.
The empirical rule · bell shapes only
68% within 1 SD  ·  95% within 2  ·  99.7% within 3
much sharper than Chebyshev, but you only get to use it when the data is roughly symmetric and bell-shaped
Week 3 · Probability & distributions

The grammar of uncertainty

The lessons deliberately write probability in words. Your textbook and the exam use the set symbols, so both are given below — they mean exactly the same thing.

SymbolSay itWhat it stands forWhere
P(A)"probability of A"The chance event A happens, always between 0 and 1.§3.2
P(not A)"probability of not-A"The complement — the chance it doesn't happen, = 1 − P(A). Textbook: P(A′) or P(Ac).§3.2
P(A and B)"A and B"Both happen. Textbook: P(A ∩ B), "A intersect B".§3.2
P(A or B)"A or B"At least one of them happens. Textbook: P(A ∪ B), "A union B".§3.2
P(A given B)"A given B"Conditional: the chance of A once you already know B happened — you've thrown away everyone else. Textbook: P(A | B).§3.2
X"big X"The random quantity itself — "the number of coupons redeemed". Not yet a number.§3.4
k"k"A specific count X might take — "exactly k successes". Some books write x. Not Week 2's Chebyshev k.§3.4
n"n"In the binomial: the number of trials — 10 coupons issued, 20 calls made. Here it is not a sample size.§3.4
p"p"The chance of success on a single trial, the same every time.§3.4
1 − p"one minus p"The chance of failure on one trial. Many textbooks call this q.§3.4
C(n, k)"n choose k"How many different ways k successes can be arranged among n tries. Excel: =COMBIN(n,k).§3.4
E(X)"expected value of X"The long-run average of the random quantity. A centre, not a promise — no single play has to hit it.§3.4
λ"lambda"The Poisson rate: the average number of events per stretch of time or space. Its mean and its variance are both λ.§3.4
e"e", Euler’s numberA fixed constant, 2.71828… — the same kind of thing as π. Not a variable, nothing to solve for. Press the eˣ key, or type =EXP(). You meet it in P(X = 0) = e−λ, the chance a Poisson interval is completely empty.§3.4
Φ(z)"Phi of z"The area to the left of z under the standard normal curve — what a z-table gives you. Φ(1) = 0.841 means 84.1% of values fall below one SD above the mean. For the area to the right, use 1 − Φ(z).§3.5
μ, σ"mu, sigma"For a normal curve: the centre it sits on and the usual wobble either side of it.§3.5
z"z-score"Week 2's ruler, now used to read areas: turn any value into a z, look up the area, that area is the probability.§3.5
X ~ N(μ, σ)"X is normal with mean mu, SD sigma"Shorthand for "this quantity follows a bell curve centred on μ with spread σ".§3.5

The formulas, and the Excel that does them

The flip side · complement
P(not A) = 1 − P(A)
the shortcut for every "at least one" question: work out the chance of none, subtract from 1
Addition rule · "or"
P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)
add the two, then subtract the overlap so the shared cases aren't counted twice
Multiplication rule · "and then"
P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B given A)
the chance of the first, times the chance of the second once the first has happened. If they're independent, P(B given A) is just P(B).
Conditional probability
P(A given B) = P(A and B) ÷ P(B)
shrink the world down to B, then ask what fraction of it is also A. Independence check: A and B are independent when P(A given B) = P(A).
Bayes, the way we do it
imagine 10,000 people · count who is flagged · of those, count who is truly guilty
the answer is that second count over the first. Base rates ruin intuition; counting people never lies.
Binomial · k successes in n tries
P(X = k) = C(n, k) · pk · (1 − p)n−k
mean = n · p  ·  SD = √[ n · p · (1 − p) ]
use it when there is a fixed number of independent tries and each one is a yes/no with the same chance
Excel: =BINOM.DIST(k,n,p,FALSE) for exactly k · TRUE for k or fewer
Poisson · events over an interval
mean = variance = λ  ·  P(X = 0) = e−λ
use it when events arrive at some average rate and there is no natural "number of tries" — arrivals per hour, defects per batch
Excel: =POISSON.DIST(k,λ,FALSE)
Expected value
E(X) = Σ [ each value × its chance ]
weight every outcome by how likely it is, then add. This is what a promotion is "worth" per play.
Normal · turning a value into an area
z = (x − μ) ÷ σ
then the area to the left of that z is the chance of a value at or below x
Excel: =NORM.DIST(x,μ,σ,TRUE) · from a z: =NORM.S.DIST(z,TRUE) · backwards: =NORM.INV(p,μ,σ)
Week 4 · Sampling distributions & estimation

From a sample to a statement

Week 4 is where the Greek/Latin split earns its keep: every formula here is about the distance between what you measured and what is true.

SymbolSay itWhat it stands forWhere
μ vs x̄"mu versus x-bar"The parameter you want versus the statistic you have. Week 4 is the study of the gap between them.§4.2
σ vs s"sigma versus s"Population spread (rarely known) versus your sample's spread (always available).§4.2
p vs p̂"p versus p-hat"The true proportion versus the proportion your sample gave you — 62% of 60 surveyed, say.§4.5
SE"standard error"How much your estimate wobbles from sample to sample — not how spread out the data is. Always smaller than s.§4.3
√n"root n"The square-root law: error falls with √n, so halving your error costs four times the data.§4.3
z"z-critical", or "z-star"The critical value off the normal curve for your confidence level — 1.96 at 95%. Written z* in many books.§4.4
t"t", or "t-star"The same idea from the fatter t-curve, used when you had to estimate σ with s. Always a little bigger than z.§4.4
df = n − 1"degrees of freedom"Which row of the t-table to use. n = 25 → df = 24 → t = 2.064 at 95%.§4.4
E"margin of error"The ± half-width of the interval: critical value × standard error. Not to be confused with E(X) from Week 3.§4.4
CI"confidence interval"The estimate ± its margin. Read it as "values the data can't rule out", never as "95% chance the truth is in here".§4.4
0.05"alpha", the 0.05 in Excel1 − your confidence level. 95% confidence → 0.05. It's the first argument of every CONFIDENCE.* function.§4.4

The formulas, and the Excel that does them

Standard error of the mean
SE = σ ÷ √n   (or s ÷ √n when σ is unknown)
how far a sample mean typically lands from the truth — the data's spread, damped by the size of your sample
Excel: =STDEV.S(rng)/SQRT(COUNT(rng))
Every confidence interval in the course
estimate ± (critical value × standard error)
the middle · how confident you want to be · how noisy your estimate is. Learn this shape and the rest is substitution.
CI for a mean · σ known
x̄ ± z × (σ ÷ √n)  with z = 1.96 at 95%
the textbook case, and the rarer one in real work
Excel: margin = =CONFIDENCE.NORM(0.05,σ,n) · z itself = =NORM.S.INV(0.975)
CI for a mean · σ unknown
x̄ ± t × (s ÷ √n)  with df = n − 1
what you will actually use: you had to estimate the spread, so you pay for it with a fatter curve
Excel: margin = =CONFIDENCE.T(0.05,s,n) · t itself = =T.INV.2T(0.05,df)
CI for a proportion
SE = √[ p̂(1 − p̂) ÷ n ]  then  p̂ ± z × SE
same shape as always — only the standard error changes
Excel: =SQRT(p*(1−p)/n)
Sample size for a proportion
n = (z ÷ E)² × p(1 − p)  — then always round up
decide the margin you can live with, and this tells you what it costs. No idea what p is? Use 0.5 — it is the worst case, so you can never under-sample.
Excel: =CEILING(NORM.S.INV(0.975)^2*0.25/E^2,1)
Week 5 · Hypothesis tests, one & two samples

Claims, evidence and verdicts

One warning before the table: p means something different this week. In Weeks 3 and 4 it was a proportion. Here it is the p-value. Context is the only thing that tells them apart.

SymbolSay itWhat it stands forWhere
H₀"H-nought"The null hypothesis — "nothing is going on". Presumed true, always written with an equals sign, and always about a population parameter.§5.2
H₁"H-one"The alternative — the claim you need evidence for. Never contains an equals sign. Many textbooks write it Ha; same thing.§5.2
μ₀"mu-nought"The specific value the null claims — the 14.80 in "H₀: μ = 14.80". The subscript zero marks it as the null's number, not the data's.§5.2
α"alpha"The false-alarm rate you accept, chosen before seeing data — usually 0.05. Reject H₀ when p < α. It is a business decision, not a statistical one.§5.4
p"the p-value"The probability of a result at least as extreme as yours, if H₀ were true. Not a proportion this week, and not the probability that H₀ is true.§5.3
t"t", the test statisticHow many standard errors your result sits from the null value. The same ruler as a z-score, measured against the wobble of the estimate.§5.3
df"degrees of freedom"Which t-curve to compare against. n − 1 for one sample or a paired test; a fractional Welch value for two independent samples.§5.3
β"beta"The chance of a Type II error — failing to detect an effect that is really there. Nobody sets it deliberately, which is the problem.§5.4
1 − β"power"The chance a study finds a real effect of a given size. Below about 80%, "no significant difference" says more about the study than the world.§5.4
x̄₁ − x̄₂"x-bar-one minus x-bar-two"The difference between two independent group means — the thing being tested when the groups are different people.§5.5
d̄, s_d"d-bar, s-sub-d"The mean and spread of the paired differences — one difference per unit measured twice. s_d is usually far smaller than s, which is the whole advantage of pairing.§5.5
pooled p̂"pooled p-hat"The two samples' proportions combined into one rate, used for the standard error because H₀ says both groups share a single true p.§5.5

The formulas, and the Excel that does them

The shape of every test statistic
statistic = (what you saw − what H₀ claims) ÷ standard error
"how many wobbles from the null am I?" — learn this once and every test below is substitution
One-sample t-test
t = (x̄ − μ₀) ÷ (s ÷ √n)  with df = n − 1
for a claim about a single mean against a known target value
Excel: p = =T.DIST.2T(ABS(t), df) · critical value = =T.INV.2T(0.05, df)
The decision rule
reject H₀ when p < α  ·  otherwise fail to reject
"fail to reject" is never "prove equal" — it means the data could not convict
Two independent samples (Welch)
SE = √( s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂ )  then  t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) ÷ SE
variances add even though the means subtract — uncertainty never cancels. The degrees of freedom come out fractional.
Excel: =T.TEST(rng1, rng2, 2, 3) returns the p-value directly
Paired samples
t = d̄ ÷ (s_d ÷ √n)  with df = n − 1
a one-sample test run on the differences. Use it whenever the same unit is measured twice — it removes all the variation between units.
Excel: =T.TEST(before, after, 2, 1)
Two proportions
pooled p̂ = (x₁ + x₂) ÷ (n₁ + n₂)
SE = √[ p̂(1 − p̂)(1/n₁ + 1/n₂) ]  then  z = (p̂₁ − p̂₂) ÷ SE
pool the two rates because, if H₀ is true, there is only one underlying rate to estimate
Excel: p = =2*(1−NORM.S.DIST(ABS(z), TRUE))
Sample size for a target power
n ≈ [ (zα/2 + zβ) × s ÷ effect ]²
for 80% power at α = 0.05, that is (1.96 + 0.84)² ≈ 7.85 × (s ÷ effect)². Run it before the study, not after.
Week 6 · Analysis of variance

Many groups, one test

Two warnings before the table. k is not n. k counts groups; n counts observations in a group; N counts everybody. And MS is a variance — "mean square" sounds like something new, but a mean square is exactly a sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom, which is what a variance always was.

SymbolSay itWhat it stands forWhere
k"k"The number of groups being compared — four branches, three suppliers. Sets the between-groups degrees of freedom, k − 1.§6.2
nj"n-sub-j"The number of observations in group j. When every group is the same size the subscript is usually dropped and it is just n.§6.3
N"big N"Everybody — all observations across all groups. N = Σnj. Sets the total degrees of freedom, N − 1.§6.3
x̄j"x-bar-sub-j"The mean of group j — one branch's average. Four of these in our data.§6.3
x̄̄"x-double-bar"The grand mean — every observation, all groups pooled. Not the average of the group means unless the groups are equal sized.§6.3
SS"sum of squares"A sum of squared deviations. On its own it is a total, not an average — which is why every SS gets divided by its df before being used.§6.2
SSB"S-S-between"The between-group sum of squares — how far the group means sit from the grand mean, weighted by group size. The signal. Some texts write SStreatment or SSA.§6.3
SSW"S-S-within"The within-group sum of squares — how far each observation sits from its own group mean. The noise. Also written SSerror or SSE.§6.3
SST"S-S-total"The total sum of squares. Always SSB + SSW exactly — if your two pieces do not add up, you have an arithmetic error.§6.3
MS"mean square"A sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom. A mean square is a variance — the word just marks which SS it came from.§6.3
MSB"M-S-between"SSB ÷ (k − 1). The numerator of F. Inflated by real group differences.§6.3
MSW"M-S-within"SSW ÷ (N − k). The denominator of F, and your best estimate of the common within-group variance. Its square root is the pooled standard deviation.§6.3
F"F"MSB ÷ MSW — signal over noise. About 1 when H₀ is true. Named after Fisher. Always tested in the right tail only.§6.3
(df₁, df₂)"df one, df two"F needs two degrees of freedom, numerator then denominator: (k − 1, N − k). Ours was (3, 20). Order matters — F tables are not symmetric.§6.3
η²"eta squared"Effect size: SSB ÷ SST, the share of total variation the grouping explains. Bands: 0.01 small, 0.06 medium, 0.14 large. ANOVA does not give it to you — add it yourself.§6.4
q"q", the studentised rangeThe critical value in Tukey's HSD, looked up for k groups and dfwithin. Plays the role a critical t plays for one comparison, but inflated to cover all pairs at once.§6.5
HSD"H-S-D"Tukey's honestly significant difference — the smallest gap between two group means that counts as real. One number, applied to every pair.§6.5
The three sums of squares
SSB = Σ nj (x̄j − x̄̄)²  ·  SSW = Σ Σ (x − x̄j)²  ·  SST = SSB + SSW
SSB looks at group means only; SSW never looks at other groups at all. That independence is what lets their ratio behave like an F.
Excel: =DEVSQ(group) for each SSW piece · =DEVSQ(all) for SST
Degrees of freedom
dfbetween = k − 1  ·  dfwithin = N − k  ·  dftotal = N − 1
they add up exactly like the sums of squares do. N − k, not k(n − 1), is the form that survives unequal group sizes.
The F ratio
F = MSB ÷ MSW = [SSB ÷ (k − 1)] ÷ [SSW ÷ (N − k)]
reject H₀ when F exceeds the critical value, or equivalently when p < α. Never a left tail: a small F is what H₀ predicts.
Excel: =F.DIST.RT(F, df1, df2) for p · =F.INV.RT(0.05, df1, df2) for the critical value
Effect size
η² = SSB ÷ SST
the answer to "how big", which the p-value never gives. Report it alongside F, and translate it into money before the meeting.
Excel: =SSB/SST
Tukey's HSD
HSD = q × √( MSW ÷ n )
any two group means further apart than HSD differ significantly. For unequal group sizes replace n with the harmonic mean of the two sizes (Tukey–Kramer).
Excel: =q*SQRT(MSW/n) — q comes from a studentised-range table, not a built-in function
The multiple-comparisons risk
P(at least one false alarm) = 1 − (1 − α)m  with m = k(k − 1) ÷ 2 pairs
six pairs at α = 0.05 gives 0.265; forty-five pairs gives 0.90. Bonferroni's fix is to test each pair at α ÷ m instead.
The ones that trip everyone

Same letter, different job

Nearly every notation mistake in this course is one of these twenty-one. When a symbol looks familiar but the answer comes out strange, check here first.

n vs N
n is your sample — the things you measured. N is the whole population. If a formula has a little n in it, it wants your count.
n in Week 3 vs n everywhere else
In the binomial, n is the number of trials — 10 coupons issued. It is not a sample size. Same letter, completely different quantity.
k in Week 2 vs k in Week 3
Chebyshev's k (§2.4) = how many standard deviations out you are asking about. The binomial's k (§3.4) = how many successes you want the chance of. Read the sentence around it — "within k SDs" or "exactly k of them" tells you instantly.
s vs σ
s comes from your data and divides by n − 1. σ belongs to the population and divides by N. In Excel that is STDEV.S versus STDEV.P — and you will almost always want .S.
s vs SE — the big one
s says how spread out the data is. SE says how much your estimate of the mean wobbles, and equals s ÷ √n. SE is always the smaller number, and it shrinks as you collect more data. s does not.
p vs p̂
p is the truth you're chasing; p̂ is what your sample gave you. Exception worth remembering: in the sample-size formula the p is a planning guess made before you have data — use 0.5 when you have nothing to go on.
p in Week 3 vs p in Week 4
Week 3: the chance of success on one trial. Week 4: the share of a population with some property. Related ideas, but you plug them into different formulas.
s² vs s (and σ² vs σ)
The squared one is the variance — the standard deviation before the square root. Variance is in squared units (francs²), which is why we report the SD instead.
z vs t
Use z when σ is genuinely known; use t when you had to estimate it with s — which is nearly always. t is always slightly bigger, and the gap closes as n grows.
e vs E vs E(X)
Three different things wearing the same letter. Lower-case e (Week 3) is the constant 2.71828… — never a variable. A bare capital E (Week 4) is the margin of error, the ± half-width of an interval. E(X) with brackets (Week 3) is the expected value, a long-run average. Case and brackets are the whole difference.
e and π are constants — everything else is a variable
When a formula contains e or π, there is nothing to find: they are fixed numbers, 2.71828… and 3.14159…, built into every calculator. Every other letter in this course stands for something you either measure (n, x̄, s) or are trying to learn (μ, σ, p). If you are hunting for the value of e in the question, stop — it never varies.
p the p-value vs p the proportion
The worst collision in the course, and it lands in Week 5. In Weeks 3–4, p is a share of a population. In Week 5, p is the p-value — a probability about your data. Only the sentence tells you which: "p = 0.62 of customers" is a proportion, "p = 0.62 so we fail to reject" is a p-value.
α in Week 5 vs the 0.05 in Week 4
They are the same number doing two jobs. In Week 4, 0.05 is 1 − confidence, the argument of CONFIDENCE.T. In Week 5 it is your accepted false-alarm rate. A 95% interval and a two-tailed test at α = 0.05 agree by construction.
"fail to reject" vs "accept"
Never write "we accept H₀". A test can only fail to convict. If the study had 30% power, failing to reject is exactly what a real effect would most likely have produced — the result describes the study, not the world.
df = n − 1 vs Welch's df
One sample or paired: df = n − 1, a whole number. Two independent samples: a fractional Welch value (72.2, say) that Excel computes for you. If your two-sample df is a tidy n₁ + n₂ − 2, you assumed equal variances — make sure you meant to.
β here vs β later
In Week 5, β is the Type II error rate. From Week 7 it becomes a regression coefficient. Unrelated quantities, same letter — the giveaway is that a Type II β is always a probability between 0 and 1.
k vs n vs N in Week 6
Three counts, one ANOVA table. k = how many groups (4 branches). n = how many observations in one group (6 days). N = everybody (24). Every degree of freedom in the table is built from these: k − 1, N − k, N − 1. Mixing up n and N is the single commonest way to get df wrong.
k in Week 6 vs k in Weeks 2 and 3
A third job for the same letter. Week 2: how many standard deviations out (Chebyshev). Week 3: how many successes (binomial). Week 6: how many groups. Nothing in common but the glyph.
SS vs MS
SS is a total of squared deviations — it grows just because you collected more data. MS is that total divided by its df, which makes it an average, and an average of squared deviations is a variance. You can never compare two SS values directly; MS values you can, which is the entire point of the F ratio.
F the statistic vs f the frequency
Capital F (Week 6) is the ANOVA test statistic, MSB ÷ MSW. Lower-case f back in Week 1 was a frequency — a count in a table. Nothing to do with each other.
median vs Q2
The same number, wearing two names. Q2 is just the median seen as the second quartile.
