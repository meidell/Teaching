The brief

What to do

Find one claim that somebody in your organisation — or in a news story, or in the Café Léman case — states as fact and acts on. Then test it with the right tool from Weeks 5 to 8, and report what the evidence supports.

The claim can be one your organisation makes, one a news story makes, or one you make about your own life — the Week 6 and Week 8 “take it outside” exercises suggested logging your own commute, sleep or spend, and an analysis of your own data is entirely welcome here. What matters is that the claim rests on a comparison or a trend, because those are what this half of the course can settle. Some shapes that work:

  • "Branch A performs better than branch B." → a two-sample test (Week 5)
  • "These three suppliers / four regions / five shift patterns are not the same." → ANOVA, and Tukey if F is significant (Week 6)
  • "X drives Y — every franc of X buys us Y." → regression, with the residual plot (Week 7)
  • "We are down 20% since May." → decomposition: is that trend, or is it August? (Week 8)
  • "I sleep worse on Mondays." → same shape as the first two, on your own data — and the within-group noise is usually the lesson.

You do not need a big dataset. Twenty honest rows in a spreadsheet is enough to make a real point; a made-up dataset is not, and reads as one.

If you have no data at all — some of you work somewhere that will not let numbers out of the building, and that is fine. Use the Café Léman datasets from the essay brief, or any open dataset you can cite (a federal statistics office, a company's annual report, a Kaggle set). Say where it came from.
The post

Five things your post must contain

250–400 words, in this order. They are the same five steps every honest analysis takes, and they are what I mark.

1 · The claimone sentence

State it the way somebody actually said it — the sentence as spoken in the meeting, not a tidied-up version. Say who says it and what decision rides on it.

e.g. "Our Tuesday promotion pays for itself — Tuesday revenue is clearly up." Said by the marketing lead; the promotion costs CHF 4,000 a month to run.
2 · The datatwo or three lines

What you have, how much of it, over what period, and where it came from. If it is confidential, say so and describe it without the identifying detail — see the ground rules below.

3 · The test, and why that onethe part people skip

Name the technique and say why it is the right one for this shape of question — and name the assumption that worried you most, and what you did about it. Independence? Equal variances? A residual plot that fanned out? A series too short to decompose?

"I ran a t-test" is not an answer. "Two independent groups, unequal sample sizes, and the spread looked similar enough to pool — so a two-sample t-test" is.

4 · The result, twiceonce in statistics, once in francs

Give the statistic and the p-value or the interval. Then say the same thing again in the units your business actually uses — francs, customers, minutes, percentage points. If you cannot do the second one, you have not finished reading your own output.

e.g. t = 1.42, p = 0.17 — and: "the Tuesday difference is CHF 310 a week, and the data cannot rule out that it is zero."
5 · What should changeone sentence

What should the organisation do differently on Monday morning? "Nothing, and here is why" is a full-mark answer if the evidence says so. So is "we cannot tell yet, and here is the data I would need."

A result that came out non-significant is a better post than a tidy one. "I was certain these two things were related and the data says they are not" is the single most useful thing anyone posts in this course, and it is the hardest to write. It is marked no lower.
The replies

Then reply to two classmates

At least 80 words each, to two different people. A reply is not "great post, I had the same problem at my company." Engage with the method, and do one of these three things:

  1. Challenge the choice of test. Would ANOVA have been better than three t-tests? Is that really a trend, or a season?
  2. Challenge an assumption. Independence is the one most often quietly broken — same customer counted twice, same week counted in both groups.
  3. Offer the alternative. Say what you would have run instead, and what it would have told them that this one does not.

Disagree freely and directly — that is the point of a forum, and nobody loses marks for being challenged. Challenge the analysis, never the person.

Reply to the posts that have no replies yet where you can. Two students should not collect six replies while three collect none.

What loses marks

  • A p-value above 0.05 read as "we proved there is no difference". It means the data cannot rule out zero — a different sentence.
  • A trend claimed from a series that was never deseasonalised.
  • r or R² presented as proof that one thing causes the other.
  • A screenshot of Excel output with no interpretation. The output is not the analysis.
  • Replies posted in the last hour, all to the same person, saying "well done".
Ground rules

Before you post

  • This page is visible to the whole class and to me. It is not private, and it is not encrypted. Post nothing you would not put on a slide in front of your employer.
  • Anonymise anything confidential. "A mid-size Swiss logistics firm" beats naming it. Round or index the numbers if you must — the analysis still works on indexed data, and nobody needs your real revenue.
  • Name your source. Where the numbers came from, and over what period.
  • You may use Excel, Python, R or an AI assistant to compute. You must be able to explain every number you post and say which tool produced it. A post whose author cannot explain its own output scores nothing, whatever the tool.
  • You can edit your post until the deadline, but you cannot delete it — and neither can I, easily. Replies, once posted, stand. Write it as you mean it.
